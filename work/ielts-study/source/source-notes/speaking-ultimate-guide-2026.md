## The seven tips, distilled

### 1. Do not perform friendliness for marks

The video challenges advice to smile, maintain eye contact, gesture, or otherwise manage the examiner’s impression. These behaviours are not part of the four Speaking assessment criteria: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Feedback should address the candidate’s spoken performance. Courteous interaction is fine, but it is not a band-raising tactic. ([0:41–5:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=41s), [official Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))

### 2. Use language that fits the conversation

The video rejects the blanket rule to speak in academic or highly formal English. Speaking includes everyday topics and common experiences, so natural conversational language is appropriate. Learners should choose a register that fits the moment and use informal expressions only when they know how they work. The correction is not “use slang”; it is “do not make ordinary speech unnaturally formal.” ([5:28–9:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=328s), [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking))

### 3. Build usable vocabulary instead of collecting impressive words

The video warns against memorizing advanced word lists and forcing unfamiliar vocabulary into answers. Learn words with their meaning, pronunciation, grammar, collocations, and context; then use them when they express the intended idea accurately. A familiar precise word is more useful than an impressive but misused one. Do not assume every learner needs new “Band 9” words: diagnose actual range and accuracy from recorded answers first. ([9:14–13:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=554s), [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking))

### 4. Practise topic flexibility, not predictions

The video warns that lists titled “most common questions” may lead learners to overprepare scripts for a narrow set of topics. Build the ability to respond to unfamiliar or unexpected questions instead. The useful point is flexible response, not the source’s overstatement that common-topic practice is a waste: official guidance says Part 1 uses familiar topics and gives examples, so these are useful for practice when treated as examples rather than guaranteed test content. Part 2 uses a task card; Part 3 explores issues related to its topic. ([13:32–17:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=812s), [IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking), [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking))

If a question is outside the candidate’s experience, it is fine to say so briefly and then give a related general, imagined, or future example where appropriate. The examiner assesses language performance, not expertise or the candidate’s opinion. Do not turn this into a prepared fictional biography. ([20:48–22:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=1248s), [IELTS Speaking myths](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/ielts-speaking-tips-common-myths-and-how-to-overcome-them))

### 5. Prepare response skills, not recitable scripts

The video criticizes memorized phrases and polished full answers. Scripts may sound unnatural or fail to answer the question actually asked. Practise flexible functions instead: giving a reason, adding an example, comparing options, explaining uncertainty, and extending a point. Official guidance also recommends natural speech and warns that rehearsed answers can limit a score. Avoid claiming that every memorized phrase triggers an automatic penalty; the practical risk is an unnatural, irrelevant, or inflexible response that does not demonstrate live communication. ([17:17–22:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=1037s), [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking), [IELTS: Don’t overdo it](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/dont-overdo-it-how-to-ace-your-ielts-speaking-test))

### 6. Treat idioms as one part of natural vocabulary, not a quota

The video explains that “idiomatic vocabulary” in the band descriptors is broader than memorized sayings: it includes natural, accurate word combinations and expressions that fit the context. Do not count idioms or insert a fixed number. Learn an expression’s meaning, grammar, pronunciation, and collocations; use it only when it is natural and precise. This aligns with official advice that idioms should not sound forced. ([22:24–30:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=1344s), [British Council idiom guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/blog/top-20-english-idioms-to-be-more-fluent-in-ielts-speaking))

### 7. Use Part 2 cue-card prompts to organize a focused talk

The video pushes back on giving each bullet equal time or pointing through them in a rigid order. The central task is to speak at length about the cue-card topic; supporting details can make the talk coherent and help sustain it. Official guidance says to use the card’s points to structure the talk and try to cover all parts. Teach learners to treat the points as a coverage checklist: address each briefly where possible, combine related points, and develop the response coherently without invented equal-time quotas. ([30:10–34:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l7_4tKrZqM&t=1810s), [IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking), [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking))

## Course-design takeaways

- Start with the official four criteria. Remove eye contact, gestures, politeness, and examiner “impression” from score rubrics.
- Teach register through examples of context-appropriate speech, not a blanket formal-English or slang rule.
- Diagnose vocabulary needs from speech samples; teach accurate use and collocation rather than word-list accumulation.
- Use varied prompts, including unfamiliar ones, to practise relevance and flexible development. Topic examples are practice material, not predictions.
- Practise answer functions and idea development without memorizing full responses.
- Teach idiomatic language as natural, accurate usage, not an idiom-count target.
- Model Part 2 planning from the cue card’s central topic and prompts; give learners room to choose and connect supporting details.

## Source caveats

The video invokes IDP and British Council advice to debunk popular tips, which is a useful structure. Some of its conclusions are stronger than the official pages support: it calls common-topic practice broadly useless and presents memorized language as an automatic score penalty. Keep the narrower, better-supported lessons above. Its claims that the discussed myths categorically “lower” or “raise” scores are not accompanied by a controlled comparison. The closing argument about other YouTube creators’ motives is opinion and is not part of the teaching synthesis.

### Companion lessons, distilled

- **Do not coach a scripted greeting.** The instructor calls the identity-and-greeting exchange before the interview “part zero.” This is his shorthand, not an official IELTS section: IELTS lists three parts and calls Part 1 “Introduction and interview,” where the examiner introduces themself and checks identity before familiar-topic questions. Teach learners to follow instructions and use ordinary courtesy; reserve practice time for assessed spoken performance rather than rehearsing a greeting. ([0:00–4:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=0s); [official test format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Treat body language as a comfort aid, not a scoring target.** The lesson says gestures, eye contact, and smiling are not separate scoring criteria. IDP likewise says smiling or laughing brings neither more nor fewer marks, while noting that feeling relaxed may help performance. A natural posture or breath routine can be useful if it makes a learner more comfortable; avoid grading mannerisms or implying they directly raise the band. ([4:55–8:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=295s); [IDP Speaking advice](https://ielts.idp.com/about/ielts-speaking); [IELTS body-language and stress article](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/using-body-language-to-reduce-stress-levels-and-improve-ielts-performance))
- **Use a natural, context-appropriate register.** The lesson contrasts conversational responses with unnecessarily formal answers and notes that the Speaking test is shared by Academic and General Training candidates. Teach natural spoken English with accurate, flexible grammar and vocabulary, rather than either mandatory academic phrasing or slang. ([8:45–13:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=525s); [IDP Speaking advice](https://ielts.idp.com/about/ielts-speaking); [official criteria](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Turn authentic listening into usable vocabulary practice.** Notice a small number of unfamiliar or useful expressions while listening to conversational English; check the transcript and surrounding context; infer and verify meaning, word form, and collocation; record only what is worth learning; then try it in a fresh Speaking response. Record and review whether the expression fits the meaning and sounds accurate and natural. The channel’s demonstration supports this learning loop, not its “lists lower your score” generalization. It complements the [vocabulary learning system](vocabulary-learning-system.md). ([13:45–22:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=825s); [34:15–41:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=2055s))
- **Practise familiar and less-familiar topics without predicting the test.** Part 1 officially uses familiar topics; Part 2 and Part 3 assess longer and broader discussion. Use common-topic questions as practice, then vary the prompt and practise generating a relevant response. The video’s claims about examiner topic selection, topic rarity, and its analysis of large sets of top-band transcripts are not independently verified. ([22:20–26:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=1340s); [official test format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Prepare flexible response skills, not full answers.** Rehearsed scripts can be irrelevant to the exact question and difficult to adapt; practise answering, explaining, illustrating, comparing, and paraphrasing instead. Official guidance warns against memorizing large chunks, but do not claim that every learned phrase or repeated expression triggers an automatic penalty. The lesson's stories about examiners deliberately choosing “trick” topics are not verified test policy. ([26:50–34:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=1610s); [British Council fluency guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking/tips-fluency-coherence))
- **Use cue-card points to stay on task, without equal-time quotas.** The demonstration shows how mechanically reciting bullets can sound disconnected and end early. However, the instructor’s alternative—selecting only the comfortable bullets—should not become the course rule. Official IELTS guidance says the card identifies points to include, and British Council advises trying to cover all parts. Teach candidates to address each point at least briefly when possible, combine related points, and add relevant detail to sustain a coherent talk. Band labels attached to the staged candidates are not independently verified. ([41:45–48:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fUGVFbFXRMo&t=2505s); [IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking); [British Council Speaking guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking))

### The 18 expressions: meaning, use, and cautions

| Expression | Meaning and usable context | Form or accuracy check |
|---|---|---|
| **Bite the bullet** | Face an unpleasant or difficult task and do it. The sample concerns choosing to study law. | Common frames: *I decided/had to bite the bullet*. Keep the article and verb tense. |
| **A piece of cake** | Something is very easy. | Keep **a**: *It was a piece of cake*, not *it was piece of cake*. Informal; use when the context fits. |
| **Kill two birds with one stone** | Achieve two aims with one action. The sample combines a birthday and Christmas present. | Match the verb tense: *I killed two birds…* for a past event; *I can kill…* for a present/future plan. |
| **Let the cat out of the bag** | Reveal a secret, often by accident. The sample phone notification gives away a surprise watch. | Keep *the cat* and *the bag*; do not drop articles. |
| **Don’t put all your eggs in one basket** | Avoid concentrating all effort or resources in one place; diversify risk. | The investment example is the speaker’s illustration, not financial advice. |
| **Once in a blue moon** | Very rarely. The sample describes meeting friends infrequently. | Use only when the low frequency is accurate; the interviewer need not ask “how often” verbatim. |
| **Burn the midnight oil** | Work or study very hard, traditionally late at night; the speaker also uses it more generally for sustained hard work. | Keep **the**; do not confuse it with the exhaustion implied by the next expression. |
| **Burn the candle at both ends** | Work too hard while also using up energy elsewhere, often leading to exhaustion. | Keep **the candle** and **at both ends**. The source contrasts this negative sense with *burn the midnight oil*. |
| **At the drop of a hat** | Immediately, without hesitation. The sample refers to being able to move quickly. | Keep **the**; use for an action that is genuinely quick or spontaneous. |
| **Cry over spilled milk** | Dwell on a past mistake or loss that cannot be changed. | *Spilled* and British *spilt* occur; preserve the fixed phrase and then show learning or moving on. |
| **You can’t judge a book by its cover** | Do not judge a person or thing only by appearance. | A set expression; connect it to a relevant example rather than use it as decoration. |
| **Don’t count your chickens before they hatch** | Do not assume a hoped-for result before it happens. | The full form is clearest; the speaker notes *don’t count your chickens* as a common shorter form. |
| **Go the extra mile** | Make more effort than is required. | Keep **the** and use a context that actually involves extra effort. |
| **Rain cats and dogs** | Rain very heavily. The sample describes frequent heavy rain in Ireland. | The common phrase is *it’s raining cats and dogs*; keep the cats-before-dogs order and use a matching tense. This is colloquial; *heavy rain* is often more precise. |
| **Throw in the towel** | Stop trying or give up. The sample describes abandoning a difficult plan after a long day. | Keep **the towel**; use only if the context is truly about stopping. |
| **Cross that bridge when you come to it** | Deal with a future problem when it actually arises. | Keep the relative pronouns and tense aligned: *I’ll cross that bridge when I come to it*. |
| **Rome wasn’t built in a day** | A significant result takes time; often used to console someone or resist rushing. | Keep **wasn’t** and **in a day**; attach it to a long-term goal. |
| **When in Rome** | Adapt to or respect local customs while visiting a place. | A shortened form of *When in Rome, do as the Romans do*. Use it only when a real custom/context fits. |

### What the lesson adds to vocabulary instruction

The instructor reports an average of **1.2 idioms** in an analysis of his Band 9 students’ speaking tests and says it was uncommon for a candidate to use more than four or five. The underlying recordings, sample, method, and scoring are not published; treat the number as an unverified source claim, not a target or evidence of how many expressions candidates need. The sound teaching point is that “idiomatic” vocabulary describes language that sounds natural and fits style and collocation, not an idiom count. A candidate can receive a high score without using set idioms.

Teach expressions through complete examples and spaced, contextual use. Ask learners to explain the meaning in plain English, adapt the phrase to a new prompt, and check if it is grammatically and pragmatically natural. Do not memorize a list for insertion into every response, and do not assume a memorized idiom automatically raises or lowers a score. The birthday-cake analogy is the speaker’s way of separating a broad base of everyday language from a few optional expressive items; use it as a metaphor, not a percentage rule. ### Distilled teaching points

- **Answer the question that was asked, even when the topic is unfamiliar.** The video contrasts a memorized hometown speech with a personal question about an unfamiliar subject such as wigs. Teach a short honest response about limited experience, followed by a relevant opinion, reason, or plausible example when appropriate. Learners do not need to pretend to be an expert, invent personal history, or keep talking after the question has been answered. ([0:54–3:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELxGmf9f_ZM&t=54s))
- **Use flexible response skills instead of complete scripts.** Practise explaining a view, qualifying a guess, giving a reason, and paraphrasing when exact vocabulary is unavailable. Whole memorized answers can sound unnatural or fail to fit the prompt; IELTS guidance likewise warns that rehearsed responses may not accurately show a candidate’s communication ability. Do not teach the instructor’s categorical claim that examiners will automatically discard all memorized material. ([1:22–3:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELxGmf9f_ZM&t=82s); [IELTS on memorized answers](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/dont-overdo-it-how-to-ace-your-ielts-speaking-test))
- **Do not infer a score from question difficulty.** The instructor says Part 3 becomes harder when an examiner believes a candidate is stronger, and that performance on unexpected questions can determine the score. The video does not establish that procedure or support the claim that one subset of answers alone determines a band. Teach the candidate to listen carefully, address the question, and develop a clear answer; assess the performance against all four official criteria across the test. ([3:17–6:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELxGmf9f_ZM&t=197s); [IELTS Speaking criteria and format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Learn vocabulary for meaning and topic fit.** The lesson’s useful lexical point is to choose words that accurately express an idea in the current context rather than forcing a memorized “Band 9” list. Practise retrieving topic-appropriate language across varied prompts and check its meaning, collocation, and natural use. ([6:28–8:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELxGmf9f_ZM&t=388s); [IELTS Speaking band descriptors](https://ielts.org/for-organisations/ielts-scoring-in-detail))

The film analogy, claims that students are “cheating” by using tactics, the instructor’s student-success anecdotes, and the promise of life-changing results are motivational or promotional material, not evidence about scoring. The useful course design is broad prompt practice, responsive answers, and accurate topic vocabulary, with feedback tied to the official criteria.

### Five principles and course-use corrections

1. **Use a different response routine for each part.** In Part 1, answer the personal question directly, then add a short relevant reason, example, or detail. Do not count sentences or words. This is a flexible scaffold, not a required response length. ([0:24–2:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=24s))
2. **In Part 2, make a connected talk about the main topic.** Use the one-minute planning time to choose and connect supporting points. The instructor suggests creating personal bullet points and skipping some printed prompts; qualify this against official instructions. Teach learners to use the card’s points to organize the response and try to cover them where possible, without giving each equal time. ([2:32–4:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=152s); [official Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
3. **In Part 3, respond before expanding.** Give a direct answer, explain why, support it with a relevant example, and add another perspective if useful. Personal examples are permitted, but broader questions can also be answered with general reasoning. Avoid rambling that never addresses the question, and do not require every response to follow a fixed four-part formula. ([4:18–5:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=258s))
4. **Aim for natural fluency while maintaining accuracy.** The instructor’s exercise—recording one response with attention to accuracy and another for flow, then comparing them—can expose over-monitoring. His headline “fluency beats perfection” should not become permission to neglect grammar or lexical accuracy: IELTS assesses fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation with equal weighting. ([5:57–9:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=357s); [official Speaking criteria](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
5. **Grow usable vocabulary through input, retrieval, and feedback.** Read and listen widely; learn new items with meaning, pronunciation, collocation, and usage; then practise using them in fresh answers and writing. Accurate, topic-appropriate language is more useful than forced advanced words or a quota of idioms. The video’s “100% rule” is a teaching heuristic: learners should aim to control a word’s meaning and use, but need not avoid every unfamiliar or newly learned word. ([9:58–13:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=598s); [vocabulary learning system](vocabulary-learning-system.md))

**Feedback and claims:** Recorded practice and informed, specific feedback can reveal recurring issues; build a short priority list and revisit it with new prompts. The instructor’s suggestion that feedback must come from an ex-examiner is not an IELTS requirement; feedback should be accurate and tied to the criteria. The claimed fluency-to-band gains and score guarantee are not independently verified, and the video’s course offers and scarcity language are promotional. ([13:06–15:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqznnq9gOsU&t=786s))
