# CH80 — Health and Medicine Vocabulary

**Outcome:** Name selected body parts, symptoms, professionals, and care-related terms to describe a concern and ask a question.  

## Study

You do not need a diagnosis to explain what hurts. Choose the words for the concern you need to report; use the other groups as a reference.

### Body parts

**Head, ears, neck, shoulders, chest, back, arms, hands, hips, legs, knees, ankles, and feet** name common locations. The **elbow** is the joint in the middle of an arm; the **forearm** is between elbow and wrist; the **wrist** joins the hand to the arm. **Fingers** are on hands; **toes** are on feet. The **thigh** is the upper leg; the **calf** is the back of the lower leg; the **shin** is its front; the **heel** is the back of the foot. An **armpit** is under the arm where it joins the body.

**Abdomen** names the area between the chest and pelvis; **belly** is a familiar word for that area. **Stomach** names an internal organ, but people also use it in everyday reports such as “My stomach hurts.” The **waist** is the area around the body's middle; the **navel / belly button** is the small central mark on the abdomen. **Heart, lungs, liver, and kidneys** are internal organs. **Bone** is the hard material forming the skeleton.

Clinical body words are useful even when a concern feels private. **Breast** names body tissue on the upper front of the torso; **nipple** is the small projecting part on a breast. **Groin** is the area where the lower abdomen meets the upper thigh. **Penis** is an external genital organ. **Vagina** names an internal genital passage; **vulva** names the external genital area around the vaginal opening. These are anatomical terms, not diagnoses or insults.

### Symptoms and injuries

A **symptom** is something experienced or noticed that may relate to a health problem.

- **Sore / painful:** hurting. **Itchy:** making you want to scratch.
- **Cough:** expelling air from the lungs; **runny nose:** fluid coming from the nose; **sore throat:** pain in the throat.
- **Fever:** an abnormally high body temperature; **headache / earache:** pain in the head or ear.
- **Nausea:** feeling that you may vomit; **vomiting / throwing up:** bringing stomach contents up; **diarrhea:** loose or watery bowel movements.
- **Rash:** an area of changed or irritated skin; **bruise:** discoloration from bleeding beneath the skin; **cut / scrape:** types of skin injury.
- **Burn, bite, cramp, sprain, broken bone:** different problems. A **sprain** involves an injured ligament; a **broken bone** is a fracture. Naming a possibility does not confirm it.

**Virus**, **infection**, **allergy**, and **food poisoning** name possible causes or conditions, not interchangeable symptoms. You can say “I don't know what's causing it.”

### People and care words

A **doctor** assesses and treats health concerns; a **nurse** helps care for patients and monitors their condition; a **pharmacist** works with medicines; a **dentist** works with teeth and oral health. A **surgeon** performs surgery; an **anesthesiologist** is a doctor specializing in anesthesia; a **paramedic** provides emergency medical care. A **pediatrician** specializes in children's medical care; a **midwife** provides care connected with pregnancy and birth; a **physical therapist** works on movement and physical function. An **optician** fits or supplies eyewear; a **therapist** needs context to identify the kind of therapy.

**Emergency department / ER**, **ambulance**, **blood pressure**, **pulse / heart rate**, **blood test**, **X-ray**, **scan**, **checkup**, **bandage**, **stitches**, **injection / shot**, and **medication** are useful care terms. **Antibiotics** are a type of medicine, not a general word for every treatment.

## Practice

Use three fictional reports: a sore throat since Monday; an itchy arm with a rash; pain in the groin that started today.

1. Select the body part, symptom, and time for one report. Say it without selecting a diagnosis.
2. Choose the professional you would ask about a prescription question, then formulate the question.
3. Explain the difference between a symptom you notice and a cause you only suspect. Alone, say your explanations; with a partner, exchange cases.

**Possibilities:** “My arm is itchy, and there's a rash.” “Could a pharmacist explain this medicine's instructions?” “I have a sore throat; I don't know the cause.” Check that your vocabulary identifies the location and experience accurately. Detailed reporting and care conversations follow; this vocabulary page does not establish what treatment you need.
