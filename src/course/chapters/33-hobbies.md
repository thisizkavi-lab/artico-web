# CH33 — Hobbies

**Outcome:** Ask about someone's interests, explain what you do for fun, and talk about starting or continuing a hobby.  

## Study

A hobby conversation can begin with a broad question: **What do you do for fun?** At work, **What do you do outside of work?** makes the topic clear. **Do you have any hobbies?** is another option. An answer can be a short activity, not a complete life story.

Useful activity combinations include **play tennis**, **play the guitar**, **go hiking**, **do photography**, **make pottery**, and **play video games**. Choose activities you actually want to discuss. Chapter 26 offers more vocabulary; you do not need its whole list here.

**A:** What do you do for fun?  
**B:** Mostly photography. I like taking pictures of buildings. How about you?  
**A:** I play guitar, but I'm still learning.  
**B:** Oh, nice. What kind of music do you play?  
**A:** Mostly rock. A few country songs, too.  

**Mostly** identifies your main activity without suggesting it is the only one. **I'm still learning** lets you talk about an interest without presenting yourself as an expert. A follow-up question responds to what the other person said; it need not introduce an entirely new topic.

### Starting something new

**I've started running** focuses on a recent start. **I've taken up painting** also presents painting as a new activity. **I'm trying out a pottery class** leaves open whether you will continue.

**A:** I've started taking dance classes.  
**B:** Really? How's it going?  
**A:** It's fun, but the steps are hard to remember.  
**B:** Would you recommend the class?  
**A:** Yeah, if you don't mind being a beginner!  

You can give a mixed opinion: **I like it, but it's challenging.** To show interest, try **That sounds fun**, **How did you get into it?**, or **What do you like about it?** You do not have to pretend you share the interest: **I've never tried that. What's it like?** keeps the conversation going honestly.

### Talking about time

**How long have you been playing?** asks about an activity continuing over time. Useful replies are **For about a year**, **Since last summer**, and **I only started last month**. **For** introduces a length of time; **since** introduces a starting point. Learn the question and your chosen answer as a usable pair.

**A:** How long have you been playing tennis?  
**B:** For about two years. I usually play on weekends.  
**A:** Want to play sometime?  
**B:** Maybe. I'm pretty rusty, though.  

**Rusty** here means out of practice. The tentative **Maybe** does not confirm a plan; a definite arrangement needs a time and agreement, as in Chapter 12.

## Practice

Practice alone by reading the question, covering the answer, and replying aloud. Then speak both parts. With someone else, switch roles.

1. Use these facts: guitar; beginner; rock; started in January. Answer **What do you do for fun?** and **How long have you been playing?**
2. Your partner says, **I'm trying out a cooking class.** Ask one relevant follow-up. They answer, **It's fun, but a little expensive.** Respond without claiming you will join.
3. Use your own interest or an invented role. Explain the activity, one opinion, and when you started. Ask about the other person's interest.

**Sample possibilities:** “I play guitar. I'm still learning. Mostly rock. I started in January.” / “What's the class like?” — “It's fun, but a little expensive.” — “Oh, okay. I've never tried a cooking class.”  

Later, return without reading the model. Change the activity and the duration. Check whether you gave enough information for a follow-up and distinguished a tentative invitation from a confirmed plan.
