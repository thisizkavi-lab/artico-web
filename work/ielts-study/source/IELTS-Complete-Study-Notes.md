# Complete IELTS study notes

**Edition:** 30 October 2026.  
**Purpose:** A continuous reference from first principles to detailed task methods, language choices, error diagnosis, and final preparation.  
**Basis:** The processed IELTS Advantage reference: the course blueprint, the distilled summary, and all 39 detailed source notes.

These notes combine repeated advice. They retain the distinct teaching points and the limits of the advice. Examples explain a decision. They do not form a practice examination or a bank of memorised answers.

## How to read these notes

The writing uses a plain technical style inspired by [Simplified Technical English](https://www.asd-ste100.org/STE_faq.html). Sentences are short. Terms are consistent. Each step has one main action. A condition comes before the action when the condition affects the decision.

Use the notes to understand the work. Use the IELTS criteria to judge an actual response. Clear explanations in these notes do not remove the need for natural language and grammatical range in your answers.

Three labels distinguish different kinds of advice:

- **Requirement:** A test instruction, format condition, or assessment principle from an official source.
- **Method:** A useful procedure for doing the work.
- **Option:** A coaching choice. Check whether it improves your own accuracy, control, or timing.

A method is useful when it helps you produce a correct or well-supported response. A method does not guarantee a band.

## 1. IELTS basics

### 1.1 What IELTS measures

IELTS means International English Language Testing System.

IELTS measures English proficiency through four skills:

1. Listening.
2. Reading.
3. Writing.
4. Speaking.

Understanding information and expressing meaning are central to the test. Test preparation also requires knowledge of the tasks and their conditions.

A learner can have two different problems:

- A **language problem:** The learner cannot understand or express the required meaning.
- A **task problem:** The learner has enough language but does not follow the task correctly.

Both problems can occur in the same response. Diagnose the work before choosing a repair.

Knowing a strategy does not prove that you can use it. Understanding a lesson does not prove that you can perform under time pressure.

### 1.2 Choose the correct test

The two main versions covered here are IELTS Academic and IELTS General Training.

| Version | Main preparation distinction |
|---|---|
| Academic | Reading uses extended academic-style texts. Writing Task 1 reports visual information. |
| General Training | Reading uses a different range of everyday, workplace, and general texts. Writing Task 1 is a letter. |
| Shared work | Listening and Speaking are shared. Many language and response skills transfer between versions. |

The organisation receiving your result decides which test it accepts. Your general purpose does not settle every requirement.

Check these items:

1. The accepted test version.
2. The required overall band.
3. The minimum band in each skill.
4. The accepted delivery arrangement.
5. The submission deadline.
6. Any route-specific or professional requirements.

IELTS for UKVI can involve additional acceptance and administration requirements. IELTS Life Skills is a different assessment. Do not treat it as the four-skill Academic or General Training test. [Official IELTS test types](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types)

### 1.3 Understand the format

| Skill | Main work | Main time condition |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Four parts; 40 questions; the recording is played once | Approximately 30 minutes; use current provider instructions for checking and answer entry |
| Academic Reading | Three sections; 40 questions | 60 minutes, including answer entry |
| General Training Reading | Three sections; 40 questions; different text profile | 60 minutes, including answer entry |
| Writing | Two tasks | One shared 60-minute period |
| Speaking | Familiar interview, long turn, broader discussion | 11–14 minutes |

Academic Writing Task 1 requires at least 150 words. General Training Writing Task 1 also requires at least 150 words. Writing Task 2 requires at least 250 words.

Task 2 has twice the weight of Task 1. Do both tasks. One strong essay does not replace a missing Task 1 response.

Listening, Reading, and Writing are normally completed together. Speaking can be scheduled separately. Check the arrangement in your booking. [Academic test format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test), [General Training format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test)

### 1.4 Use current delivery instructions

IELTS announced a move away from fully paper-based delivery from mid-2026. Exact change dates vary by market.

Selected markets offer Writing on Paper within computer-delivered IELTS. The tasks appear on a screen, but the Writing responses are handwritten.

Check the local provider before choosing preparation conditions. Do not apply an old paper answer-transfer routine to a computer test. Do not assume that headphones, input tools, or local procedures are identical everywhere. [IELTS delivery update](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/updates-to-ielts-test-delivery), [Writing on Paper](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-on-computer-writing-on-paper)

Practise the relevant practical actions:

- Read a passage on a screen when your test uses a screen.
- Enter answers accurately.
- Move between questions without losing your place.
- Use the permitted highlighting and note tools.
- Type or handwrite with the required control.
- Check sound and report a problem promptly.

### 1.5 Understand the scores

IELTS reports four skill bands and an overall band. The overall result is the average of the four skill bands, rounded to a whole or half band.

Examples:

- An average of 6.25 becomes 6.5.
- An average of 6.75 becomes 7.0.
- An average of 6.125 becomes 6.0.

An overall target and a skill minimum are different requirements. A high Listening result cannot cancel a required Writing minimum.

The scale runs from 0 to 9. Band 0 indicates no attempted answers. Band 9 indicates expert performance. There is no universal pass mark for every purpose.

IELTS recommends considering results valid for two years. A receiving organisation can have its own acceptance period. Check that organisation. [Official scoring explanation](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail)

### 1.6 Raw marks and bands are different

Listening and Reading award one mark for each correct answer. The raw total is out of 40.

The following are official average examples, not fixed conversion rules:

| Band | Listening | Academic Reading | General Training Reading |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | — | — | 15 |
| 5 | 16 | 15 | 23 |
| 6 | 23 | 23 | 30 |
| 7 | 30 | 30 | 35 |
| 8 | 35 | 35 | — |

A dash means that this example table does not supply that value. The required raw total can vary slightly between test versions. Do not convert a short exercise into a band by simple proportion. [Official raw-score examples](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail)

Wrong answers do not remove marks from correct Listening or Reading answers. Make a best-supported choice when uncertain. Do not leave an answer blank only because you cannot prove it with certainty.

A spelling error can lose an item mark. It does not create one fixed band deduction.

### 1.7 Know the assessment terms

| Term | Meaning in these notes |
|---|---|
| Task | The work the question tells you to do |
| Scope | The group, time, place, condition, and strength of a claim |
| Evidence | The text, audio, data, or response feature that supports a decision |
| Paraphrase | The same meaning expressed in different words or structures |
| Gist | The main idea of a paragraph or passage |
| Inference | A conclusion supported by information, even when the exact sentence is absent |
| Criterion | One area used to assess performance |
| Coherence | Clear organisation and progression of meaning |
| Cohesion | Language and references that connect ideas |
| Lexical resource | The range and control of vocabulary |
| Collocation | Words that commonly occur together |
| Register | Language suited to the audience, purpose, and situation |
| Intelligibility | How easily a listener can understand speech |
| Baseline | Evidence of current performance before a planned change |
| Transfer | Use of a learned skill with fresh material |
| Diagnostic | Work used to identify a specific weakness |
| Distractor | A plausible option or detail that does not answer the actual question |

## 2. Build the skills that support every section

### 2.1 Use one preparation cycle

Use this sequence:

1. Understand the task.
2. Observe a worked example.
3. Explain the method in your own words.
4. Apply the method with support.
5. Apply it to fresh material without support.
6. Review the evidence.
7. Identify one specific weakness.
8. Practise the smallest useful repair.
9. Check the repair on fresh material.

Timed work measures whether the skill works under a time condition. Slower work can build the skill.

Use replay, pauses, hints, and models during learning when they help. Record their use. Assisted work and independent work provide different evidence.

A retake can support learning. It does not provide the same evidence as an unfamiliar task.

### 2.2 Diagnose before increasing practice volume

A total score identifies a result. It does not identify the cause.

Use the actual answer, text, recording, or essay to identify the failed operation.

Possible causes include:

- An instruction was missed.
- The wrong information was located.
- The information was found but misunderstood.
- A qualifier changed the claim.
- A word or phrase was unknown.
- Known words were not recognised in connected speech.
- The answer was entered incorrectly.
- An idea was relevant but insufficiently developed.
- A response did not cover every instruction.
- A recurring grammar or pronunciation problem reduced control.
- The learner lost attention or used time inefficiently.

Treat the first diagnosis as a hypothesis. Check it across more than one item when possible.

### 2.3 Keep a useful error record

Record these fields:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Work | Skill, task family, and date |
| Conditions | Time, assistance, previous exposure, and interruptions |
| Original response | What you actually entered or said |
| Correct evidence | The exact passage, audio point, data, or criterion involved |
| Cause | The most likely failed operation |
| Repair | A small action directed at that operation |
| Transfer check | Fresh material used to check the repair |
| Result | What improved and what still failed |

Use an observable label. Write “I treated missing information as false.” This is more useful than “I am bad at Reading.”

Keep the original response. Store a revision separately. Otherwise, you cannot inspect the change.

### 2.4 Build usable vocabulary

Vocabulary supports all four skills. Recognition and production are different abilities.

You may recognise a word in a passage but fail to retrieve it while speaking. You may retrieve a word but use the wrong grammatical pattern.

Use this vocabulary cycle:

1. Read or listen to material you can understand sufficiently.
2. Notice a useful or unfamiliar item.
3. Infer a possible meaning from context.
4. Check the meaning in a reliable dictionary.
5. Record the useful form and pattern.
6. Retrieve it later without looking.
7. Use it in a new sentence or response.
8. Check the use and correct it.

Choose input you can sustain. Include broad topics such as education, work, health, technology, society, and the environment. These topics support language development. They are not predictions of your test.

### 2.5 Make a complete word record

A useful record contains:

- The headword or phrase.
- Its meaning in this context.
- Its part of speech.
- Its pronunciation and main stress.
- Its usual word partners.
- Its grammatical pattern.
- A useful related form, when needed.
- A sentence you make.
- A translation, when it helps.

Example:

> **allocate** — give a resource to a purpose.  
> **Pattern:** allocate time or money to something; allocate resources for a purpose.  
> **Example:** The council allocated more money to maintenance.

A copied dictionary paragraph is not evidence that you can use the word.

### 2.6 Review by retrieval

Useful review methods include:

1. Read a definition and recall the word.
2. Read the word and recall its meaning.
3. Match words to contextual definitions.
4. Group words by topic, form, or sound.
5. Use several words in a short connected account.
6. Revisit the words after a gap.
7. Use them again in fresh Writing or Speaking work.

A word list or bilingual flashcard can support learning. Add context, retrieval, and accurate use.

There is no universal number of new words that every learner can or must learn each day.

### 2.7 Check a proposed synonym

Before changing a word, check five things:

1. Meaning.
2. Scope.
3. Grammar.
4. Collocation.
5. Register.

If the replacement changes one of these, it may change the answer.

Examples:

- **Residents** are people who live in a place. They are not necessarily citizens.
- **Citizens** have a membership or legal relationship to a state. Not every resident is a citizen.
- **Adolescents** are a narrower age group than children in general.
- **Household** means people living together. It does not always mean family.
- **Revenue** is income before costs. It is not profit.
- **Address a problem** can mean discuss it or act on it. It does not prove that the problem was solved.

Repeat a precise word when a replacement would distort the meaning.

### 2.8 Develop grammatical control

Grammar expresses relationships. A complex form is useful when it makes the intended relationship clear.

Common repair targets include:

- Subject–verb agreement.
- Article use.
- Countable and uncountable nouns.
- Singular and plural forms.
- Tense and time reference.
- Verb patterns and prepositions.
- Comparatives.
- Sentence boundaries.
- Pronoun reference.
- Word order.

Use your own errors to set priorities.

Repair sequence:

1. Identify the meaning you intended.
2. Correct the original sentence.
3. State the relevant pattern.
4. Make a new sentence with that pattern.
5. Check it in a fresh response.

Use simple and complex sentences with control. Do not force complexity into every sentence. Do not remove useful complexity merely to make every sentence short.

### 2.9 Make meaning easy to follow

Check whether a reader or listener can follow each idea on the first attempt.

If a sentence contains several weakly connected ideas, divide it. If a sentence needs a contrast or condition, state that relationship clearly.

Use active voice when it makes the actor and action clear. Use passive voice when the process or result matters more than the actor.

Clear language does not mean limited ideas. It means that the language shows the relationship between the ideas.

## 3. Listening

### 3.1 Understand the four contexts

| Part | Context | Main listening demand |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Everyday social conversation | Accurate details, such as names, numbers, dates, and arrangements |
| 2 | Everyday social monologue | Follow a description, explanation, route, or sequence |
| 3 | Educational or training discussion | Track people, views, reasons, agreement, and changed decisions |
| 4 | Academic monologue | Follow a sustained explanation and its structure |

The recording sets the pace. You cannot manage it like a Reading passage.

Each part contains ten questions. On computer-delivered Listening, enter answers as you listen. There are normally two minutes at the end to review them. Do not expect the older paper test's ten-minute answer-transfer period. Check the instructions for your booking. [British Council delivery comparison](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/blog/ielts-on-paper-vs-computer-how-to-choose)

Questions follow the order of information in the recording. Option letters do not have to follow that order. The wording can be paraphrased. [Official Listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening)

### 3.2 Use the preview period

Before the relevant audio starts:

1. Read the instruction.
2. Identify the answer form.
3. Check the number of answers required.
4. Check the word and number limit.
5. Read the title and headings.
6. Identify the situation and likely sequence.
7. Predict the kind of information required.
8. Compare the options when options are supplied.
9. Note the distinction that will decide the answer.

Possible answer types include a person, place, time, price, quantity, object, action, or reason.

For a sentence gap, inspect the grammar. A gap after an article may need a noun phrase. A plural cue can affect the form.

A prediction directs attention. It does not determine the answer before the recording.

Do not spend the whole preview period writing a long synonym list. Use a few likely meaning cues.

### 3.3 Listen for the complete message

Follow who says what, when it applies, and why it matters.

Watch for:

- An original plan followed by a correction.
- A suggestion followed by rejection.
- A price followed by an exception.
- A date followed by a new date.
- Agreement on one point but disagreement on another.
- A current feature compared with a former or planned feature.
- A negative that changes the answer.
- A word repeated in an incorrect option.

Example:

> “We first booked Room 14. The projector is broken, so we will use Room 18.”

If the question asks for the final room, the answer is Room 18.

Do not stop listening when you hear the first plausible answer.

### 3.4 Recognise paraphrase

The recording may express the question's idea with different words.

| Question wording | Possible recorded meaning |
|---|---|
| Cost | The amount you pay |
| Main difficulty | The problem that caused the most trouble |
| Current location | Where it is now |
| Reason for choosing | Why the speaker selected it |
| Disadvantage | A problem or limitation |

These are examples of meaning relationships. They are not guaranteed word substitutions.

Compare the whole proposition. A familiar keyword can occur inside a rejected choice.

### 3.5 Separate the important distractors

| Pattern | Risk | Decision method |
|---|---|---|
| Changed arrangement | Record the old information | Follow the final relevant decision |
| Similar options | Choose without noticing the difference | Compare person, reason, time, condition, or attitude |
| Singular/plural | Recognise the noun but enter the wrong form | Hear the ending and check the sentence |
| Unusual name | Replace the spelling with a familiar name | Record the spelling actually given |
| Similar sounds | Guess from familiarity | Use spelling and surrounding meaning |
| Negative | Miss not, never, or an exception | Check what is excluded or denied |
| Quoted view | Assign one person's view to another | Track attribution |
| Current versus future | Label a planned feature as existing | Identify the time or status requested |

Treat these as meaning checks. Do not assume every sentence contains a trick.

### 3.6 Form and note completion

A form organises specific details. Use the labels to predict each answer type.

Common details include:

- Names and addresses.
- Telephone or reference numbers.
- Dates and times.
- Prices and quantities.
- Objects or materials.
- A required action.

Check whether part of the answer is already printed. A unit, article, or currency symbol can already be present.

If the instruction requires one word, do not add an explanation. Copy the required form accurately.

### 3.7 Summary, table, and flow-chart completion

Use the structure before listening:

- Read a table's row and column headings.
- Read a summary for the main topic.
- Follow a flow chart's stage order.
- Use completed information to understand the relationship.
- Predict the grammatical form of each gap.

During the recording, locate the relevant stage or category. Enter the detail that fits both the recording and the structure.

A nearby fact may be true but belong in a different row or stage.

### 3.8 Sentence completion and short answers

Read the whole sentence or question.

For sentence completion:

1. Identify what is missing.
2. Predict the grammar and meaning.
3. Follow the relevant recording section.
4. Enter the required words.
5. Check the completed sentence.

For a short answer:

1. Identify the question word.
2. Listen for the precise information requested.
3. Answer within the stated limit.

Do not give a reason when the question asks for a place. Do not add unnecessary words around a correct detail.

### 3.9 Multiple choice

Use this procedure:

1. Read the exact question.
2. Identify its scope.
3. Compare the options.
4. Restate the difference in plain language.
5. Listen through the relevant exchange.
6. Eliminate options that the recording rejects.
7. Select the best-supported answer.
8. Return attention to the next item.

Important scope distinctions include:

- What one speaker thinks versus what both speakers agree on.
- A mentioned advantage versus the main reason for a decision.
- An earlier opinion versus the final opinion.
- A fact about an option versus the reason that option was selected.

The recording can mention every option. Mention alone does not make an option correct.

If the task requires two answers, enter two. Follow the actual instruction.

### 3.10 Matching

Matching connects an item with a feature, person, place, or description.

Before listening:

1. Read the option list.
2. Identify a short meaning cue for each option.
3. Check whether an option can be used more than once.
4. Check whether the response must be a letter or words.

During the recording:

1. Identify the current item or speaker.
2. Follow its complete description or view.
3. Check any correction or qualification.
4. Select the supported match.

Do not assume that the last unused option must be correct. Reuse and unused-option rules depend on the task.

### 3.11 Maps, plans, and diagrams

Use a route and relationship method.

**Before listening:**

1. Read the required answer form.
2. Inspect the title and labels.
3. Find the entrance or starting point.
4. Check orientation or compass directions.
5. Identify roads, paths, gates, and landmarks.
6. Predict the location language that may occur.

**During listening:**

1. Establish the speaker's starting position.
2. Follow each movement in sequence.
3. Use named landmarks to maintain position.
4. Distinguish current, former, moved, closed, and planned features.
5. Verify the final location.
6. Enter the requested label.

Useful relationships include:

- Next to and beside.
- Opposite and across from.
- Between.
- Beyond and past.
- At the end of.
- On the corner.
- Inside and outside.
- To the north, south, east, or west.
- On the left or right of the route.

Left and right depend on the direction of travel. Do not apply your own viewpoint without checking the route.

A map question may require a letter. Do not enter the place name when the instruction requests a letter.

### 3.12 Educational discussion

Track the discussion's changing structure:

- The issue under discussion.
- The person making a proposal.
- The reason for the proposal.
- Another person's response.
- Agreement, partial agreement, or disagreement.
- A revised plan.
- The final relevant conclusion.

A speaker may describe someone else's opinion. Keep reported views separate from the speaker's own view.

A tutor's criticism can concern one part of a proposal. Do not interpret it as rejection of every part.

Use wording and delivery together to interpret attitude. Intonation alone is not a reliable answer shortcut.

### 3.13 Academic monologue

Use headings to anticipate the lecture's structure.

Listen for:

- Definitions.
- Classification.
- Sequence.
- Cause and effect.
- Comparison.
- Examples.
- A change of topic.
- A summary or conclusion.

An example supports an idea. It is not necessarily the main idea.

If an unknown term is not needed for the answer, continue. If the term blocks the meaning, identify it during later review.

### 3.14 Enter answers accurately

Check:

1. The word limit.
2. The number limit.
3. Spelling.
4. Singular or plural form.
5. The exact requested detail.
6. Any article or unit already printed.
7. Whether the task requires a letter.

Articles and prepositions count as words. Official Listening guidance treats hyphenated forms such as check-in as one word. Follow the task's instruction. [Listening completion rules](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening)

A correct idea in the wrong answer form can lose the mark.

Use capitals or lower case as permitted. All capitals can be a personal consistency choice. They are not a compulsory route to a correct Listening answer. Spelling still matters.

### 3.15 Recover after a missed answer

Use a short recovery sequence:

1. Accept that one item is uncertain.
2. Enter a best-supported choice if possible.
3. Stop reconstructing the missed sentence.
4. Find the next question's topic or signpost.
5. Rejoin the recording.

One missed answer does not have to cause several more misses.

Correct an entered answer quickly when needed. Do not spend the next answer period making a perfect correction to the previous item.

### 3.16 Diagnose a Listening error

| Observation | Possible cause | Evidence to inspect | Useful repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| The answer is clear in the transcript but not in the audio | Recognition of connected speech | The exact phrase and word boundaries | Short transcription and phrase replay |
| The transcript also contains unknown language | Vocabulary or grammar | Meaning of the relevant phrase | Contextual language study |
| An old answer was selected | Missed correction or time frame | Original information and revision | Track original → change → final |
| The correct information was heard but entered wrongly | Spelling, form, or instruction | Entered answer versus key | Focused answer-entry repair |
| One question family causes repeated errors | Task method | Directions and decision procedure | Worked examples for that family |
| Discussion items fail | Speaker or argument tracking | Attribution and final views | Summarise each speaker's position |
| Lecture items fail | Structure, vocabulary, or sustained comprehension | Headings and relevant passages | Map a short academic explanation |
| Errors increase later | Several possible causes | Vocabulary, method, attention, and pace | Isolate the cause before adding volume |
| Errors appear scattered | Attention, hearing, or task confusion | The exact missed moments | Check each cause separately |

These are possible explanations. The position of an error does not prove its cause.

### 3.17 Use micro-listening

Micro-listening examines a short difficult segment.

1. Select the sentence that caused the problem.
2. Listen without the transcript.
3. Record what you can identify.
4. Replay to inspect the unclear sounds or boundaries.
5. Check the transcript.
6. Confirm the meaning.
7. Listen again with the text closed.
8. Check the skill with a fresh segment later.

Reduced speed can help diagnosis. Return to normal pace when the phrase becomes clear.

Do not replay indefinitely without checking the actual wording. Repeated guessing can reinforce an incorrect interpretation.

### 3.18 Build concentration and accent familiarity

Use manageable periods of focused listening. Increase the duration gradually.

A suggested ladder such as 5, 7, 10, 15, then 30 minutes is an option. Adjust it to the learner.

Choose material with the relevant demand:

- Everyday arrangements for detail recognition.
- Clear monologues for sequence.
- Discussions for speaker and opinion tracking.
- Lectures for sustained academic meaning.

Use a range of accents. Official examples include British, Australian, New Zealand, and North American varieties. Broader listening can also help.

Interesting podcasts and talks develop English. They do not reproduce every IELTS task or answer-entry condition.

Short breathing or attention routines can help some learners settle. Use them as optional comfort tools. They do not replace language or task diagnosis.

### 3.19 Listening pitfalls

- Selecting the first familiar word.
- Ignoring a correction or negative.
- Confusing arrival time with start time.
- Confusing an included item with an excluded item.
- Assigning a view to the wrong speaker.
- Ignoring singular or plural form.
- Entering more words than permitted.
- Writing the place name when a letter is required.
- Allowing one uncertain item to consume the next item.
- Treating every unknown word as essential.
- Calling every late error a concentration problem.
- Using repeated audio as proof of independent readiness.
- Assuming one spelling error creates a fixed band loss.
- Assuming a delivery preference guarantees a better result.

## 4. Reading

### 4.1 Understand the Reading task

Reading tests how accurately you understand written English under a time limit.

There are 40 questions in three sections. The total time is 60 minutes. This time includes answer entry. There is no separate Reading answer-transfer period.

Academic Reading uses longer texts with an academic or general-interest style. The texts can contain description, explanation, argument, or discussion. You do not need specialist subject knowledge to answer from the text.

General Training Reading moves through everyday information, workplace information, and a longer general-interest text. Its text profile differs from Academic Reading. Do not use the Academic raw-score conversion as the General Training conversion.

Read the instructions for each question set. They control the response type, number of answers, word limit, and reuse of options. See the official [Academic Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading) and [General Training Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-reading).

### 4.2 Use five operations

Do not apply one reading speed to every task.

1. **Decode the question.** Identify the required information and response form.
2. **Skim for structure.** Read for the topic, paragraph purpose, and main argument.
3. **Scan for location.** Search for an anchor, such as a name, date, technical term, or related meaning.
4. **Read closely.** Examine the relevant sentence and its surrounding context.
5. **Decide from evidence.** Compare the exact question with the exact meaning in the passage.

Skimming and scanning locate and organize information. They do not replace close reading when the answer depends on a qualification, comparison, or implied relationship.

A difficult task may need more than one pass. A simple item may not need all five operations.

### 4.3 Build a passage map

A passage map is a short record of what each paragraph does.

Useful labels include:

- Introduces the problem.
- Describes the old method.
- Explains the experiment.
- Gives a counterargument.
- Compares two explanations.
- Reports the result.
- Describes a limitation.

A paragraph can discuss the same topic as another paragraph but perform a different function. This distinction is important for headings.

Do not copy a long summary while the clock runs. A few words can be enough to help you return to the relevant location.

### 4.4 Identify the full claim

Separate these parts of a statement:

- **Who or what:** all residents, some residents, one researcher, or the writer.
- **Action or condition:** increased, remained stable, was required, or was suggested.
- **Time:** before, during, after, now, or in the future.
- **Place or group:** one country, one age group, or the whole sample.
- **Strength:** may, often, always, only, most, or every.
- **Relationship:** cause, association, comparison, sequence, or purpose.

Example:

> Passage: Some adults used the service after its price fell.

This does not establish that all adults used it. It does not prove that the price reduction caused their decision. It does not state that they had never used it before.

An answer can be wrong because of one changed qualifier. A shared keyword does not make the whole claim correct.

### 4.5 True / False / Not Given

These questions compare a statement with information in the text.

- **True:** the passage supports the statement.
- **False:** the passage supports an incompatible statement.
- **Not Given:** the passage does not provide enough information to confirm or contradict the statement.

Use this procedure:

1. Read the statement as a complete claim.
2. Locate the relevant passage section.
3. Read before and after the likely evidence.
4. Identify the part that determines the decision.
5. Ask whether there is support, contradiction, or insufficient information.
6. Enter the label requested by the instructions.

Example:

> Passage: The museum opened in 1998. It now receives about 50,000 visitors each year.

- “The museum opened before 2000.” → True.
- “The museum opened in 2001.” → False.
- “The museum received 50,000 visitors in its first year.” → Not Given.

The last claim is not false merely because the number is not stated for that year.

Do not use outside knowledge. Do not assume every question set must contain all three labels. Do not decide from a presumed label pattern.

If a claim compares two things, you need evidence for that comparison. A figure for one thing alone may be insufficient.

### 4.6 Yes / No / Not Given

These questions concern the writer's views or claims.

- **Yes:** the view agrees with the writer's position.
- **No:** it contradicts the writer's position.
- **Not Given:** the writer's position on that claim cannot be determined.

Track attribution carefully:

- “Some critics argue…” reports a view.
- “This argument is unconvincing…” evaluates that view.
- “The evidence suggests…” states a qualified conclusion.

A quoted scientist's view is not automatically the writer's view. A writer can describe an argument and then reject it.

Preserve degrees of certainty. “Could help” does not mean “will solve.”

### 4.7 Matching headings

A heading represents a paragraph's central idea or purpose. It does not merely name an interesting detail.

A useful gist-first method is:

1. Read the paragraph.
2. State its main job in a few plain words.
3. Distinguish the main point from examples.
4. Compare your summary with the headings.
5. Reject headings that are too narrow, too broad, or about another paragraph.
6. Recheck the closest options against the whole paragraph.

The first or last sentence can help. Neither position guarantees the main idea. A paragraph can begin with a previous argument and then challenge it.

If several headings share a topic word, compare their functions. “Why the method failed” differs from “How the method was developed.”

Do not choose a heading because one unusual word appears in both places.

A paragraph may contain several examples. Ask what those examples jointly demonstrate.

There are more heading options than paragraphs to match. Some options remain unused. Do not use the same heading twice. Preserve any completed example when working through the remaining matches.

### 4.8 Matching information to paragraphs

This task asks where a specific detail appears. The detail may be:

- A reason.
- An example.
- A description.
- A comparison.
- A reference to a problem.
- An explanation of a process or result.

Read the requested detail closely. Use the passage map to identify likely paragraphs. Then inspect those paragraphs for the exact information.

This is different from matching headings. The answer can be a small detail inside a paragraph whose main topic is broader.

Check whether a paragraph letter can be used more than once. Do not assume that every paragraph is used or that answers follow passage order.

Some learners leave this family until they know the passage better. This is an optional sequencing strategy. Check whether it improves your own timing and accuracy.

### 4.9 Matching features, names, or categories

These tasks connect statements with a person, period, place, theory, or other feature.

1. Identify the feature list.
2. Locate its appearances in the passage.
3. Read what is attributed to each feature.
4. Track pronouns and references across sentences.
5. Match the whole statement, not a shared noun.
6. Check the rules for reusing an option.

A person can appear several times. Their first appearance may not contain the required view. A later paragraph can describe a change in their opinion.

Starting with a name that appears only once can reduce searching. This is a useful option, not a rule.

Do not confuse the researcher who conducted a study with another researcher who criticized it.

### 4.10 Matching sentence endings

The beginning supplies part of a claim. The ending must complete both its grammar and its meaning.

1. Read the sentence beginning.
2. Predict the type of completion needed.
3. Locate the relevant information.
4. Compare possible endings with that information.
5. Read the complete selected sentence.

Grammatical fit eliminates some options. It cannot prove the answer. Several endings may be grammatically possible.

Check subject reference, cause and effect, time, and logical scope.

### 4.11 Sentence completion

Identify what the gap requires: a person, object, place, quantity, condition, or other information.

Use grammar to predict the answer form. For example, an article before the gap can signal a singular countable noun. This is a prediction, not proof.

Locate the evidence. If instructions require words from the passage, copy those words accurately. Do not replace them with your own synonym.

Check:

- Word limit.
- Spelling.
- Singular or plural form.
- Whether the answer already includes a unit or article printed outside the gap.
- Whether the completed sentence preserves the passage meaning.

In these completion instructions, a normally hyphenated word counts as one word. Do not add artificial hyphens to evade a word limit.

### 4.12 Summary and note completion

First identify the part of the passage summarized by the task. Read the whole summary briefly to understand its logic.

Then work gap by gap:

1. Predict the information and grammatical form.
2. Locate the corresponding meaning in the passage.
3. Read the relevant context closely.
4. Select the answer using the required method.
5. Read the completed summary for coherence.

There are two different answer methods:

- Select words from a supplied option list.
- Take words from the passage within a stated limit.

Follow the method in the instructions. Do not mix them.

A summary can paraphrase the passage heavily. Search for meaning, not only repeated words.

### 4.13 Table and flow-chart completion

Read the row and column headings before choosing an answer. They define the relationship between the entries.

For a table, ask what each row compares. For a flow chart, follow the arrows and stages.

A gap may require an input, action, result, location, date, or condition. Use neighbouring entries to predict the required type.

Check sequence and dependency. Something that occurs before a stage is not necessarily its cause.

Apply the same word-limit and exact-copy checks as other completion tasks.

### 4.14 Diagram labels

Inspect the diagram before searching the passage.

Identify:

- The object or process.
- Label lines and arrow endpoints.
- Orientation and relative position.
- Existing labels.
- The part or stage each gap refers to.

Locate the description. Connect the wording to the correct visual part.

You do not need to invent specialist knowledge. A label should come from the passage evidence and the task instructions.

Do not enter the whole object name when the line points to one component.

### 4.15 Short-answer questions

Read the question word and the exact information requested.

- **Who:** person or group.
- **Where:** place.
- **When:** time or date.
- **What:** specified object, action, or information.
- **How many / how much:** quantity.

Find the answer in the passage. Keep it concise and within the limit. Extra words can invalidate an otherwise correct response.

Answer the requested detail. A nearby relevant phrase may answer a different question.

### 4.16 Multiple choice

1. Identify the question's focus.
2. Notice restrictions such as “main,” “according to,” or “in the final paragraph.”
3. Locate the relevant section.
4. Decide what the passage says before committing to an option.
5. Compare every serious option with the full evidence.
6. Select the option that answers the stem accurately.

Common distractors include:

- A true statement that does not answer this question.
- A statement with one correct part and one unsupported part.
- A stronger claim than the passage makes.
- A reason mentioned but not identified as the main reason.
- A view assigned to the wrong person.
- A reversed comparison or cause.

Elimination helps when you can explain why an option is wrong. “It sounds less natural” is weak evidence.

Some multiple-choice tasks require more than one selection. Check the number required and how to enter the selected letters.

### 4.17 Use order carefully

Several question families commonly track passage order. Other matching tasks may not.

Order can help narrow a search. It does not override contradictory evidence or the instructions.

Do not assume that all question sets jointly form one uninterrupted sequence. A new set can return to another part of the passage.

Summary, table, flow-chart, and diagram answers need not follow passage order. They commonly draw on one passage area. Use the task structure and evidence to navigate.

If you lose the location, use a reliable anchor and rebuild it. Avoid repeatedly reading the whole passage without a purpose.

### 4.18 Handle unfamiliar vocabulary

Ask whether the unknown word is essential to the answer.

If it is not essential, continue. If it matters, use:

- Its grammatical role.
- A definition or restatement nearby.
- An example.
- A contrast.
- A familiar word family.
- The paragraph's main idea.

An inferred meaning can support a decision. Do not treat a guess as certain when the question depends on a precise distinction.

During study, verify the word afterwards. Record useful language in context. Do not stop to collect every unknown word during timed work.

Reading is more than a synonym test. Paraphrase recognition matters, but so do reasoning, reference, sentence relationships, and evidence judgment.

### 4.19 Manage the hour

Protect time for all three sections and answer entry.

A workable plan contains:

- A rough time budget for each section.
- A limit on unproductive searching.
- A method to mark unresolved items.
- Time to check missing or mistyped answers.

Sections need not take equal time. A suggestion such as 30–35 minutes for the first two sections can be tested in practice. It is not an official allocation.

When progress stops, make a provisional decision or mark the item and move on. Return if time remains.

If the passage is difficult, simplify the operation. Locate one requested detail or determine one paragraph's purpose. Panic-driven rereading rarely produces a useful map.

### 4.20 Diagnose Reading errors

For each wrong or uncertain answer, record the following:

1. What did I think the question required?
2. Where did I look?
3. Which exact words support the correct answer?
4. Why did my answer fail?
5. Which operation needs repair?

Use the error type to select the repair:

| Evidence from the attempt | Likely repair |
|---|---|
| You could not understand the relevant sentence even without a timer | Work on language, reference, and close reading |
| You understood the sentence but chose the wrong label | Practise the decision rule for that question family |
| You knew the answer but entered too many words | Practise instructions and answer entry |
| You repeatedly searched in the wrong paragraph | Improve passage mapping and anchor selection |
| You found a detail but missed the paragraph's main point | Practise gist and paragraph function |
| You were accurate with extra time but incomplete at 60 minutes | Add gradual pacing work after confirming accuracy |
| Most errors occurred in one family | Review that family with fresh examples |
| You changed a correct answer without new evidence | Require a textual reason before changing it |

One attempt can suggest a problem. Confirm the pattern with more evidence before rebuilding the whole study plan.

### 4.21 Improve speed without removing understanding

First establish an accurate operation. Then reduce the time available gradually.

For example, a learner who is accurate in 90 minutes may try 80, then 70, then 60. These are adjustable training steps, not a required schedule.

If untimed answers are also wrong, a faster timer will not repair the underlying problem.

Use short targeted work for a weak question family. Return to fresh mixed work to check transfer.

Regular reading develops familiarity and vocabulary. Choose material you can follow and continue consistently. A familiar book with its audio can be useful. Choose audio speed for understanding; there is no required speed multiplier or fixed 30-day formula.

### 4.22 Reading pitfalls

- Using outside knowledge instead of passage evidence.
- Treating absent information as a contradiction.
- Ignoring a word such as “some,” “only,” or “before.”
- Selecting a heading from an example.
- Matching one keyword without reading the relationship.
- Assuming all matching tasks follow passage order.
- Assuming an option cannot be reused without reading the instructions.
- Copying the wrong grammatical form.
- Forgetting answer-entry time.
- Reading every part equally slowly.
- Skimming a sentence that needs close analysis.
- Repeating full sets without examining mistakes.
- Measuring readiness with remembered answers.
- Following a new trick before understanding the task it is meant to solve.

## 5. Writing foundations

### 5.1 Understand the work before choosing language

Writing has three different purposes:

- **Academic Task 1:** report the supplied visual information.
- **General Training Task 1:** write a letter for a specified situation and reader.
- **Task 2:** develop a response to the exact essay question.

Use the purpose to select the content, structure, and register.

A report does not need an opinion argument. A letter does not need an essay introduction. An essay does not succeed by discussing the broad topic while ignoring the actual question.

### 5.2 Know the four assessment criteria

| Criterion | Question to ask during review |
|---|---|
| Task Achievement, Task 1 | Did I fulfil this task accurately, sufficiently, and appropriately? |
| Task Response, Task 2 | Did I answer every demand and develop a clear, relevant response? |
| Coherence and Cohesion | Can the reader follow the order and relationships of the ideas? |
| Lexical Resource | Do my words express the meaning precisely, naturally, and with appropriate range? |
| Grammatical Range and Accuracy | Do I control the sentence forms needed to express the message? |

The four criteria have equal weight within a task. Task 2 has twice the weight of Task 1. A weakness can constrain the result, but the lowest criterion does not automatically become the entire Writing band. [IELTS scoring](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail)

Use the criteria together. Strong vocabulary cannot repair an unanswered instruction. A relevant argument can still be difficult to follow if sentences repeatedly break down.

The notes below translate the criteria into practical review questions. They do not replace the published [Writing assessment criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf).

### 5.3 Separate coherence from cohesion

**Coherence** is the logical organization of the message.

Check that:

- Each paragraph has a clear job.
- Ideas appear in a useful order.
- A reason supports the claim it follows.
- An example demonstrates the stated point.
- The conclusion follows from the discussion.

**Cohesion** is the language that connects the message.

Useful tools include:

- Pronouns with clear references.
- Repetition of necessary key terms.
- Accurate synonyms where available.
- Demonstratives such as “this change,” with a clear antecedent.
- Conjunctions and transition phrases that express real relationships.

Adding “moreover” does not create a logical addition. Adding “therefore” does not prove a cause.

Do not begin every sentence with a visible linker. Let the content, sentence structure, and reference system carry some connections.

### 5.4 Choose words by function and meaning

Before using a word, check:

1. Does it mean what I intend here?
2. Does it fit the sentence grammar?
3. Does it combine naturally with nearby words?
4. Does it fit the register?
5. Can I spell it accurately?

A precise ordinary word can be more effective than a rare inaccurate word.

However, “keep it clear” does not mean “use only basic vocabulary.” Develop range through useful distinctions, topic language, collocations, and accurate paraphrase.

Repeat a technical or topic term when alternatives would change the meaning. Countries are not automatically kingdoms, provinces, or states. Plastic bottles are not accurately described as all artificial containers.

### 5.5 Build grammatical range through meaning

Use grammar to express relationships:

- A simple sentence can make a clear main claim.
- A subordinate clause can show a reason or condition.
- A relative clause can identify a group or object.
- A comparison can show a difference accurately.
- A passive can focus on a process or result.
- A modal can qualify certainty or obligation.

Complex grammar means clause relationships. It does not mean a long sentence full of difficult words.

Example:

> A shorter commute can reduce stress because workers spend less time travelling.

The reason clause adds meaning. It is useful complexity.

If a sentence contains several unrelated points, split it. If short sentences conceal an important relationship, connect them clearly.

Do not apply a rule that passive voice, long sentences, or every repetition is always wrong. Judge whether the construction serves the task accurately.

### 5.6 Use a three-stage production process

1. **Plan the meaning.** Decide what the answer needs to say.
2. **Draft the response.** Express that plan in connected text.
3. **Check the result.** Compare the draft with the task and your known error patterns.

These stages can overlap slightly. Keeping their main purposes separate reduces the burden of inventing an argument while checking every article.

During study, take enough time to learn the procedure. Then reduce the time gradually. Do not confuse a guided, untimed success with independent timed performance.

### 5.7 Manage the shared hour

A common working allocation is approximately 20 minutes for Task 1 and 40 minutes for Task 2. This is a planning method, not two separately enforced timers.

Doing Task 2 first is an option. It can protect time for the more heavily weighted task. It can also leave a poorly managed learner too little time for Task 1.

Try an order during realistic preparation. Choose an order that lets you complete both tasks and check them.

A sample Task 2 split of 10 minutes planning, 25 drafting, and 5 checking is also optional. A learner's needs can differ.

Do not spend most of the period perfecting an opening sentence. Do not count every word repeatedly while neglecting completion and review.

### 5.8 Respect minimum length without padding

Task 1 requires at least 150 words. Task 2 requires at least 250 words.

Teacher targets such as 160–200 for Task 1 or 260–300 for Task 2 can provide a planning margin. They are not official maximums or automatic high-score ranges.

More words create more opportunities for development and more opportunities for irrelevance or error. Write enough to complete the task well within the available time.

A short answer can lack coverage or support. A long answer can remain shallow if it repeats the same claim.

### 5.9 Write connected responses

These notes use tables and bullet points for reference. Your Writing response should use the appropriate connected form: a report, letter, or essay.

Do not submit lesson headings, planning fragments, or bullet lists as the response. Use paragraphs that a reader can follow. Avoid copying another person's answer or inserting a memorised essay that does not fit the task.

### 5.10 Review from large meaning to small form

Review in this order:

1. **Task:** Have I answered the actual requirements?
2. **Whole response:** Is the message accurate and consistent?
3. **Paragraph:** Is the point clear and developed?
4. **Sentence:** Is the relationship understandable?
5. **Language detail:** Are tense, agreement, articles, word forms, punctuation, and spelling controlled?

Use your error log to make the last pass specific. If you repeatedly omit plural endings, inspect them deliberately.

During training, reading a draft aloud can expose awkward wording. It is one tool. It does not prove factual accuracy, task coverage, or grammatical correctness.

## 6. Academic Writing Task 1

### 6.1 The reporting principle

Report what the visual shows. Select the important features. Support them with accurate details and relevant comparisons.

Do not invent:

- Causes not supplied by the visual.
- Opinions about whether a change is good.
- Geographic or political classifications not stated.
- Exact values that the chart cannot support.
- Benefits or purposes that the diagram does not show.

The main question is: **Can the supplied visual support this exact sentence?**

Task 1 is an information-transfer task. Read the [official Academic Writing description](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing) for its format and instructions.

### 6.2 Identify the visual's job

Common visual families are:

- Line graph.
- Bar chart.
- Pie chart.
- Table.
- Two or more related visuals.
- Map or plan.
- Process or cycle diagram.

A visual can be:

- **Dynamic:** it shows change over time.
- **Static:** it compares a situation at one time.
- **Sequential:** it shows stages or operations.
- **Spatial:** it shows locations and physical changes.

The visual label alone does not determine the method. A bar chart can show change over time. A pair of pie charts can require both composition and change comparisons.

### 6.3 Decode the information before drafting

Check these items in order:

1. Task statement.
2. Title.
3. Subject or population.
4. Axes and scales.
5. Units.
6. Legend or key.
7. Dates and time range.
8. Categories.
9. Footnotes, projections, or other qualifications.

Identify exactly what is measured. “Percentage of adults unable to obtain treatment” is different from “number of patients treated.”

Read a negative measure carefully. A rise in the percentage unable to receive treatment is not a rise in treatment access.

Do not let an unfamiliar place name stop you if the task only requires reporting its figures.

### 6.4 Find the main features

Step back from individual values.

For data visuals, ask:

- What is the broad direction?
- Which categories are largest or smallest?
- Which groups behave similarly?
- Which category is an exception?
- Does the rank change?
- Is there a major turning point or phase change?
- Do categories converge or diverge?
- Is stability itself an important feature?

Check every superlative. “Highest” needs a defined measure and time. “Largest increase” needs a comparison of changes, not only final values.

Do not say that every series increased if one fell. Do not call every movement a significant fluctuation.

### 6.5 Plan the report

A useful beginner structure is:

1. Introduction.
2. Overview.
3. First detail group.
4. Second detail group.

This structure separates the visual's identity, its main message, and the evidence.

The exact paragraph count is not a rule. The overview can occupy a different appropriate position. Details can be grouped differently when the visual requires it.

A separate opinion-style conclusion is normally unnecessary. The overview already summarizes the main features. End when the reporting task is complete.

### 6.6 Write the introduction

State what the visual presents. Preserve its important facts.

Useful components are:

- Visual or information type.
- Subject.
- Measure or unit.
- Groups or place.
- Time reference.

Example:

> The chart compares the proportion of households using three transport options in 2010 and 2020.

This is an explanatory pattern, not a sentence to memorize for every chart.

Paraphrase by meaning. You can change sentence structure or word form. You do not need to replace every word.

Keep exact years, proper names, and technical labels when appropriate. “From 2010 to 2020” and “between 2010 and 2020” preserve the same endpoints. “During the last decade” may not.

“Shows,” “compares,” and “illustrates” have different uses. Choose a natural reporting verb. Do not force “data” or “illustration” as a universal substitute for every visual noun.

One sentence is often enough. Sentence count itself is not a criterion.

### 6.7 Write an accurate overview

The overview states the visual's main message at a broad level.

A useful planning question is:

> If the reader could remember only a few features, which features would explain this visual best?

This is the source's “headline” idea. It helps selection. It does not replace verification.

For a time-series chart, an overview might identify:

- The overall increase in most categories.
- The category that moved differently.
- A major change in which category was largest.

For a static chart, it might identify:

- The dominant category.
- A broad contrast between groups.
- The smallest share or another important exception.

For a map, it might identify major development and the main unchanged area.

For a process, it might identify the overall transformation and whether the system is linear or cyclical.

Two or three major features can be a useful starting point. There is no mandatory feature count. Avoid a list of detailed figures that hides the main message.

A figure is not forbidden in an overview. Use it only when it helps the broad summary.

An overview must be correct. Merely including a paragraph labelled “overall” does not fulfil the purpose.

### 6.8 Group details for the reader

Choose a visible reason for each group.

Possible groupings include:

- Categories with similar trends.
- Higher and lower categories.
- Increasing and decreasing groups.
- Earlier and later phases.
- Related measures from two visuals.
- Collection stages and processing stages.
- One part of a site and another part.

Do not group countries by geography unless geography helps explain the visual comparison. Do not follow legend order automatically.

A year-by-year description can make cross-year comparison difficult. Use it only when it is the clearest organization.

Introduce the grouping clearly. Then report representative values and useful comparisons.

Do not list every point simply because it is available. Do not omit so much that the report no longer represents the task.

### 6.9 Describe change in phases

A line can contain an initial rise, a plateau, and a final fall. Treat these as meaningful phases.

This is sometimes called **chunking**.

1. Identify where the direction or rate changes materially.
2. Divide the period at those points.
3. State the broad movement in each phase.
4. Support the important movement with selected values.
5. Compare another series where useful.

Choose boundaries from the graph. Do not split every graph at the same midpoint.

Do not describe a series as fluctuating if it simply rises once and falls once without repeated movement that justifies the term.

### 6.10 Use values and approximations accurately

If the value is clear, report it accurately.

If a plotted value is approximate, use a suitable qualifier:

- About.
- Around.
- Approximately.
- Just over.
- Just under.
- Nearly.

“Nearly” normally indicates a value below the reference point. “Just over” indicates a value above it.

Preserve units. Thirty thousand people is not 30 people. A percentage is not a number of people.

Do not add unnecessary decimal precision. A chart grid does not justify a value such as 23.74 if it only supports roughly 24.

Use “respectively” only when the order is clear:

> A and B recorded 12 and 18 units, respectively.

This pairs A with 12 and B with 18. A long unclear list is better rewritten.

### 6.11 Distinguish percentages, percentage points, and proportions

A **percentage** expresses a share out of 100.

A **percentage-point change** is the difference between two percentage values.

A **relative percentage change** compares the change with the starting value.

Example 1: a share falls from 60% to 40%.

- Fall: 20 percentage points.
- Relative fall: 20 ÷ 60 × 100, or about 33.3%.

Example 2: a share rises from 20% to 30%.

- Rise: 10 percentage points.
- Relative rise: 10 ÷ 20 × 100, or 50%.

A category's share can fall while its absolute number rises, if the total grows sufficiently. Do not infer one from the other without the necessary totals.

Useful fractions include a half, a third, a quarter, and a fifth. Check whether the fraction is exact or approximate.

### 6.12 Use trend language with correct grammar

| Function | Verb construction | Noun construction |
|---|---|---|
| Increase | Sales rose sharply | There was a sharp rise in sales |
| Decrease | Demand fell gradually | There was a gradual fall in demand |
| Stability | The figure remained stable | There was little change in the figure |
| Maximum | Output peaked at 80 units | Output reached a peak of 80 units |
| Variation | Prices fluctuated | Prices showed fluctuations |

Check the difference between:

- **To:** the final value. “It rose to 30.”
- **By:** the amount of change. “It rose by 10.”
- **At:** a level. “It remained at 30.”
- **From … to:** the starting and finishing values.

Use adjectives with nouns: “a gradual increase.” Use adverbs with verbs: “increased gradually.”

Describe the scale honestly. A small movement is not necessarily dramatic. A high final value does not prove rapid growth.

### 6.13 Use comparisons accurately

Compare like with like:

- The same unit.
- The same period or clearly identified different periods.
- The intended population.
- The same category definition.

Useful forms include:

- A was higher than B.
- A had a larger share than B.
- The figure for A was twice that for B.
- A and B followed similar patterns.
- A overtook B in the final year.

“Twice as high as” requires a real two-to-one relationship. “Twice higher” can be ambiguous. Prefer the clear form.

A category can overtake another only if their relative positions change. Do not use “overtake” to describe a category that was already larger throughout.

### 6.14 Line graphs

Look for overall direction, phase changes, peaks, lows, crossing points, and gaps between series.

Choose a few anchor values: often a start, a finish, and an important turning point. Add more only when they help represent the pattern.

If several lines move together, summarize that relationship. Explain the exception separately.

Distinguish a steady increase from an irregular increase. A rising endpoint does not make every interval an increase.

### 6.15 Bar charts and tables

Read category labels and scales before ranking values. A truncated scale can make small differences look large.

For a static visual, compare groups and extremes. For a time-based visual, also compare change.

A table can contain many figures. Identify the relationships rather than converting each cell into a sentence.

If the table includes totals and subgroups, avoid counting the total as another independent category.

Select details that demonstrate the overview. Check that different columns use compatible units before making a direct comparison.

### 6.16 Pie charts

Identify what each whole represents. Check whether the pies compare years, groups, locations, or different totals.

Then inspect:

- Largest and smallest shares.
- Broad concentration or distribution.
- Changes in rank.
- Major increases or decreases.
- Similar shares.

Choose a grouping that makes comparisons easy. For one dataset, the largest categories versus the smaller categories may work. For another, increasing versus decreasing categories may be clearer.

Do not force a grouping that leaves one paragraph with little useful content.

A share such as 46.7% can be described as almost half when this approximation suits the comparison. Exact values can also be appropriate.

Do not infer sales revenue, total output, or popularity beyond what the measure supports.

### 6.17 Multiple visuals

Read each visual separately before connecting them.

Identify what they share:

- A time range.
- A population.
- A category.
- A total and its components.

Report supported relationships. If one chart shows visitor numbers and another shows spending, do not claim a causal relationship merely because both rise.

Give adequate attention to both visuals. A report that describes one fully and ignores the other is incomplete.

Avoid comparing a percentage with an absolute total as though they were the same measure.

### 6.18 Maps and plans

First determine the time status:

- Past layout.
- Present layout.
- Proposed future layout.
- Two or more observed layouts.

Identify major changes and important unchanged features.

Common changes include construction, removal, replacement, expansion, relocation, and conversion to another use.

Organize details spatially or by type of change. Use landmarks and orientation that the visual supplies.

Useful forms include:

- To the north of.
- In the centre of.
- Beside.
- Opposite.
- Along the eastern boundary.
- Was replaced by.
- Was converted into.
- Is proposed for.

Do not invent a compass direction when the map does not establish one. Use clear relative positions if necessary.

Match tense to status. A proposed building has not already been built. Do not describe a plan as a completed improvement.

Do not infer benefits such as less congestion or better access unless supplied. Describe the depicted changes.

### 6.19 Process diagrams

Inspect the entire process before writing.

Identify:

- Inputs.
- Stages.
- Arrows and sequence.
- Branches.
- Outputs.
- Return paths.
- Major transformations.

Determine whether it is linear or cyclical. A cycle returns to an earlier point. Do not describe its output as a final endpoint if the diagram shows a return.

For the overview, explain the broad transformation and the overall shape. Mention a stage count only when it is clear and useful.

Group details at a meaningful transition. Collection and initial sorting may form one group; material processing and manufacture may form another. The split depends on the diagram.

Describe the stages in arrow order. Include the important stages and branch relationships.

Use active or passive grammar as appropriate:

- “Workers sort the bottles” focuses on the workers when shown.
- “The bottles are sorted” focuses on the material and operation.

Do not add an agent that the diagram does not identify.

Use sequence language carefully: first, next, after this stage, subsequently, finally. “Finally” may introduce the last described operation, but it should not conceal a continuing cycle.

Keep uncertain details general and accurate. A liquid-like symbol does not prove that the liquid is water. A machine image does not establish every mechanical operation it might perform.

A recycling diagram does not automatically justify a paragraph about environmental benefits.

### 6.20 Review an Academic Task 1 report

**Content check:**

- Does the introduction identify the right visual and measure?
- Does the overview state accurate main features?
- Do the details support the overview?
- Are all supplied visuals represented?
- Are important comparisons included?
- Are figures, dates, units, and category names correct?
- Is every inference supported?

**Language check:**

- Are tense and time references consistent?
- Are comparisons complete?
- Do noun and verb constructions work?
- Are singular, plural, and agreement forms correct?
- Are references and transitions clear?
- Is the response at least 150 words?

During study, circle each reported value on the original visual. This creates a direct accuracy check.

### 6.21 Learn the subskills separately

You can improve report writing without drafting a full report every time.

Useful focused activities are:

- Identify a visual's purpose.
- Write an introduction.
- Write an overview without looking at a model.
- Compare two possible detail groupings.
- Convert a value into a correct comparison.
- Correct a misleading percentage statement.
- Rewrite a list of points into a phase-based description.
- Remove unsupported inferences from a process report.

One optional peer check is to give a reader only the overview. Ask whether they can describe the broad shape of the visual. They need not recreate every figure.

Return to complete reports to check whether the subskills work together under the shared time limit.

## 7. General Training Writing Task 1

### 7.1 Identify purpose, reader, and requested action

Read the entire situation before planning the letter.

Identify:

1. Who you are writing to.
2. Your relationship with that person.
3. Why you are writing.
4. The information or action the reader needs.
5. Every bullet point and sub-instruction.
6. Any opening supplied by the task.

A bullet can contain two jobs. “Describe the problem and explain how it affected you” requires both description and effect.

If the task asks for reasons, make sure the response supplies the requested range. Do not overlook a plural instruction.

The letter needs at least 150 words. It can require personal, semi-formal, or formal language. [Official General Training Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-writing)

### 7.2 Choose the register from the situation

**Register** is the level and style of language suited to the reader and purpose.

| Register | Typical relationship | Useful characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Personal or informal | A friend or close relative | Natural personal wording, familiar details, appropriate contractions |
| Semi-formal | A known person in a practical or professional relationship | Polite, clear wording with some personal familiarity |
| Formal | An unknown recipient or a formal institutional situation | Respectful, precise wording; clear purpose and requests |

These are guides. The full prompt controls the decision.

A landlord, teacher, manager, or colleague does not automatically require the same tone in every situation.

Keep the tone consistent. A formal complaint should not suddenly use texting slang. A friendly invitation need not sound like a legal notice.

Do not change every word to make a letter informal. Much useful English is neutral.

### 7.3 Plan each required function

A useful structure is:

1. Suitable greeting.
2. Clear opening purpose.
3. Paragraphs covering the task's required functions.
4. Appropriate closing and name.

Giving one paragraph to each bullet makes coverage easy to inspect. Another organization can work if every function remains clear.

For each bullet, plan:

- The main information.
- A relevant supporting detail.
- Any reason, effect, or requested action.

Do not merely repeat the bullet in different words.

An ordinary plausible detail is enough. Do not spend several minutes choosing an impressive name, venue, or event.

### 7.4 State the purpose early

The reader should quickly know why the letter exists.

Useful functional openings include:

- “I am writing to ask about…”
- “I am writing to explain…”
- “Thank you for…”
- “I am sorry that…”
- “I would like to report…”

Choose the function that fits. Do not force the same opening into every letter.

A personal letter can begin more naturally when the relationship supports it. It still needs a clear purpose.

### 7.5 Develop useful detail

Details should help the recipient understand or act.

For a complaint, explain:

- What happened.
- When or where it happened, if relevant.
- Its practical effect.
- The reasonable remedy requested, if the task asks for one.

For an apology, explain the relevant problem and any corrective action. Avoid a long unrelated story.

For an invitation, explain the event, the reason it matters, and the arrangement or response required.

For a request, make the requested action clear. State the information the reader needs to fulfil it.

For a recommendation or review, explain what was useful and support it with a concrete detail. Address any required problem as well as praise.

### 7.6 Use suitable greetings and closings

Follow a supplied opening exactly where the task requires it.

Common conventional choices include:

- An unnamed formal recipient: “Dear Sir or Madam” with “Yours faithfully.”
- A named formal recipient: “Dear Ms Patel” with “Yours sincerely.”
- A friend: “Dear Sam” with “Best wishes” or “All the best.”

“Kind regards” can fit many polite contexts. The whole relationship and message matter more than treating a phrase as a scoring code.

Do not add a postal address unless the instructions require it. Do not let decorative formatting consume time.

The closing does not need an essay-style summary of every paragraph. A clear final request, thanks, or personal closing can finish the letter.

### 7.7 Control informal language

Contractions such as “I'm” and “can't” can fit a personal letter. Familiar phrasal verbs can also be natural.

Informal does not mean unclear, careless, or full of slang.

Avoid:

- Text-message abbreviations that obscure meaning.
- Emojis.
- Unfamiliar local slang.
- Jokes that distract from the task.
- A postscript inserted where a normal paragraph would be clearer.

There is no useful universal ban on every phrasal verb in every formal context. Choose the expression by actual register and meaning.

### 7.8 Control length and sentence boundaries

A teacher target of 40–60 words per bullet can help estimate development. It is not a quota. Some functions need more explanation than others.

The minimum is 150 words. A 160–200 target is an option, not a maximum.

Use full stops to show completed sentences. Do not join several independent statements with commas alone.

Check capital letters, apostrophes, names, and paragraph breaks. A visible paragraph structure helps the recipient follow the message.

### 7.9 Final letter check

Read the prompt beside the letter.

- Is the purpose clear near the start?
- Does every bullet receive a complete response?
- Are all sub-instructions included?
- Does each detail serve the purpose?
- Is any requested action explicit?
- Does the tone match the relationship throughout?
- Do the greeting and closing fit?
- Is the response connected prose?
- Are grammar, punctuation, and spelling controlled?
- Is the response at least 150 words?

Do not assume a polite tone compensates for missing content.

## 8. Writing Task 2

### 8.1 Answer the question that was asked

Task 2 requires an essay of at least 250 words. The response should develop the exact task using an appropriate style.

Three layers define a prompt:

1. **Topic:** the general subject, such as education.
2. **Specific focus:** the particular issue, such as public funding for adult education.
3. **Instruction:** the decision or explanation requested.

Read all three layers.

An essay about the benefits of education can still miss a question about whether governments should pay adults' tuition fees.

Underline limiting words mentally or in your permitted notes. Examples include “children,” “in cities,” “in the future,” “main,” and “to what extent.”

### 8.2 Convert the prompt into a response plan

Before choosing a structure, state in plain words what you need to do.

Examples of response jobs are:

- Decide how far you agree with a claim.
- Explain two different views and give your own view.
- Describe advantages and disadvantages.
- Decide whether advantages outweigh disadvantages.
- Explain causes and propose solutions.
- Identify problems and propose responses.
- Answer two separate direct questions.

Labels help recognition. They do not replace reading. Two prompts within the same family can require different content.

### 8.3 Opinion and extent questions

Identify the proposition precisely.

Choose a position you can explain consistently. You can agree, disagree, or take a qualified position when that answers the question.

For “to what extent,” make the extent clear. A qualified answer should explain its boundary or condition. It should not drift between incompatible conclusions.

Plan reasons that support your actual position. Do not add a paragraph arguing the opposite merely because you think every essay must be balanced.

If a counterargument helps qualify your view, explain how your judgment deals with it.

### 8.4 Discussion questions

If the task asks you to discuss both views and give your opinion, there are three required functions:

1. Explain one view.
2. Explain the other view.
3. State and support your judgment.

Represent both views fairly. A one-sentence mention of one view may not constitute a developed discussion.

Your opinion can be integrated into the discussion. It does not require an identical sentence in every paragraph.

Make the distinction between the views explicit. Do not write two paragraphs that repeat the same general benefit with different wording.

### 8.5 Advantages and disadvantages

Check whether the task asks only for advantages and disadvantages or also asks which side outweighs the other.

For a plain advantages/disadvantages task, develop the requested positive and negative consequences.

For an outweigh task, add a comparative judgment. Explain why one side matters more overall.

Counting two benefits and one drawback does not prove that benefits outweigh drawbacks. Consider importance, reach, duration, severity, or the possibility of reducing the harm.

An outweigh essay can acknowledge a real disadvantage while explaining why the benefits remain stronger.

A positive-or-negative-development question also requires an evaluation. Identify what has changed. Explain its consequences. Then make the requested judgment about the development as a whole. Do not merely describe the trend.

### 8.6 Causes, problems, effects, and solutions

These terms are different:

- **Cause:** what contributes to the situation.
- **Problem:** an undesirable condition that results or exists.
- **Effect:** a consequence, positive or negative.
- **Solution:** a measure intended to address a cause or problem.

Do not answer a causes question with only harmful effects.

Connect a solution to the stated issue. If the cause is lack of access, a response that only changes attitudes may not address it adequately.

Explain the mechanism:

> What would the measure change, and how would that change reduce the problem?

If the task asks who should act, identify the relevant actor. A vague “something should be done” leaves the solution undeveloped.

### 8.7 Two direct questions

Write a separate short answer to each question before drafting.

Check whether either question contains more than one component.

Allocate enough development to both. Do not let an easy first question consume nearly the whole essay.

Paragraph organization can reflect the two questions, but the final plan must fit the actual content.

The conclusion should not forget one of the two answers.

### 8.8 Generate manageable ideas

You need relevant ideas that you can explain. You do not need specialist originality or an impressive political theory.

A useful idea has three properties:

- It answers the specific task.
- It is reasonable and supportable.
- You can develop it with the English you control.

The source offers two optional prompts:

- **Conversation prompt:** What would I say to a friend discussing this issue calmly?
- **Familiar-answer prompt:** What is a common, plausible reason that directly fits this question?

The first is sometimes called the coffee-shop method. The second is compared with a popular-answer game.

Use either to start thinking. Then screen the idea against the exact question. The most familiar or popular idea is not automatically the best one.

### 8.9 Select fewer ideas and develop them

A shopping list of five reasons can remain less convincing than two explained reasons.

For each candidate idea, ask:

1. What is my point?
2. Why is it true or plausible?
3. How does the mechanism work?
4. What concrete case would illustrate it?
5. How does it answer this task?

Keep the number of ideas manageable. Cover all requested aspects even if that requires more than two points.

Do not add a difficult idea only to appear intelligent. Do not simplify so far that the point becomes vague or loses the required nuance.

### 8.10 Make a brief usable plan

A plan can contain:

- Your direct answer or position.
- The job of each body paragraph.
- A main point for each paragraph.
- A reason or mechanism.
- A possible example.
- Any qualification needed.

Write keywords rather than a draft essay in miniature.

Check the plan before drafting:

- Does it answer the specific focus?
- Does it cover every instruction?
- Are the paragraphs different?
- Can I explain the links?
- Can I finish within the available time?

Prompt analysis comes before planning. Do not select a memorised structure and then force the prompt into it.

### 8.11 Write a direct introduction

An introduction normally needs to identify the issue and establish the response direction.

A useful process is:

1. Restate the issue accurately.
2. Give the answer or position required by the prompt.
3. Indicate the main direction where useful.

Do not add a generic history of humanity. Do not begin with a quotation unrelated to the actual question.

Paraphrase by preserving meaning, not by changing every word. Keep terms that have no accurate convenient substitute.

A phrase such as “this essay will discuss” is not automatically penalised. It also does not replace a clear answer when the task asks for one.

Avoid an introduction so long that it repeats the whole essay before the explanation begins.

### 8.12 Build a focused body paragraph

A useful development pattern is:

1. **Point:** state the paragraph's main claim.
2. **Explanation:** show why or how it follows.
3. **Support:** give a relevant example or concrete detail.
4. **Connection:** make its relevance to the question clear where needed.

This is a flexible scaffold. It is not a required four-sentence template.

Example of the reasoning chain:

> A reliable bus service can help workers accept jobs farther from home. Frequent services make arrival times more predictable, so a longer journey becomes practical. For example, a worker without a car could accept a shift in an industrial area if a bus reached it before the shift began.

The example explains the mechanism. It does not introduce an unrelated advantage.

A paragraph can contain more than one supporting detail when they develop the same central idea.

### 8.13 Make explanation explicit

Do not require the reader to supply a missing logical step.

Weak chain:

> Online courses are convenient. Therefore, they improve education.

More complete chain:

> Online courses remove the need to travel to a classroom. This can allow adults with limited free time to attend lessons regularly. More consistent attendance can support learning.

The improved chain also qualifies the outcome. Convenience can help; it does not guarantee learning.

Check whether the explanation uses circular language. “It is beneficial because it has benefits” adds no mechanism.

### 8.14 Use examples as support

An example should show the point in a concrete situation.

It can be based on ordinary experience or a plausible scenario. It does not need an exact statistic or a named research paper.

Do not invent a university study, survey percentage, or official report to create authority.

A personal example illustrates a possibility. It does not establish that everyone behaves the same way.

If a prompt concerns a broad social issue, connect a personal case to the general mechanism. Avoid allowing the anecdote to become the entire argument.

### 8.15 Express a consistent position

Your position should be understandable across the essay.

Check for contradictions between:

- Introduction and body.
- One body paragraph and another.
- Body and conclusion.

A concession is not a contradiction if you explain it:

> The policy can be costly at first, but its long-term savings can justify that cost.

A claim such as “the policy is entirely ineffective” conflicts with “its benefits are clearly greater” unless the essay resolves the difference.

Do not use “I completely agree” if your argument later depends on major exceptions that make agreement partial.

### 8.16 Compare and qualify precisely

Useful qualification includes:

- Some, many, or most, according to the intended scope.
- Can or may, when the outcome is possible rather than inevitable.
- In some circumstances.
- For groups with a specified need.
- Provided that a necessary condition holds.

Do not weaken every sentence until the position becomes unclear. Qualify where the evidence or logic requires it.

Avoid stereotypes about countries, age groups, professions, or educational systems. Explain the relevant condition instead.

### 8.17 Write the conclusion

The conclusion should close the response logically.

1. Restate the final answer or judgment.
2. Summarize the central reason or balance of reasons.
3. Include both direct answers if the task has two questions.
4. Keep the position consistent with the essay.

Do not introduce a new major argument that the body never develops.

Do not add a generic prediction, recommendation, or slogan unless it belongs to the task and follows from the argument.

A conclusion can be concise. It does not need to copy the introduction sentence by sentence.

### 8.18 Use cohesion without mechanical display

Choose a connector for its relationship:

| Relationship | Possible language | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Also; in addition | Is this a distinct relevant addition? |
| Contrast | However; whereas; by contrast | Are the ideas actually opposed or different? |
| Concession | Although; despite | What is accepted before the main claim? |
| Reason | Because; since | Does the clause give the reason? |
| Result | Therefore; as a result | Does the result follow from the previous claim? |
| Example | For example; for instance | Does the case demonstrate the point? |
| Condition | If; provided that | Is the condition necessary or relevant? |

Grammar differs across these forms. “Although” introduces a clause. “Despite” normally takes a noun phrase or an -ing form.

Do not attach an entire new independent sentence after a comma merely because it begins with “however.” Use suitable sentence punctuation.

Repeated clear topic terms can support cohesion. An uncertain synonym can damage it.

### 8.19 Review the essay in priority order

**Task review:**

- Did I answer the exact focus?
- Did I complete every instruction?
- Is the position or judgment explicit where required?

**Argument review:**

- Does each paragraph contribute a distinct useful point?
- Are reasons explained?
- Do examples support those reasons?
- Are claims proportionate and consistent?
- Does an outweigh judgment actually compare importance?
- Do solutions address the problems or causes identified?

**Language review:**

- Are references clear?
- Do linkers express the intended relationships?
- Are tense, agreement, articles, plurals, and word forms accurate?
- Are sentence boundaries clear?
- Are any words decorative or misleading?
- Is spelling controlled?

Check minimum length and completion. Do not sacrifice an unanswered task component to polish an isolated adjective.

### 8.20 Learn from sample essays actively

Use a sample as evidence to analyse, not as a script.

1. Read the prompt before reading the sample.
2. Generate your own answer and possible plan.
3. Inspect how the sample interprets the task.
4. Identify each paragraph's job.
5. Trace one point through explanation and support.
6. Notice one useful language feature.
7. Compare your decisions with the sample's decisions.
8. Apply the lesson to a different prompt.

A displayed band label is not independent proof of quality. Check the response against the prompt and criteria.

Do not copy an elegant paragraph into an unrelated essay. Useful language functions can transfer; task-specific content must fit the new question.

For a targeted model review, choose one investigation:

- Label each connector as addition, contrast, result, example, or reference. Check whether the relationship is valid.
- Identify how a sentence's tense or clause structure expresses its meaning. Create a new sentence with the same function.
- Classify a suspected grammar error and explain the correction. A sample can contain errors; verify them.
- Compare word choice and collocation. Decide whether a proposed alternative preserves meaning and register.
- Compare two valid responses to the same prompt. Different ideas or wording are not automatically errors.

### 8.21 Turn feedback into a repair

Good feedback identifies:

- The criterion involved.
- The exact location of the problem.
- Why the wording or reasoning fails.
- A possible correction.
- What to practise next.

A useful repair sequence is:

1. Restate the intended meaning in plain words.
2. Correct the reasoning or language.
3. Compare the original and revised versions.
4. Practise the specific pattern.
5. Use it in a fresh response.

Rewriting the same essay can develop control. It cannot by itself show transfer to an unfamiliar task.

Repeatedly writing full essays without diagnosis can leave the same errors unchanged. Combine full responses with targeted work.

### 8.22 Task 2 pitfalls and false shortcuts

- Discussing the topic without answering the instruction.
- Using a fixed four-paragraph essay regardless of the task.
- Giving an unclear or shifting position.
- Confusing causes with effects.
- Listing solutions without explaining their mechanisms.
- Mentioning both sides without making the required judgment.
- Listing many undeveloped ideas.
- Treating an example as proof of a universal claim.
- Inventing studies or precise statistics.
- Replacing every word with a thesaurus alternative.
- Adding a hook, idiom, or linker only for display.
- Assuming longer automatically means better.
- Assuming a fixed percentage of error-free sentences guarantees a band.
- Assuming a wholly memorised essay and a flexible outline are the same thing.
- Believing that one correction, paid course, or simple-language rule guarantees a result.

## 9. Speaking

### 9.1 Understand the interview

Speaking has three parts and lasts 11–14 minutes.

| Part | Main demand | Approximate time |
|---|---|---|
| Part 1 | Answer questions about familiar subjects and personal experience | 4–5 minutes |
| Part 2 | Prepare and give a connected individual talk from a task card | 3–4 minutes in total, including preparation and follow-up |
| Part 3 | Discuss broader issues connected with the Part 2 topic | 4–5 minutes |

Part 2 provides one minute to prepare. The talk lasts one to two minutes. Follow the examiner's timing cues. [Official Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking)

The demand changes across the parts. A short personal answer and an abstract discussion should not use identical response lengths or structures.

### 9.2 Know what is assessed

The four criteria have equal weight:

1. Fluency and Coherence.
2. Lexical Resource.
3. Grammatical Range and Accuracy.
4. Pronunciation.

Review the [official Speaking assessment criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf).

Practical review questions are:

- Can I continue with connected, relevant meaning?
- Can I express and paraphrase the intended idea precisely?
- Can I use suitable grammar with control?
- Can the listener follow my sounds, stress, rhythm, and intonation?

Communication is the central practical aim. It is not a replacement fifth criterion or a reason to ignore grammatical range.

No published criterion awards a separate band for clothing, smiling, eye contact, or a fashionable accent.

### 9.3 Respond naturally to procedural questions

Answer identity and administrative questions directly.

If asked for your name, give your name. Do not attach a memorised biography, hometown speech, and career history.

Be polite in an ordinary way. Do not perform friendliness as a scoring technique.

A first impression is not a separate language score. Continue concentrating on the answers throughout the interview.

### 9.4 Part 1: answer first, then add useful detail

Start with the requested information.

- For “how often,” give a frequency.
- For “which,” identify the choice.
- For “do you prefer,” state the preference.
- For “why,” give a reason.
- For “where,” identify the place.

Then add a relevant reason, explanation, example, or personal detail.

Example:

> I usually walk to work. It takes about fifteen minutes, and the route runs beside a park.

The extra information explains the habit. It does not turn the answer into a long unrelated story.

A one-word response may reveal too little language. A long monologue may become repetitive. There is no required sentence count.

### 9.5 Match the question's scope

If asked for a favourite, choose a favourite and develop it. Do not list every film, city, food, or activity you know.

If asked for several examples, a list can be appropriate. Explain or select from the list as the question requires.

A focused answer reduces the number of ideas you must manage. It also gives you something concrete to explain.

Do not change a personal preference into a claim about everyone. “I find walking relaxing” does not mean all people find it relaxing.

### 9.6 Part 2: decode the task card

Identify:

- The exact person, place, object, event, or experience requested.
- Any category restriction.
- The required time reference.
- Each cue point.
- The final reason or explanation prompt.

Choose an example that actually fits. A familiar business owner does not necessarily fit a card about a famous business person.

Select a manageable example quickly. Do not compare ten possible answers while the preparation minute disappears.

Familiarity helps because you can recall concrete details. It does not justify forcing every card into your favourite topic.

### 9.7 Use the preparation minute

Write short prompts, not full sentences to read aloud.

Useful note categories are:

- Identity or setting.
- Time and place.
- Main event or description.
- One or two memorable details.
- Reason, feeling, or effect.

Connect notes in a sequence that will make sense to a listener.

Use the task card to check that all cue points have a place. Briefly cover each point. Give more time to details that support a connected talk. Equal time per bullet is unnecessary.

Do not plan an entire polished script. The notes should help recall meaning.

### 9.8 Give a connected long turn

Open by identifying the subject. Then develop it through an appropriate sequence.

For an event, time order may be natural. For an object, describe what it is, how you obtained it, how you use it, and why it matters.

Possible detail sources include:

- Appearance or location.
- A specific action.
- A remembered conversation.
- A practical problem.
- A personal reason.
- A feeling and its cause.
- A consequence.

Choose details that serve this card. You do not need every category.

Glance at your notes when needed. Do not rush through the cue points as a checklist and then run out of material.

Try to sustain the talk until the examiner stops you within the allotted period. A talk within the one-to-two-minute window does not need padding merely to reach an exact second.

### 9.9 Use time frames only when relevant

Past–Present–Future is an optional idea generator.

It can help with a topic such as an activity you used to do, your current situation, and whether you might return to it.

Do not force all three time frames into a question that asks about one completed event.

Choose grammar from meaning:

- Completed event: past simple where appropriate.
- Current habit: present simple.
- Earlier background to a past event: an appropriate past form.
- Continuing experience or duration: an appropriate perfect form.
- Genuine future possibility: a suitable future or modal expression.

A tense change should reflect a real change in time relationship.

### 9.10 Part 3: identify the thinking operation

Part 3 can require you to:

- Explain why something happens.
- Compare groups or periods.
- Evaluate benefits and drawbacks.
- Consider responsibility.
- Suggest a response.
- Predict a possible development.
- Discuss a condition or exception.

Answer that operation directly.

If asked to compare, identify a meaningful similarity or difference. If asked why, explain a cause or reason. Do not give a general opinion and leave the requested operation unanswered.

### 9.11 Develop a Part 3 answer

A useful sequence is:

1. Give a direct view.
2. Explain the reason or mechanism.
3. Add a concrete example.
4. Add a qualification, consequence, or alternative perspective if useful.

You do not need all four moves every time.

Example of a useful qualification:

> Remote work can help people with long commutes, but some practical jobs still require workers to be on site.

The contrast identifies a relevant condition. It does not insert a second side solely to satisfy a formula.

A personal case can illustrate a broader point. Connect it back to the general question.

Do not search for specialist expertise. Select a modest claim you can explain accurately.

### 9.12 Optional response frameworks

The source describes several overlapping frameworks. Their useful functions are:

| Framework | Working procedure | Suitable use |
|---|---|---|
| Answer, detail, example | Answer directly; add relevant information; illustrate when helpful | Familiar questions and short development |
| Point, explain, link | Give a view; elaborate with a reason or experience; add a useful consequence or contrast | Extended explanation |
| Two perspectives and judgment | Explain the relevant perspectives; give a context-specific position | A question that genuinely invites comparison or evaluation |
| Past–Present–Future | Move through relevant time frames | A question with a meaningful timeline |

These are planning supports. They are not recitation scripts, mandatory opener sequences, or a score guarantee.

### 9.13 Handle an unfamiliar or inapplicable question

First identify the real difficulty:

- Did I fail to hear the question?
- Do I not understand its wording?
- Do I understand it but lack a ready idea?
- Does it assume an experience I have not had?

If you did not hear or understand it, ask politely for repetition or clarification. For example: “Could you repeat the question, please?” [British Council Speaking preparation](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/speaking)

If you lack experience, say so briefly. Then give a relevant related experience, a cautious view, or a clearly signalled possibility where appropriate.

For example, a person who does not use a particular service can explain that limitation and discuss why they might or might not use it.

Do not assume the examiner will change the topic or define every unfamiliar word. Attempt the question you receive.

Do not invent a personal history to maintain a script. A hypothetical should be recognizable as hypothetical.

### 9.14 Reduce idea-search friction

A pause can have different causes:

- Planning content.
- Searching for a word.
- Choosing grammar.
- Repairing misunderstanding.
- Managing anxiety.

Diagnose the cause before selecting a remedy.

If you have too many ideas, choose one relevant idea. If a word is unavailable, describe the meaning with words you know. If the question is unclear, clarify it.

A brief planning pause is normal. Frequent or long interruptions can still weaken continuity. Do not treat all pauses as harmless or count every “um” as an isolated scoring event.

Fluency is not maximum speed. Speaking quickly while losing endings and structure can make the message harder to follow.

### 9.15 Use natural vocabulary and idiomatic language

Choose language that fits spoken communication.

Useful everyday phrases include:

- “I usually…”
- “I tend to…”
- “What I like about it is…”
- “It depends on…”
- “For example…”
- “I see what you mean…”

Learn their functions and grammar. Do not insert them mechanically.

Idiomatic language includes natural conventional combinations, not only colourful sayings. A phrasal verb or ordinary collocation can be idiomatic.

There is no required idiom count. A memorised idiom used awkwardly does not improve an answer.

Topic-specific language can be useful without being rare: “attention span,” “a detached house,” “public transport,” or “a flexible schedule.”

Do not replace a controlled word merely to make the answer look advanced.

### 9.16 Use nine sentence functions flexibly

| Function | Example pattern | Main check |
|---|---|---|
| State a role | “I'm a nurse.” / “I study engineering.” | Use the correct article and role form |
| State origin | “I'm from Osaka.” | Answer the place question directly |
| State habit or preference | “I normally walk.” / “I prefer tea.” | Match the question and present meaning |
| Describe a completed event | “I visited my aunt last month.” | Use a form suited to a completed event |
| Express duration | “I've lived here for three years.” | Distinguish continuing duration from a finished stay |
| Describe a feeling | “I felt relieved.” | Choose an accurate adjective and explain why if useful |
| State a position | “I agree.” / “Not necessarily.” | Choose the actual degree of agreement |
| Give a reason | “Because it saves time…” | Complete a clear reason relationship |
| Support or contrast | “For example…” / “On the other hand…” | Use the expression only for its real function |

“I went to London for three years” describes a completed stay in a suitable context. It does not by itself describe residence that continues now.

These functions are useful because they communicate common meanings. They are not a verified ranking of high-band sentence frequencies.

### 9.17 Develop pronunciation through listener impact

Work on these features:

- Individual sounds that change or obscure words.
- Word-final consonants.
- Plural and tense endings.
- Syllable stress.
- Sentence stress.
- Meaningful grouping and rhythm.
- Intonation.
- Comfortable audibility.

Practise from an actual recording. A transcript cannot show how you produced a sound.

Do not infer pronunciation needs from nationality. Identify the learner's own pattern.

If final sounds disappear at speed, practise a few relevant words in phrases. Then return to natural connected speech and a fresh answer.

If word stress causes misunderstanding, mark and practise the stressed syllable in useful words.

### 9.18 Use stress and intonation for meaning

Stress can identify the important contrast:

> I wanted the **blue** folder.

The stress distinguishes the folder from another colour.

Intonation can signal completion, continuation, uncertainty, or attitude. Practise it in meaningful phrases, not as an exaggerated musical pattern.

Connected speech is normal. Learn to recognize and produce useful connections while keeping words intelligible.

Do not adopt a British or American identity as a requirement. A clear version of your own accent is appropriate.

### 9.19 Choose a comfortable pace and volume

Speak loudly enough to be heard without strain. Do not shout.

Adjust pace when it helps articulation and thought. There is no universal instruction that everyone should speak more slowly.

A learner who rushes and drops word endings may benefit from a more measured pace. A learner who stops between every word may need phrase-level fluency.

A phone's speech recognition can reveal possible unclear words. It can also misrecognize clear speech. Use it as a screening tool and check the recording with a listener.

A decibel display is not an IELTS score measure.

### 9.20 Self-correct selectively

If an error changes the meaning or you can repair it briefly, correct it and continue.

Do not restart every sentence to chase perfection. Constant monitoring can disrupt continuity.

Distinguish an occasional slip from a repeated gap. Repeated article omissions need focused work even when the overall message remains understandable.

A small error does not determine the whole Speaking result. Review a range of answers across the criteria.

### 9.21 Review recordings in separate passes

1. **Task pass:** Did I answer the question directly?
2. **Content pass:** Are the ideas relevant and connected?
3. **Language pass:** Which vocabulary or grammar pattern needs repair?
4. **Audio pass:** Which sound, stress, rhythm, or pace issue affects understanding?

Choose one or two targets. Correct them in a revised attempt. Then use a fresh question to check whether the improvement transfers.

A short transcript can help identify grammar and repetition. Verify apparent errors against the audio because automatic captions can be wrong.

Do not review only the first answer or one unusually strong topic.

### 9.22 Use models and shadowing carefully

Before hearing a model answer, try the question yourself. This keeps your own thinking active.

Then compare:

- Directness.
- Relevant detail.
- Organization.
- Useful phrases.
- Pronunciation features.

Shadowing means speaking along with or just after a model. It can help practise rhythm, stress, and connected phrasing.

Use a manageable passage. Understand it first. Do not copy an entire candidate's biography or accent as your own identity.

Return to an original answer. Successful imitation alone does not demonstrate spontaneous speaking.

### 9.23 Use feedback and AI appropriately

Useful feedback points to evidence in the response and explains a repair.

AI can provide prompts, conversational turns, example wording, and possible areas to inspect. Its band estimates can be inconsistent or uncalibrated.

Check whether the feedback matches the correct criterion. A text-only analysis cannot assess your actual pronunciation.

Use informed, calibrated human review when an important score or readiness judgment depends on it. A paid provider or former examiner is not a compulsory part of preparation.

### 9.24 Speaking pitfalls and score myths

- Memorising a personal introduction or complete answer.
- Giving an unrelated biography after a short question.
- Listing many ideas when one developed answer would fit.
- Turning every Part 1 answer into a long speech.
- Reading Part 2 notes as a script.
- Skipping cue-card points as the default method.
- Forcing a favourite story into the wrong topic.
- Giving only a position in Part 3 without explanation.
- Adding both sides when they do not help answer the question.
- Waiting for expert knowledge or a perfect idea.
- Searching for a rare word while a clear alternative is available.
- Treating all pauses as failure or all pauses as harmless.
- Speaking faster to appear fluent.
- Treating a native-like accent as a requirement.
- Guessing a score from examiner friendliness, interruptions, or question difficulty.
- Assuming fluency contributes 75% of the score.
- Assuming “100% understood” is a precise Band 9 formula.
- Assuming any memorised phrase automatically produces Band 0.
- Accepting a video title's band label as independent assessment.

### 10.3 Further distinctions that prevent meaning errors

**Population labels:**

- A resident lives in a place.
- A citizen has the relevant citizenship status.
- A local has a connection with a local area.
- A native can concern origin and can carry context-sensitive implications.
- “Men” does not represent all people when women and children are included.

**Age labels:**

- A teenager is in the teen years.
- Adolescence describes a developmental period between childhood and adulthood.
- A minor is a legal category whose boundary depends on context.
- An infant and a toddler describe different early stages.
- “Youth” and “young people” can cover a broader range than children.

Do not infer universal legal age limits from everyday labels.

**Business labels:**

- A manufacturer makes goods.
- An agency performs a specified service or function.
- An organization need not be a commercial company.
- A firm is commonly a business, often in professional contexts.

**Family labels:**

- A guardian can care for a child without being a parent.
- Ancestors are earlier generations.
- “Sire” is not a neutral ordinary substitute for parent in an IELTS essay.

**Intensity and precision:**

- Substantial means considerable; immense means extremely large.
- Paramount means of greatest importance, not merely noticeable.
- Intricate means complex in its arrangement; detailed means containing many particulars.
- Simple means not complicated; easy means requiring little difficulty or effort.
- Broke is an informal description of having no money at a time; it does not cover every meaning of poor.
- Bizarre, elated, excruciating, and spotless are stronger or more specific than strange, happy, painful, and clean.

Do not use a stronger word when the intended degree is weaker.

**Time and word forms:**

- “Presently” has usage differences across contexts and varieties. Check the intended meaning.
- “Improve” is a verb. “Better” is often an adjective or adverb, but can also be a verb in suitable contexts.
- “Data” has both plural and mass-noun usage in contemporary English. Follow a consistent suitable convention. Do not write the caption error “datam”; the singular technical form is “datum.”

### 10.7 Additional fixed expressions and register

- **Straight from the horse's mouth:** directly from an informed original source.
- **The apple of my eye:** a person cherished especially dearly.
- **Over the moon:** very happy. Keep “the.”
- **On the other hand:** introduces a relevant contrasting perspective. Keep “the.”
- **In a nutshell:** a brief summary. Keep “a.”
- **I should get going:** a natural conversational way to say it is time to leave.
- **Are you free?:** asks about availability in context.
- **I'm done:** says an activity is finished in context.

| Missing article before a singular role | “I'm a teacher” |
| Missing article in a score phrase | “A Band 9 score” |
| Unclear family-member construction | “There are five people in my family” |
| Unnatural verb for completing the exam | “I took the IELTS test last year” is a standard option; “sat” also occurs in suitable usage |
| Finished event used for continuing residence | “I have lived here for three years” when the residence continues |
| Adjective used where an adverb is needed | “Acted rashly”; “rose gradually” |
| Noun phrase with a trend adjective | “A gradual rise,” not “a gradually rise” |
| Confused result preposition | “Rose to 30” gives the endpoint; “rose by 10” gives the change |
| Incorrect plural | “Phenomena” is the plural of “phenomenon” |
| Incorrect plural in a work schedule | “Two days off,” not “two day offs” |
| Missing plural or tense ending in speech | Check the actual recording; practise the ending in phrases |
| “Exceed of” | “Exceed the limit” |
| Missing collocation preposition | “Detrimental to”; “prone to”; “irrespective of”; “awareness of” |
| Independent sentences joined with a comma alone | Use a full stop, suitable conjunction, or another correct construction |
| Unclear pronoun | Repeat or specify the noun so the reference is unambiguous |
| Confused apostrophe | Distinguish a possessive form from a plural; check the actual intended owner |

Learn the reason for a correction. One memorised corrected sentence does not repair every use of the pattern.

### 10.9 A sentence-level editing procedure

1. State the intended meaning plainly.
2. Identify the subject and main verb.
3. Check time and number.
4. Check articles and noun forms.
5. Check word combinations and prepositions.
6. Check how clauses connect.
7. Remove unnecessary wording.
8. Confirm that no meaning was lost.

For a dense sentence, first simplify the proposition. Then restore any necessary comparison, condition, or qualification.

Do not remove useful precision simply to make the sentence shorter.

## 11. Diagnosis, repair, and a sustainable study plan

### 11.1 Set a measurable target

Record the receiving organization's requirements before setting a study target.

Include the overall band and each skill minimum. Identify which skill currently creates the largest gap.

Use evidence from all four skills. Strong Reading and Listening do not prove that Writing and Speaking are ready.

A useful target describes behaviour as well as a score:

- Recognize corrected details in Listening.
- Distinguish contradiction from missing information in Reading.
- Develop each essay point with a clear mechanism.
- Answer Part 3 comparison questions directly.

These behaviours give you something concrete to improve.

### 11.2 Distinguish learning evidence from performance evidence

**Learning evidence** shows what you can do with support.

Examples include pausing audio, checking a dictionary, following a worked method, or receiving paragraph feedback.

**Performance evidence** shows what you can do independently under relevant conditions.

Examples include unfamiliar material, the actual time limit, no answer preview, and the correct delivery method.

Both are useful. Record the assistance so you do not confuse them.

A remembered answer can demonstrate retention. It cannot establish independent comprehension of fresh material.

### 11.3 Use one diagnosis-and-repair cycle

1. Attempt a relevant task or produce a response.
2. Preserve the original work.
3. Identify the exact failure or uncertainty.
4. Locate the evidence for a better decision.
5. Classify the failed operation.
6. Repair that operation with focused work.
7. Try a fresh task that requires it.
8. Check whether the improvement transfers.

Do not change every part of your preparation after one low result. Use the smallest repair supported by the evidence.

More than one cause can exist. A learner can misunderstand a phrase and also exceed the answer word limit.

### 11.4 Use practical error categories

| Category | What failed | Example repair |
|---|---|---|
| Instructions | The response did not follow the task conditions | Decode limits and required answer form before answering |
| Language recognition | A word, sound, or sentence relationship was not understood | Work with short contextual examples and verify meaning |
| Location or tracking | The relevant passage, route, stage, or speaker was lost | Practise anchors, maps, sequence, or attribution |
| Decision rule | The evidence was understood but classified incorrectly | Practise the task-specific judgment with explanations |
| Content development | A response lacked a reason, mechanism, or useful detail | Plan and explain one point before adding another |
| Organization | Ideas existed but their order or relationships were unclear | Assign each paragraph or response segment one job |
| Language production | Meaning was intended but word choice or grammar failed | Correct the recurring pattern and use it in a fresh response |
| Answer entry | Spelling, number, label, or format was wrong | Add a targeted entry check |
| Attention or pacing | Focus or available time failed at a particular point | Test a recovery or timing change after checking the other causes |

An error label is a working diagnosis. It should point to evidence in the actual work.

### 11.5 Keep an error log that leads to action

For each important recurring error, record:

- The original wording or answer.
- The correct evidence or intended meaning.
- The reason for the error.
- The corrected form or decision.
- One focused repair.
- The result on a later fresh attempt.

Avoid entries such as “careless” without explanation. Ask what action would prevent the same failure.

For example:

> I heard the initial appointment time but missed the correction after “actually.” I need to track replacement information, not merely recognize the first time expression.

This is more useful than “Listening concentration bad.”

### 11.6 Diagnose from patterns without turning them into rules

**Listening: errors mainly in discussion questions.**

Possible causes include speaker confusion, opinion attribution, distractors, or inability to process options while listening. Inspect the recording and your selections. Do not assume more general audio exposure alone will solve the task issue.

**Reading: one family repeatedly wrong.**

Check whether the method is understood. For headings, inspect main idea versus example. For True / False / Not Given, inspect support versus contradiction versus absence.

**Reading: accurate untimed, incomplete timed.**

Gradual pacing work may be appropriate. Also inspect unproductive searching and time spent changing answers.

**Writing: strong vocabulary, weak task coverage.**

Repair prompt analysis and the plan before collecting more words.

**Writing: relevant points, weak development.**

Explain why and how each point works. Check whether the example supports the same idea.

**Speaking: good familiar-topic answers, long pauses on new topics.**

Try varied low-pressure topics and manageable idea selection. Check whether the real issue is vocabulary retrieval, question interpretation, or content planning.

**Speaking: fluent speech with unclear endings.**

Use the recording to find the actual sound pattern. Practise short phrases, then a fresh natural answer.

A nationality, profession, personality label, or self-rating is not sufficient diagnosis.

### 11.7 Use focused practice and complete performance together

Focused work isolates one operation. Complete work shows whether operations can run together.

Examples of focused work include:

- Hearing a corrected date.
- Following a short map route.
- Explaining a Not Given decision.
- Forming a paragraph heading.
- Writing an accurate chart overview.
- Developing a letter bullet fully.
- Explaining one essay mechanism.
- Repairing an article pattern in speech.

After improvement, return to unfamiliar mixed work under suitable conditions.

A learner can explain a method from memory as a mastery check. Then demonstrate it on new material. A fluent explanation of a method is not the same as successful use.

### 11.8 Allocate effort from the current weakness

Maintain stronger skills while giving more attention to demonstrated gaps.

Do not use a permanent equal-time split if one area needs much more repair. Do not abandon a stronger skill long enough to lose practical control.

Start Writing and Speaking early. They require actual production and feedback. Watching lessons cannot reveal all your own response problems.

A useful study period can include:

- A focused target.
- An attempt.
- Review.
- Correction.
- A brief later retrieval or transfer check.

The exact length and frequency depend on your time, starting level, and deadline. The source's daily essay counts and long study-hour stories are examples, not quotas.

### 11.9 Connect input with output

Read and listen to English that you can sustain with interest.

Broad useful themes include education, work, health, transport, technology, culture, public services, and the environment. These are language-development areas, not predictions of the next test.

A connected learning sequence is:

1. Read or listen for the main meaning.
2. Notice one useful word combination or idea relationship.
3. Verify it.
4. Summarize the meaning in your own words.
5. Discuss or write about a related issue.
6. Check the accuracy of the language used.

This links receptive knowledge with production. Passive exposure alone does not show that you can retrieve a form when needed.

### 11.10 Build retrieval and useful vocabulary review

Use several review directions:

- Word to meaning.
- Meaning to word.
- Word to an original phrase.
- Phrase to an original spoken or written response.

Group items by a useful relationship, such as topic, word class, sound pattern, or contrasting meaning.

A short story using selected words can test whether they work together naturally. Do not force every item into the story if it damages the meaning.

Bilingual notes and flashcards can support learning. Pair them with context, retrieval, and accurate use.

There is no evidence here for one fixed daily word limit or one universally best notebook format.

### 11.11 Choose dependable learning material

Official [IELTS sample questions](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/sample-test-questions) are a useful format reference. Established IELTS partners also publish preparation resources.

When checking another resource, ask:

- Does it use the actual task conditions?
- Are instructions clear and realistic?
- Is the answer key defensible from the evidence?
- Is a displayed band supported by criterion-based reasoning?
- Does the explanation distinguish a requirement from a preference?
- Is a claimed rule consistent with current official guidance?

Do not assume that all unofficial material is false. Do not accept an unsupported percentage claiming that almost every online test is fake.

Use one coherent method long enough to evaluate it. Keep the method open to evidence. Blindly following one mentor is not a learning requirement.

### 11.12 Manage focus and stress practically

Identify the trigger before choosing a response.

Possible triggers include:

- An unclear task.
- A visible countdown.
- Slow idea generation.
- A fear of making an error.
- Unfamiliar equipment.
- A previous disappointing result.

Possible low-pressure adjustments include:

- Learn the operation without a timer first.
- Add time pressure gradually.
- Use a short planning routine.
- Practise the actual interface.
- Reduce notifications during a protected study period.
- Use a familiar breathing or settling routine.
- Choose a manageable idea and continue.

Check whether the adjustment helps the actual work. Do not diagnose every difficulty as a mindset problem.

Deleting an app, meditating, following an NLP routine, or adopting an English-only day is not compulsory. A personal testimonial does not establish that a routine causes a band increase.

### 11.13 Assess readiness realistically

Use multiple pieces of evidence:

- Fresh Listening and Reading performance.
- Complete Writing responses under the shared time constraint.
- Speaking across familiar and unfamiliar topics.
- Known errors and their recurrence.
- Feedback quality.
- Delivery familiarity.
- The receiving organization's skill minimums.

The source sometimes suggests three consecutive results half a band above target. Treat this as an optional margin heuristic. It is not an official threshold or a guarantee.

Readiness is uncertain. Consider deadlines, cost, available preparation time, and the consequences of a result below target.

You do not need a commercial coach's permission to book the test.

### 11.14 Final preparation and test-day notes

Check current instructions from your actual provider.

Confirm:

- Date, venue, reporting time, and Speaking arrangement.
- Required identification.
- Route and a reasonable transport backup.
- Permitted belongings and equipment.
- Delivery mode and response method.
- Any approved access arrangements.

Use the same required identification as directed in your booking. A phone photograph of an identity document is not a substitute for the required original document. Follow the provider's [test-day guidance](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/on-test-day).

Prepare in a familiar way:

- Leave time for rest.
- Avoid an all-night revision session.
- Eat and drink normally for your needs.
- Avoid an unfamiliar amount of caffeine.
- Wear comfortable clothing.
- Set an alarm and allow travel time.
- Use light English input or conversation if it helps you settle.

Do not introduce a demanding new technique at the last minute.

At the venue, report sound or equipment problems promptly. Follow staff instructions. Do not assume every centre uses identical equipment or remedies.

During the work, return attention to the next useful action. A missed Listening item, one uncertain Reading answer, or a weak Speaking response does not require abandoning the rest.

## 12. Quick reference: retain the method, reject the false rule

### 12.1 Useful principles that apply across the course

- Understand the exact task before choosing a technique.
- Use evidence before making a decision.
- Develop relevant ideas with clear support.
- Choose language for meaning, grammar, and context.
- Build range while keeping control.
- Practise the failed operation directly.
- Check improvement on fresh material.
- Use current official instructions for format and delivery.

### 12.2 Common claims and their limits

| Claim | Accurate working note |
|---|---|
| Clear communication is all that IELTS measures | It is a useful aim. The test still has four skills, task conditions, and published criteria. |
| One general trick solves every question | Each family requires a particular operation and evidence check. |
| The final Listening parts each correspond to a fixed band | Parts have different contexts and demands. They are not separate published band filters. |
| A precise proportion of Reading time should be skimming | Choose the operation and time from the task. The source does not establish universal percentages. |
| Rare or C1/C2 words create a high band | Accurate, appropriate, flexible use matters. There is no word-level quota. |
| Never repeat a word | Repeat a precise word when the alternatives change the meaning. |
| Add a linker to every sentence | Use cohesion to express genuine relationships. |
| Never use idioms | Register, meaning, and accuracy determine suitability. There is no idiom quota or universal ban. |
| Use only short simple sentences | Clear sentences help, but grammatical range also matters. |
| Passive voice is always wrong | Use it when it serves the information focus accurately. |
| Every essay needs the same four paragraphs | Paragraph jobs and task demands determine the organization. |
| Every Task 1 overview must contain exactly three features and no numbers | Select accurate main features. Counts and the usual number-free format are scaffolds. |
| Task 2 must always come first | Order is a tested personal strategy. Complete both tasks. |
| 200 words is a Task 1 maximum | The official instruction is a minimum of 150 words. |
| 300 words is a Task 2 maximum | The official instruction is a minimum of 250 words. Control relevance, time, and development. |
| A hook or familiar phrase automatically earns zero | The whole response is assessed. Do not turn memorisation warnings into invented automatic penalties. |
| The weakest Writing criterion becomes the complete Writing band | Criteria contribute to task scores; Task 2 has greater task weight. |
| A fixed error-free percentage guarantees a band | The source's numerical thresholds are not official scoring formulas. |
| Speaking fluency is worth 75% | The four Speaking criteria have equal weight. |
| A British or American accent is required | Work on intelligibility and pronunciation features. |
| Smiling, eye contact, or clothing directly earns language marks | They are not separate criteria. Be comfortable and respond to the task. |
| A difficult Speaking question proves a high band | Question difficulty and examiner behaviour are not a reliable score signal. |
| The examiner will switch a topic if you say it is unfamiliar | No switch is guaranteed. Attempt a relevant response. |
| Three good practice results guarantee the official result | They provide limited evidence, not certainty. |
| Only a paid former examiner can provide feedback | Feedback quality and calibration matter. A specific credential or service is not compulsory. |
| An AI score is an official assessment | Treat it as an uncalibrated suggestion unless independently validated for the use. |
| One learner's rapid score increase proves the method | Starting ability, prior exposure, conditions, and unreported factors can differ. |
| Predicted monthly topics remove the need for broad preparation | Prepare transferable skills and the full task range. |
| Nothing about IELTS can change | Skills, task requirements, delivery, and local administration are distinct. Check current official updates. |

### 12.3 Final skill checklists

**Listening:** decode instructions; preview meaning and form; follow the recording; track correction and attribution; recover after a miss; check entry accuracy.

**Reading:** identify the question operation; locate evidence; read the full claim; decide from the passage; respect limits; protect answer-entry time.

**Academic Task 1:** decode the visual; select main features; write an accurate overview; group details; compare and quantify correctly; remove unsupported inference.

**General Training Task 1:** identify reader and purpose; choose register; cover all bullets and subcommands; make actions clear; use suitable connected letter form.

**Task 2:** analyse topic, focus, and instruction; choose a clear response; plan manageable points; explain mechanisms; support them; conclude consistently; review.

**Speaking:** answer directly; develop relevant meaning; use the card and notes flexibly; explain broader views; paraphrase when needed; speak intelligibly; review actual recordings.
