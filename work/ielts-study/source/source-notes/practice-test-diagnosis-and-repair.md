## Distilled practice loop

1. **Learn a new operation slowly.** When learning a question-type method, pause, check each step, and take the time needed to understand what the learner is doing. Then reduce support and build speed gradually. Slow practice measures learning of the method; it is not a readiness score. ([6:00–7:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hAEajljN24&t=360s))
2. **Use an unseen, timed set to diagnose.** For a baseline, use practice materials that reflect IELTS tasks, do not preview or reuse the answers, follow the applicable timing and delivery mode, and mark against the answer key honestly. Repeatedly doing familiar items can test memory more than skill. ([7:30–10:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hAEajljN24&t=450s))
3. **Review every missed answer.** Record why it went wrong, not only the score. The video’s useful error categories are spelling or answer format; missed instructions; difficulty locating or understanding evidence; listening focus or speaker-tracking; vocabulary gaps; and a question-family strategy that has not been learned or applied. Check the work itself before assigning a cause.
4. **Repair the smallest demonstrated weakness.** Select a specific operation, study or model it, practise it slowly on fresh examples, and obtain feedback where possible. Do not respond to every wrong answer by doing another full test. ([10:45–16:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hAEajljN24&t=645s))
5. **Retest with new material.** Use a new timed set to see whether the target weakness has improved and to identify the next one. Track multiple attempts as evidence; a practice result is an estimate, not a guaranteed test-day outcome.

## Use dependable material without repeating unsupported claims

Start with current [official IELTS sample test questions](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/sample-test-questions). Cambridge English also lists IELTS preparation coursebooks, practice tests, and supplementary materials ([Cambridge English preparation](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/ielts/preparation/)). These are practical starting points. The video’s claim that these are the only five reliable places, its estimate that 90–95% of other tests are fake, and its claims about why students fail are not supported with a published comparison; do not repeat those figures as facts.

The proposed readiness rule—performing half a band above the target on three consecutive practice tests—is an instructor heuristic, not a published IELTS threshold. Nor do the source’s claims of guaranteed improvement, most students booking too early, or course outcomes establish what any learner will score. Use a learner's target, fresh timed performance, error pattern, and feedback together. IELTS explains that its test components are scored using official assessment criteria and publishes sample tasks; the exact result still cannot be guaranteed from a small set of practice scores. ([IELTS scoring](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail))
