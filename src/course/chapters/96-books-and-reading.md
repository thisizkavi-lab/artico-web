# CH96 — Books and Reading

**You can:** describe your reaction to a book, ask for a suitable recommendation, and handle a bookstore or subscription question.  

## Study

### Give a reaction with enough meaning

“I'm reading a mystery” names the kind of book. “It's about two sisters looking for a missing letter” gives its basic subject. You can add what you think without revealing the ending.

> **Drew:** How's that novel you started?  
> **Casey:** I'm really enjoying it. The chapters are short, but a lot happens.  
> **Drew:** Is it scary?  
> **Casey:** A little tense, more than scary. I want to know what happens next.  
> **Drew:** Would you recommend it?  
> **Casey:** Yes, if you like mysteries. Don't read the description on the back too closely—it gives away part of the story.  

A **novel** is a long work of fiction. A **mystery** centers on something to be explained, often a crime. A **biography** tells a person's life; an **autobiography** is written by that person. **Fiction** presents invented stories; **nonfiction** presents factual subjects, though you can still question a writer's claims.

Useful reactions include:

| Expression | What you mean |
|---|---|
| I couldn't put it down. | I wanted to keep reading. |
| It's a page-turner. | It makes me eager to read what comes next. |
| It kept me on the edge of my seat. | I felt strong suspense or excitement. |
| The ending was a letdown. | The ending disappointed me. |
| I found it hard going. | Reading it took effort or felt difficult. |

You can also say exactly what you mean: “I liked the characters, but the ending disappointed me.” You do not need an idiom to give a good reaction. “Hard going” might refer to difficult language, slow progress, or heavy subject matter; explain which you mean.

Someone else can love a book you dislike. “I can see why you liked it. I thought the middle was too slow” expresses both recognition and disagreement. You can be more direct: “It didn't work for me at all.” Add a reason if you want to keep the conversation going.

### Choose something in a bookstore

> **Customer:** I'm looking for a book for a long flight. Something short and funny, preferably fiction.  
> **Bookseller:** How about this collection of short stories? Each one takes about ten minutes to read.  
> **Customer:** That sounds good. Is it available in paperback?  
> **Bookseller:** We have the paperback here for twelve dollars.  
> **Customer:** I'll take it.  

These price and reading-time estimates belong to this fictional scene. **Paperback** has a flexible paper cover; **hardcover** has a stiff cover. “I'm just browsing, thanks” means you are looking without needing help yet. If a book is **out of stock**, the shop does not currently have it available. Ask, “Can you order it?” and “When would it arrive?” without assuming the answer.

### Other reading habits

“I skim the headlines first” means you read quickly for the main points. “I flip through the magazine” means you look through its pages without necessarily reading everything. A **subscription** gives continuing access or delivery under stated terms. **Renew** continues it for a new period: “How much would it cost to renew?”

## Practice

### 1. Recommend from the facts

These books are invented for this exercise:

- **The Last Ferry:** a 180-page mystery; tense, with short chapters; paperback, $14.
- **Small Accidents:** funny short stories; paperback, $12.
- **A Life in Gardens:** a long biography about a landscape designer; hardcover, $28.

Partner wants short, funny fiction for a trip. Recommend *Small Accidents* and explain the fit. Partner then says, “Actually, I'd rather have a mystery.” Change your recommendation. Possible response: “Then try *The Last Ferry*. It's a short mystery with lots of suspense.”

### 2. Give a different reaction

Fictional reading facts: you liked the characters and found the first half exciting, but the ending felt rushed. Tell a friend without giving away the ending. Partner: “Really? I loved the ending.” Continue with your own view rather than automatically agreeing.

Possibility: “I liked the characters, but the ending was a letdown for me. I wanted more explanation.” Another sensible response is “I can see that. I still thought it felt rushed.”

### 3. Ask before deciding

Role facts: the book you want is out of stock. Staff: “We can order it. It would arrive next Wednesday.” You need it by Monday. Explain that the arrival is too late and ask about another format or a different book. Staff: “The e-book is available today.” You prefer a physical book; decline and ask for a different recommendation.

Then ask about a magazine renewal when you do not know the price. “What would it cost to renew for a year?” is enough; do not invent a price.

**Solo route:** read the partner cues aloud, answer, and switch to the alternative preference. Cover the table and explain two reactions in plain words. Check whether your recommendation uses the supplied facts and whether your reaction tells the listener what you actually liked or disliked.
