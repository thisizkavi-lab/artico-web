# Irodori × Artico editorial method

Irodori gives Artico a useful teaching discipline: begin with what a learner needs to do in life, place language inside a situation, support understanding in the learner’s strongest language, and move toward an achievable action. Artico adds a deeper theory layer and a stronger speaking/practice loop.

## The lesson movement

### 1. Can-do before grammar

Start with an observable action, not a grammar label. “I can ask someone to repeat a detail” is more useful than “Today we study the past tense.”

### 2. Situation before explanation

Give the learner a reason for the language. Who is speaking? What just happened? What does the learner want to make happen?

### 3. Listen and notice

Let the learner hear a natural version before asking for performance. The first listen is for the scene and the relationship; the second can focus on a phrase or sound.

### 4. Theory in the learner’s language

Use Japanese to explain social meaning, contrast, register, and likely friction. Keep the English examples visible and reusable.

### 5. Controlled practice

Change one variable at a time. Use the shadowing audio, then remove support gradually. Practice is where the learner’s mouth and timing catch up with understanding.

### 6. Personal mission

End with a small action connected to the learner’s life in Japan. The mission should be possible without a perfect conversation partner.

### 7. Return

Invite the learner back after a gap. Repetition over time is part of the lesson, not a punishment for forgetting.

## Zoom out → zoom in → zoom out

This is the reading rhythm for the orientation and every chapter.

- **Zoom out:** why this matters in a real person’s life; what the learner will be able to do.
- **Zoom in:** one sound, phrase, pattern, distinction, or social choice explained properly.
- **Zoom out:** a dialogue, a choice, a practice task, and a real-world mission.

Never zoom in so far that the learner forgets the person and situation. Never zoom out so far that the learner receives motivation without usable language.

## Relevance and emphasis

Use relevance to answer “Why should I care now?” Connect examples to life in Japan: a workplace, a train station, a café, an online call, a hobby group, a hotel, or a conversation with someone visiting Japan.

Use emphasis sparingly. A callout should mark something worth remembering, not decorate every paragraph:

- **Why this matters** — the social consequence.
- **Watch this** — a likely Japanese-learner trap.
- **Say it this way** — the usable frame.
- **Try it now** — a small action.
- **Good to know** — a memorable cultural or language detail.

## Artico’s modification

Irodori’s practical guidance is the base. Artico’s modification is to make the explanation feel like a book: enough context to care, enough theory to understand, enough repetition to embody, and enough space to return later. The lesson should be simple without being thin.

## Editorial guardrails

- Keep Japanese support as a bridge, not a crutch.
- Do not turn every lesson into a test or a game.
- Do not promise a fixed number of days to fluency.
- Do not use generic “native speaker” claims when a specific register or region matters.
- Do not add audio, images, or video merely because the medium exists.
- Do not write a phrase without explaining when it is socially appropriate.
