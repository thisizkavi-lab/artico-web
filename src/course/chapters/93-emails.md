# CH93 — Emails

**You can:** check what happened to a message, write a clear email with a specific request, and respond to it.  

## Study

### What happened to the email?

The **inbox** holds received messages. A **draft** is a message you have saved without sending. **Spam** or **junk** is the folder for messages the service treats as unwanted. **Trash** holds deleted messages; whether you can recover one depends on the service and its settings.

An **outbox** holds messages waiting to be sent. A **contact** is a saved person or address you can message. An **attachment** is a file sent with a message. **Forward** sends a received message to someone else. **Reply** answers the sender; **reply all** also answers the other recipients included in that reply. Check the recipients before using it. A **signature** is saved text, such as your name and contact details, added at the end. These words help you describe a problem even when different services have different buttons.

> **Nora:** Did you get the room change I emailed you?  
> **Eli:** I haven't seen it. What was the subject line?  
> **Nora:** “Tuesday workshop: new room.” Could you check your junk folder?  
> **Eli:** There it is. Room 204 at two, right?  
> **Nora:** Right. I also sent the updated agenda.  
> **Eli:** I got the message, but I don't see an attachment.  
> **Nora:** You're right—I forgot to attach it. I'll send it now.  

“Did you get it?” asks about receiving it, not necessarily reading it. “I got it, but I haven't read it yet” makes that distinction clear. “I haven't gotten back to you” means you have not responded. “Sorry I haven't gotten back to you” is useful when you want to acknowledge that delay; you can also simply answer the pending question.

Other useful reports are “It's still in my drafts,” “I sent it to the wrong address,” and “Could you forward the details to me?”

### Write enough for the reader to act

A **subject line** names the message's purpose. In the body, give the relevant facts and say what response you need. The greeting and ending depend on the relationship; an ordinary professional email can start with “Hi Eli” or “Hello Eli.” A longer or more formal greeting is not automatically clearer.

Here is an original email in a fictional workshop:

> **Subject:** Tuesday workshop: new room  
>
> Hi Eli,
>
> Tuesday's workshop will meet in Room 204 instead of Room 110. The start time is still 2 p.m.
>
> I've attached the updated agenda. Could you confirm that you received it?
>
> Thanks,
> Nora

In this scene, Nora really has attached the agenda. If she has not, she should change that sentence or attach the file before sending.

A useful reply is:

> Hi Nora,
>
> Thanks—I received the agenda. I'll be in Room 204 at 2 p.m. on Tuesday.
>
> Eli

Between friends, “Got it—204 at two. Thanks!” can do the same job. Use the details needed to prevent a misunderstanding, rather than adding formality just to fill space.

### An unexpected attachment

You can say, “I wasn't expecting this file. Did you send it?” An unfamiliar attachment or urgent request does not become safe just because the message displays a familiar name. Verify an unexpected request through contact details you already know are genuine. This is a communication task; it is not an exercise in opening suspicious files.

## Practice

### 1. Check delivery

Use these facts: you sent a schedule, the recipient received the message but not the attachment, and you realize you forgot the file.

Partner cues: “I got your email, but there's no attachment.” Then: “Thanks. Does the meeting still start at ten?” The schedule says **10 a.m.**

Possible exchange: “Sorry, I forgot to attach it. I'll send it now.” / “Yes, it still starts at ten.” Change the problem: the message is still in your drafts. Explain that instead of claiming it was sent.

### 2. Write and revise

Fictional facts: a book club moves from Thursday to Friday; the time remains 6 p.m.; the place remains the library meeting room. You need each member to tell you whether Friday works. No file is attached.

Improve this unclear draft: “Hi everyone, things changed. See you then. Please answer.”

One possibility:

> **Subject:** Book club moved to Friday  
>
> Hi everyone,
>
> This week's book club will meet on Friday instead of Thursday. We'll still meet at 6 p.m. in the library meeting room.
>
> Please let me know whether Friday works for you.
>
> Thanks,
> Sam

Now write a reply if Friday works, and a different reply if it does not. “Friday works for me” and “I can't make Friday. Could we meet next week?” are both reasonable when they match your intention.

### 3. Retrieve without the model

Cover the examples. A colleague asks you to forward an email, but it includes another person's private message. Ask what information they need before deciding what to share: “Do you just need the time and address? I can send you those details.”

**Solo route:** write your message, then write both possible recipient replies and answer each one. Read the delivery exchange in both roles. Check that the reader can identify the change, the correct details, and the requested response. Different wording is acceptable.
