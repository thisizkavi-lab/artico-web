## 1. Learn from model essays without copying them — 0:00–25:03

The opening section treats model essays as material for active study, not templates. The instructor warns against trusting unattributed online “Band 9” samples, reading passively, self-awarding a band, searching only for phrases to borrow, or copying an essay into the test. His numerical claim that more than 95% of online top-band essays are fake is not independently established and should not be repeated as a fact.

The useful model-essay routine is:

1. Hide the model and generate relevant ideas for the same prompt. Compare the ideas afterward for relevance and development; different ideas are not automatically worse.
2. Choose one model idea and write an independent explanation and example. Compare how each version develops the point.
3. Map the organization for that prompt family across several samples. Learn the logic, not a memorized paragraph pattern.
4. Identify cohesive devices and the relationship each one expresses. Notice when a linker is inaccurate, repeated, or unnecessary.
5. Find grammar errors, correct them, and identify the rule behind the correction.
6. Notice effective grammatical structures and why they fit the meaning. Add a structure to active practice rather than lifting the sentence.
7. The speaker’s numbering skips from item 6 to item 8; this note does not infer a missing strategy.
8. Investigate useful unfamiliar vocabulary in context. Record its meaning, collocations, synonyms or related forms, and an original example; revisit and use it.
9. Notice inaccurate vocabulary and avoid unnecessary repetition, while keeping the clearest word when no precise alternative exists.
10. Write a fresh essay before comparing it with a sample. Use the comparison to investigate a specific weakness, not to treat every difference as an error.

The video advertises 100 essays as authentic and marked. That provenance is the publisher’s claim. Use samples only when their source and task are credible, and evaluate them against the prompt and official criteria.

## 2. Task 2 course: answer first, then build the essay — 25:03–3:29:48

The course works mostly with two-part questions and says that some of its planning methods can transfer to other prompt families. It identifies opinion, discussion, causes/problem–solution, advantages/disadvantages, and two-part questions. Its example asks why robots are taking over tasks at home and work, and whether that change is positive or negative.

The instructor’s seven-step teaching sequence alternates instruction with feedback: introduction; review; body paragraphs; review; conclusion; complete essay and global feedback; then extension to other essay types. For the example prompt, he uses a four-paragraph scaffold: introduction, one body paragraph for each question, and conclusion. This is a teaching scaffold, not a required IELTS paragraph count.

### Plan before drafting

- Separate the broad subject from the exact scope and instruction words. Identify every question the task asks.
- Decide on a clear, supportable answer before composing the introduction.
- Choose a few straightforward, relevant ideas that can be explained. Avoid abstract brainstorming that produces ideas unrelated to the exact prompt.
- Make a short road map so each paragraph has a job. The video delays drafting until the task and ideas are understood.

### Introduction

Use an accurate paraphrase of the prompt and answer its required parts briefly. Select synonyms that fit the exact context; changing every word can distort meaning or create grammatical errors. The introduction should make the response visible to a reader. The instructor rejects generic hooks, memorized sayings, and attempts to show off in the opening.

### Body paragraphs

Give each paragraph a clear topic sentence that responds to one part of the task. Explain how or why the point answers the question, then support it with a relevant, plausible example. Ask “so what?” when the reasoning does not yet connect the claim to the task. Keep unrelated ideas out of the paragraph, and do not use a broad-topic example as if it automatically proves the specific claim.

### Conclusion and review

Summarize the position and reasons already established; do not introduce a new argument. Proofread for task coverage, logical links, grammar, spelling, and word choice. The video includes homework, teacher feedback, and a live writing demonstration, emphasizing revision against the question rather than memorizing a finished model. See the [six-hour Task 2 note](writing-task2-complete-6h.md) and [one-hour guided essay note](writing-task2-one-hour-course.md) for the overlapping material in greater detail.

## 3. Speaking: practise, record, diagnose, repair — 3:29:48–5:06:40

The long course repeats and extends the separate Speaking course. Keep the three parts distinct in practice: personal and familiar answers in Part 1, a sustained individual talk in Part 2, and broader discussion in Part 3. The official criteria remain fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation.

The “VIP” self-practice method is a useful test-like review routine:

1. Select about nine Part 1 questions, one Part 2 cue card, and three or four Part 3 questions.
2. Do not research the questions or write prepared answers. Recreate the sequence of a speaking test and record the response.
3. Listen back and review against the four criteria. Check whether each answer addresses the question and develops a clear point; then inspect pauses and fillers, intelligibility, repeated or misused words, and grammar patterns.
4. Transcribe difficult sections when useful, but verify transcription against the audio. Choose one recurring weakness, practise a targeted repair, and record again.
5. Ask a qualified listener for feedback when self-review cannot resolve the issue.

The course presents each part’s common problems, a response strategy, and a challenge. In Part 1, answer directly and add relevant detail without forcing a fixed number of sentences. In Part 2, plan one coherent talk around the task-card topic. In Part 3, give a relevant view, explain it, and support or extend it with an example or perspective. Avoid memorized scripts, forced vocabulary, and anxiety about non-scoring performance details. The paid service promoted for feedback is not necessary to adopt the learning routine.

## 4. Reading: close reading and error diagnosis — 5:06:40–6:36:50

The Reading introduction argues that repeated full tests and generic speed advice do not automatically repair a reading weakness. First decide which skill or task needs attention; use credible practice material; check each answer against passage evidence; diagnose why a response failed; then increase timing gradually. Do not repeat a memorized set simply to raise a practice score.

The vocabulary section frames vocabulary as a key part of understanding the passage and recognizing paraphrase. Its practical method is to read useful material, notice unknown words, infer them from context, check the meaning, and keep a record with examples and related forms. Revisit selected items and use them actively instead of collecting a large list without practice.

The reading-skills and strategy sections argue for question-specific reading operations. For matching headings, read for the paragraph’s main point and make a plain-language heading before looking at the options. Then compare scope and meaning, rejecting an answer that matches only an example or detail. Skimming can orient a reader and scanning can locate a likely passage; neither alone establishes the answer. Close-read the evidence and explain why the selected option fits.

The video recommends free practice tests and describes some sources as “real” or official. Verify provenance before using them as official practice; the video’s counts and authenticity claims are not adopted as course facts. For question families and full examples, see the [complete Reading course note](reading-course-only-reading-course-2026.md).

## 6. Student interviews: useful study practices, unverified results — 7:11:30–11:24:25

The final four hours are ten student success-story interviews. Their titles and reported bands are publisher-selected claims, not independently verified outcomes, and the interviews are promotional. They can suggest questions to ask learners about practice, but they cannot show that a method caused a score change or that another learner should expect the same result.

The recurring practice ideas that align with the course lessons are:

- **Work slowly for accuracy before adding time pressure.** A Reading interview describes taking much longer on a question while learning the method, then improving completion time later. Treat this as an accuracy-first progression to try and measure, not a universal schedule. ([7:11:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=25908s))
- **Use a method and explain errors instead of repeating tests blindly.** Across the Reading and Writing interviews, the teacher asks what changed, what the learner did with feedback, and which mistake was repaired. A student discusses essay volume; the defensible lesson is to inspect and revise work, not to adopt a fixed “number of essays” rule. ([8:35:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=30917s), [8:56:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=32214s))
- **Build language through reading and focused review.** Interviews mention reading external articles and studying language patterns such as prepositions. These are individual routines; their value depends on noticing and using the language, not on a named website or a promised outcome. ([7:25:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=26754s), [9:08:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=32916s))
- **Get specific feedback on productive skills.** One Writing interview describes asking questions in a student group; the course’s broader point is that informed feedback can expose patterns learners miss on their own. Build accessible feedback into a course without requiring the source’s paid group. ([8:40:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=31228s))
- **Prefer clear, controlled expression to complexity for its own sake.** The closing interview discusses studying sentence structure and checking intended meaning against a dictionary; the teacher also cautions against making explanations needlessly complex. This aligns with accuracy and clarity, not a ban on grammatical or lexical range. ([10:54:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=39269s), [11:10:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGtKdsVxV8A&t=40211s))

Other interviews describe time-bound study journeys and scores. Keep those as personal stories. The interviews do not establish a guaranteed timeline, a causal effect of the paid course, or a reliable estimate of typical results.

## Course-design takeaways

- Teach model answers as objects of analysis: idea relevance, organization, cohesion, grammar, and vocabulary. Require an independent attempt before comparison.
- Teach Task 2 from prompt interpretation and idea selection through paragraph development, then whole-essay review. Use a paragraph-count scaffold as a support, not as a rule.
- In Speaking, make recording and criterion-based self-review routine. Pair self-review with specific feedback and one repair target at a time.
- In Reading, develop vocabulary and close-reading skill alongside task strategy. Separate finding the evidence from deciding what it means.
- Use accuracy-first practice, targeted error analysis, and gradual timing as hypotheses to test with learner work.
- Keep score stories, paid-course claims, essay authenticity claims, and fixed timelines outside the course rulebook unless independently verified.
