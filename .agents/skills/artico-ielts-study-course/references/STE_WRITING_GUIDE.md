# Artico Study English (ASE): the writing standard

ASE is a house style inspired by Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100), the controlled language used for aerospace maintenance manuals. We borrow the **discipline**, not the certification. Never claim the course is "STE-compliant".

**Why we use it.** An aerospace technician reads a step once and acts correctly. An IELTS learner must do the same under a clock. Short, exact, consistent sentences let the learner follow the logic without translation, without re-reading, and without feeling lost. The learner may be at IELTS Band 5.5 to 7.0. Many use translation tools. ASE text must survive both.

**What ASE is not.** It is not baby talk. The ideas stay advanced. The sentences stay simple. A page on Task 2 argument can be intellectually rich and still use 15-word sentences.

---

## 1. The twelve house rules

| # | Rule | Bad | Good |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One idea per sentence.** | "Because Task 2 has twice the weight, which means you should plan it first, you may want to start there, although some learners prefer Task 1." | "Task 2 has twice the weight of Task 1. Some learners write Task 2 first. Test both orders before the exam." |
| 2 | **One action per step.** A step is an imperative sentence. | "Read the question and underline the keywords and find them in the passage." | "1. Read the question. 2. Mark the key words. 3. Find them in the passage." |
| 3 | **Condition first, then action.** | "Count the words, if the question has a limit." | "If the question has a word limit, count the words." |
| 4 | **Active voice.** Use passive only when the doer is unknown or unimportant. | "The answer should be checked by you." | "Check the answer." |
| 5 | **Simple tenses.** Present for rules. Past for examples of what happened. Future only for real future events. | "You will be wanting to have been checking…" | "Check your answer." |
| 6 | **One word for one meaning.** Use the approved term every time (see Section 4). | "passage… text… article… extract" for the same thing | "passage" every time |
| 7 | **Keep articles.** Do not drop *the*, *a*, or *an*. | "Read passage." | "Read the passage." |
| 8 | **No idioms, slang, or phrasal verbs with several meanings** in explanation. (Teach them only in language pages, inside a labelled example.) | "Nail the overview", "stumble over", "get across" | "Write the overview correctly", "make an error", "explain" |
| 9 | **No contractions in explanation.** (Allowed inside learner-language samples.) | "don't", "it's" | "do not", "it is" |
| 10 | **Define a term where it first appears.** Then never use a different word for it. | "Use paraphrase." (undefined) | "A **paraphrase** is the same meaning in different words." |
| 11 | **Numbers as numerals.** Keep units. Keep official numbers exact. | "forty questions", "an hour" | "40 questions", "60 minutes" |
| 12 | **Say what is true, and no more.** Do not promise scores. Do not invent statistics. Keep source qualifiers (*can*, *may*, *often*) when the source has them. | "This trick guarantees Band 8." | "This method can improve accuracy. It does not guarantee a band." |

## 2. Length limits (the linter enforces these)

| Item | Target | Warning | Error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Words per sentence, **steps and instructions** | ≤ 15 | > 20 | > 25 |
| Words per sentence, **explanations** | ≤ 20 | > 25 | > 32 |
| Sentences per paragraph | ≤ 4 | > 5 | > 6 |
| Consecutive prose blocks without a callout, table, figure, example, or checkpoint | ≤ 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Words per page (prose only, excluding tables and samples) | 450–900 | > 1,100 | > 1,500 |
| Words per callout | ≤ 45 | > 60 | > 90 |
| Words per checkpoint explanation | ≤ 40 | > 55 | > 80 |

Learner-language samples (inside `annotated`, `contrast`, `sample`, `` `inline code` ``, `wordBank` example cells) are **exempt**. They may be longer, because they copy real IELTS-style language.

## 3. How to write each kind of sentence

**Definition.** Term first, in bold. Then "is" or "means". Then the simplest true description.
> A **distractor** is a wrong option that looks right.

**Rule.** State the rule as a command or a plain fact. Put the reason in the next sentence.
> Use the passage as the only source. Your own knowledge is not evidence.

**Procedure step.** Imperative verb first. No more than one condition.
> If a question has two parts, answer both.

**Warning (Trap).** Say the wrong action, then the result. Then the correct action.
> Many learners write the first time they hear. The speaker can change it later. Wait for the final time.

**Example intro.** One sentence. Say what the example shows, not what it is.
> This passage extract shows a statement that is Not Given.

**Contrast.** Use two short sentences. Do not use "whereas" or "although" in the same sentence as another clause.
> A resident lives in a place. A citizen has legal membership of a state.

**Why-sentence.** Start with "This is because" only when you cannot restructure. Prefer: cause as a separate sentence.
> The speaker said "However". This word signals a change.

**Bridge (end of section).** One sentence. Say what the next section does and why you need it.
> You can now read a claim in full. Next, you decide if the passage supports it.

## 4. Approved terms (use these words only)

Use the **Use** term every time. Do not use the **Avoid** words for the same idea. The linter flags **Avoid** words.

| Idea | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| The reading material in the Reading test | **passage** | text, article, extract (except "extract" for a short quoted part), reading text |
| The audio in the Listening test | **recording** | audio (except in `media` metadata), track, clip |
| The written words of a recording | **script** (course material) / **transcript** (after the test, for review) | text of the audio |
| A numbered item in Listening or Reading | **question** | item, problem, task (reserve *task* for Writing/Speaking) |
| What the learner writes in Listening or Reading | **answer** | response (reserve *response* for Writing/Speaking output) |
| What the learner writes or says in Writing/Speaking | **response** | answer (when meaning a whole essay or talk), output |
| A Writing or Speaking assignment | **task** | assignment, exercise |
| The Task 2 question | **prompt** | topic (topic = general subject only), title |
| The chart, graph, table, map, or diagram in Task 1 | **visual** | picture, image, figure |
| The same meaning in different words | **paraphrase** | synonym (use *synonym* only for single words), rewording |
| The exact words or data that support an answer | **evidence** | proof, clue, hint |
| A claim to judge (True/False/Not Given) | **statement** | sentence, claim (use *claim* for arguments in essays) |
| A score from 0 to 9 | **band** | grade, level, mark (reserve *mark* for one Listening/Reading point) |
| A point scored for one correct answer | **mark** | point, score |
| A criterion used to assess | **criterion** (plural **criteria**) | rubric, standard |
| IELTS Academic | **Academic** | AC, the academic test |
| IELTS General Training | **General Training** (after first use per page: **GT**) | General, GT test |
| The person who takes the test | **you** / **the candidate** (only for official rules) | test-taker, student (in rules) |
| The person who conducts Speaking | **the examiner** | interviewer, teacher |
| A thing to avoid | **trap** (callout) / **error** (in a learner response) | mistake (allowed in everyday phrasing), pitfall, myth (reserve *myth* for the Claims chapter) |
| Preparing before the clock starts | **preview** | pre-reading, look ahead |
| Short practice of a failed skill | **repair** | fix, remedy |
| Evidence of current level | **baseline** | starting score |
| Using a skill on new material | **transfer** | generalisation |

The linter also reads `src/web/ielts-study/glossary.js`. Every term in bold at first use must exist there with a plain definition of 25 words or fewer.

## 5. Preferred simple words

| Use | Do not use |
|---|---|
| use | utilise, employ, leverage |
| start | commence, initiate, kick off |
| end, finish | conclude (as a verb), terminate |
| show | demonstrate, illustrate (unless teaching the verb) |
| help | facilitate, assist (unless teaching the verb) |
| need | require (except in a **Requirement** callout, where "required" is the official word) |
| check | verify, ascertain |
| choose | select (except "select the answer" in UI-style text) |
| get | obtain, acquire |
| explain | elucidate, articulate |
| make clear | clarify |
| before | prior to |
| after | subsequent to, following |
| because | due to the fact that |
| to | in order to |
| if | in the event that |
| many | numerous (except in language pages that teach it) |
| about | approximately (except in Task 1 language pages) |
| try | endeavour, attempt |
| also | furthermore, moreover, additionally |
| but | however (use *however* only when teaching it as a linker) |

Never use these words in explanation text: *simply*, *just* (as "only"), *obviously*, *of course*, *easy*, *easily*, *trick*, *hack*, *secret*, *guarantee*, *perfect*, *amazing*, *awesome*, *congrats*, *congratulations*, *nailed*, *streak*, *XP*, *badge*, *leaderboard*, *level up*, *supercharge*, *skyrocket*, *crush*, *ace*, *magic*, *crucial* (use *important*), *vital*, *ensure* (use *make sure*), *utilize*, *leverage*, *delve*, *realm*, *tapestry*, *navigate* (as metaphor), *journey* (as metaphor), *unlock*, *master* (as a verb).

Never use gamified praise or UI copy anywhere in Study mode (for example "Great job!", "Keep your streak alive!", "+10 XP", "You unlocked the next lesson!"). Serious learners read to understand the test, not to collect tokens.

Never use these abbreviations: *e.g.*, *i.e.*, *etc.*, *vs.*, *cf.*. Write "for example", "that is", "and so on" (or list the items), "against".

## 6. Structure rules that keep the learner oriented

1. **Name the section as the learner's question or action.** "How do I choose between False and Not Given?" is better than "Distinguishing the options".
2. **Put the big picture first.** Every page opens with a `lead` of 1 to 3 sentences: what this page is, why it matters, what the learner can do after it.
3. **Never use a term before you define it.** If page B needs a term from page A, list A in `needs`. The linter checks that A comes earlier in the course order.
4. **End each section with a bridge** (one sentence) when another section follows.
5. **Give the rule, then the example, then the exception.** Do not hide the rule inside the example.
6. **Put the Requirement (official fact) before the Method.** The learner should know the fixed limits before they choose a method.
7. **Use lists for three or more parallel items.** Use a table for two or more dimensions.
8. **Use parallel grammar in lists.** If one item starts with a verb, all items start with a verb.
9. **Keep one topic per paragraph.** The first sentence says the topic.
10. **Avoid pronouns that point back more than one sentence.** Repeat the noun.

## 7. Labels: Requirement, Method, Option, Trap

The source notes use three labels. Keep them exactly. Add one: **Trap**.

| Label | Meaning | Visual | Writing rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Requirement** | An official instruction, format condition, or assessment principle. | Blue bar, shield icon | Quote exact numbers. Link the official source. Never soften. |
| **Method** | A useful procedure. It helps. It does not guarantee a band. | Neutral bar, steps icon | Imperative steps. State what the method is for. |
| **Option** | A coaching choice. The learner should test it. | Dashed bar, branch icon | Begin with "You can…" or "Some learners…". End with "Check whether it helps you." |
| **Trap** | A common wrong action, a false rule, or a risky shortcut. | Amber bar, warning icon | Wrong action first. Result second. Correct action third. |

If you cannot tell which label applies, return to the source. If the source gives no label, use **Method**. Never present an **Option** as a **Requirement**.

## 8. Before and after: eight rewrites from the source notes

**1. Source (1.1).**
> A learner can have two different problems: a language problem or a task problem.

**ASE page.**
> You can have two kinds of problem. A **language problem** means you cannot understand or express the meaning. A **task problem** means you have the language but you did not follow the task.

**2. Source (3.3).** "Do not stop listening when you hear the first plausible answer."

**ASE step.** "Keep listening after the first possible answer. The speaker can change it."

**3. Source (4.5).**
> Not Given: the passage does not provide enough information to confirm or contradict the statement.

**ASE page.**
> **Not Given** means the passage does not say. It gives no support. It gives no contradiction. Do not mark it False only because the information is missing.

**4. Source (6.11).** "A category's share can fall while its absolute number rises, if the total grows sufficiently."

**ASE page.**
> A share can fall while the number rises. This happens when the total grows faster.

**5. Source (8.13).** "Do not require the reader to supply a missing logical step."

**ASE rule.** "Write every step of your reason. The reader cannot see your thoughts."

**6. Source (9.14).** "A pause can have different causes."

**ASE page.** "A pause has more than one possible cause. Find the cause first. Then choose a repair."

**7. Source (11.2).** "Learning evidence shows what you can do with support."

**ASE definition.** "**Learning evidence** shows what you can do with help. **Performance evidence** shows what you can do alone, under test conditions."

**8. Source (1.6).** "A spelling error can lose an item mark. It does not create one fixed band deduction."

**ASE Trap.** "A spelling error loses the mark for that question. It does not lower your band by a fixed amount."

## 9. Learner-language samples

Samples are text the learner might write, say, hear, or read in a test. They are **not** ASE. They should look like real IELTS-style language: natural, correct, and at the target level.

Rules for samples:
1. **Original only.** Never copy official IELTS questions, Cambridge books, or any past paper. Write new material in the same shape.
2. **Fictional names and data.** Use invented places and numbers (for example "Bellford", "Riverbend"). Label invented data as such: "Practice data, invented."
3. **Label every sample.** "Original practice passage", "Sample response (not an assessed band)".
4. **Correct unless it is a deliberate error example.** Mark deliberate errors with a `contrast` block, never by surprise.
5. **Never attach a band to a sample.** The notes say model bands are not independent evidence.
