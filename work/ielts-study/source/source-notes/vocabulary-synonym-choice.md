## Distilled lesson

The useful point is not “replace every common word.” Learn several accurate ways to express a concept, then choose the option that fits the intended meaning, grammar, collocation, and register. An alternative may be a phrase rather than a single word. A precise familiar word is better than a synonym that shifts the meaning. ([0:23–4:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=23s))

Examples that show why context matters:

- For *people*, *individuals* focuses on persons one by one; *the public* treats them as a collective. ([0:23–1:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=23s))
- For *children*, age labels are not interchangeable. The video distinguishes young people or youth from minors, adolescents, infants, and toddlers; choose the exact group the prompt means. ([7:53–9:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=473s))
- For *students*, *learners* emphasizes learning broadly, *pupils* usually means school-age learners, and *scholars* can imply advanced or high-achieving study. ([9:31–10:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=571s))
- For *work*, *employment* can name paid work generally while *career* implies a longer professional path. For *family*, *household* is the people living together, not necessarily an extended family. ([13:51–14:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=831s); [20:37–21:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=1237s))
- *Popular* means liked by many; *common*, *frequent*, or *widespread* describe how often something occurs or how broadly it exists. ([34:11–35:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=2051s))
- *Data* suggests facts or figures; *knowledge* suggests organized or understood information. *Feasible* and *viable* both relate to possibility but foreground practicality or likelihood of working in different ways. ([28:55–29:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=1735s); [32:24–33:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=1944s))
- Some alternatives have different grammar or collocation. Check singular/plural, countability, prepositions, word forms, and the nouns or verbs that naturally accompany the item. ([14:54–15:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=894s))

The instructor’s practical check is to look up an uncertain word in a reliable learner’s dictionary, compare the definitions and example uses, and use it only in a sentence where it fits. Keep the item in a context-rich vocabulary notebook and retrieve it in fresh speaking or writing practice; this connects to the full [vocabulary learning system](vocabulary-learning-system.md). ([12:17–12:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=737s); [37:18–38:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8oYpg7Gb1QI&t=2238s))

## A second lesson: when a synonym changes the meaning

The later video calls alternatives “close” or “loose” synonyms. Its transferable method is to compare definitions, scope, grammar, and the context of the actual sentence before replacing a word. “Safe to use” is not a permanent label: the right choice depends on the exact intended meaning.

- **People:** *residents*, *citizens*, *locals*, and *natives* name narrower groups or relations; they are not neutral replacements for people in general. *The public* or *individuals* may fit in some contexts. A word such as *men* can exclude women and children, so match the category the prompt means. ([2:32–7:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tPrdQAefy4U&t=152s))
- **Children:** *young people* may include adults under some uses; *teens* and *adolescents* are narrower age groups; *minors* and *juveniles* can sound legal or formal. Choose the group and register the sentence requires; legal age cutoffs and everyday age labels vary by context. ([7:07–11:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tPrdQAefy4U&t=427s))
- **Time:** first identify whether the sentence means a duration, a historical period, a point in time, or scheduling/measuring as a verb. *Period*, *era*, and *age* may describe spans but are not interchangeable with every sense of *time*. Check usage and dialect for adverbs such as *presently* rather than treating them as universal one-word swaps. ([11:58–14:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tPrdQAefy4U&t=718s))
- **Company:** *business* can be a close alternative, while *corporation*, *firm*, *organization*, *manufacturer*, and *agency* indicate different legal forms, structures, functions, or scope. Use the specific term only when it is accurate. ([14:55–18:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tPrdQAefy4U&t=895s))
- **Parents:** *mother* and *father* can specify a parent; a *guardian* can care for someone without being a parent. *Ancestors* refers to earlier generations, and *sire* is not a neutral alternative for a parent in ordinary Task 2 prose. ([18:06–19:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tPrdQAefy4U&t=1086s))

If the learner cannot retrieve a precise alternative, repeating a correct word is better than changing the meaning or making a grammatical error. Then use the revision as a vocabulary-learning moment: check a reliable dictionary, inspect example sentences and collocations, and practise a fresh sentence. The channel’s claim that repeatedly using the same word necessarily lowers a score is too broad; accuracy and appropriacy remain central, and development can use reference words or a relevant phrase rather than a mechanical synonym.

## A quiz lesson: vocabulary, register, and fixed expressions

The quiz is useful as a set of quick contrasts, not as a dependable answer key or a prescription to sound “native.” Its strongest teaching point is to check the word’s part of speech and sentence fit as well as its rough meaning. *Improve* is a verb, while *better* is usually an adjective in the intended sense; *substantial* often means considerable or significant, while *immense* means extremely large. *Intricate* and *detailed* overlap but are not interchangeable in every sentence. The quiz itself rejects *understandable* for *feasible*; feasible means possible or practicable, as confirmed by the [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/feasible). Likewise, *paramount* means foremost or of greatest importance, not simply “significant.” The opening example “new architecture is making a place” also shows why a replacement must work in a complete sentence, not just match a gloss.

The “textbook versus native English” round is better taught as **register choice**. Phrases such as *I should get going*, *Are you free?*, *I’m done*, *That’s tough*, and *I get what you mean* can sound natural in conversation, while *I must depart immediately* or *I am experiencing considerable difficulty in comprehending this concept* may sound unnecessarily formal in an everyday exchange. But naturalness depends on audience, purpose, and situation: the quiz’s slang examples (*piss me off*, *I’m dead*) are informal and context-bound, and are not general IELTS Writing models. IELTS Speaking calls for natural, relevant communication; IELTS Writing requires a register suited to the task. Concision is useful, but do not treat all formal wording as “robotic,” or swap in slang simply to sound native.

The idiom round contains a teachable correction: the established form is *Don’t count your chickens before they hatch* (see [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/don-t-count-your-chickens-before-they-hatch)), not “don’t count your eggs.” The other examples include *straight from the horse’s mouth*, *once in a blue moon*, *the apple of my eye*, and *kill two birds with one stone*. Teach idioms with their exact form, meaning, and appropriate context; never force them into Speaking or Writing for a quota. The generation-specific slang round (*rizz*, *slay*, *yapping*, *fit*, *hits different*, *no lie*) is time-sensitive and best used only to notice informal register, not as core exam vocabulary.

The “very” round can prompt work on intensity and collocation—*very strange → bizarre*, *very happy → elated*, *very painful → excruciating*, *very clean → spotless*—but these are not universal substitutions. *Broke* usually means having no money at the moment, not every sense of *poor*; *simple* and *easy* also differ. In the final round, rewriting pompous sentences into clear everyday English models a useful editing move: keep the intended proposition, remove needless wording, and choose a tone that fits the audience. The prize and free e-book promotion at the end is not instructional evidence.

## Claims and teaching limits

- The original video’s 50-word ranking is said to come from 100 Band 7–9 essays; a later video claims a separate analysis of thousands of essays and software counts for five frequent terms. Neither video publishes enough data, prompts, verification, or method here to assess those claims. Use the examples as practice material, not a representative profile of IELTS writing or a topic forecast.
- Both videos sometimes present varied wording as evidence of lexical range, and the newer title overstates that a single wrong synonym will “destroy” a score. Official Writing and Speaking descriptors require accurate, appropriate, flexible vocabulary and precise meaning; they do not create a synonym quota or imply one word guarantees a band change. See the [2027 range-and-accuracy note](vocabulary-range-and-accuracy-2027.md) and official [Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf).
- A synonym list alone does not teach reliable use. Do not memorize the examples as interchangeable pairs, insert a fixed number into an essay, or vary wording mechanically. The advertised PDF and VIP course are promotions, and this note does not treat them as evidence of score outcomes.
