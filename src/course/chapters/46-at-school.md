# CH46 — At School

**Outcome:** Ask about a school, take part in a first-day introduction, and discuss a child's progress with a teacher.  

## Study

This chapter mainly gives a parent or caregiver's role. You may practice that role even if you do not have a child. Useful subjects include **English**, **math**, **science**, **history**, **art**, **music**, and **physical education**, often abbreviated **PE**. A **curriculum** describes what students study; a **class size** is the number of students in a class.

### Asking about a school

**Caregiver:** We're considering this school. Could I ask a few questions?  
**Staff member:** Of course. What would you like to know?  
**Caregiver:** How many students are in each class?  
**Staff member:** Twenty-two in this class.  
**Caregiver:** What after-school activities do you offer?  
**Staff member:** This term, we have art and soccer clubs.  
**Caregiver:** How do families find out about signing up?  
**Staff member:** We'll give you the information for each club.  

The numbers and programs describe this fictional school. Other questions include **How much homework is assigned?**, **Is there an after-school program?**, **Do students wear a uniform?**, and **Who should I speak to about enrollment?** **Enrollment** concerns joining as a student. Asking the relevant staff member lets you obtain the school's actual process.

### A first day

**Caregiver:** Hi, I'm Elena, Sam's aunt. Sam is starting today.  
**Teacher:** Welcome, Sam. I'm Ms. Patel.  
**Student:** Hi.  
**Caregiver:** He's excited, but a little nervous.  
**Teacher:** That's understandable. I'll show him where to put his bag.  
**Caregiver:** Thank you. Who should I contact if I have a question later?  
**Teacher:** The school office can direct you to the right person.  

**A little nervous** describes a feeling without saying the child cannot participate. A student can ask **Where should I put this?** or **Could you show me where to go?** These are supplied first-day support phrases, not an assumption that the student already knows every school instruction.

### A parent–teacher conversation

**Teacher:** Sam is making progress in reading.  
**Caregiver:** That's good to hear. How is he doing in math?  
**Teacher:** He understands the ideas, but sometimes rushes through the work.  
**Caregiver:** Could you give me an example?  
**Teacher:** Yesterday, he skipped one part of an assignment.  
**Caregiver:** What could we practice at home?  
**Teacher:** We can agree on a small activity and check how it goes.  

**How is he doing?** is a genuine progress question here, not simply a greeting. **Could you give me an example?** helps turn a broad report into something concrete. You can raise a concern yourself: **She's been worried about coming to school. Who can we talk to?** You can also disagree or request clarification: **That isn't what we're seeing at home. Could we compare notes?**

## Practice

For a solo route, read the staff or teacher lines and speak the caregiver's turns. Use the fictional facts in the models.

1. Ask about class size, an after-school program and enrollment information. The staff member offers to provide a leaflet; ask them to explain the enrollment section.
2. Introduce a child who is nervous on the first day. Ask who to contact with later questions.
3. The teacher says **She's doing well, but some assignments are unfinished.** Ask for an example and a possible next step.
4. Your concern is that the child is worried about school. State it and ask whom to speak to.

**Sample possibilities:** “How many students are in a class? Could you explain the enrollment information?” / “This is Sam. He's a little nervous. Who should I contact with questions?” / “Could you give me an example? What could we work on?” / “She's worried about coming to school. Who can we talk to?”  

Repeat later with a different subject or concern. Check that the questions invite specific information and that you did not have to invent a school rule to complete the exchange.
