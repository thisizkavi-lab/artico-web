## Main through-line

The course’s most reusable principle is: **make the response easy to understand by answering the exact prompt, developing a small number of relevant ideas, and using language the writer can control.** The teacher repeatedly links a clear plan to a clear essay: identify every part of the task, choose a position or response, preview the main ideas, develop them in the body, and summarize them in the conclusion.

The course’s sample essay frame is commonly an introduction, two body paragraphs, and a conclusion. This is a practical scaffold, not an IELTS-mandated paragraph count. IELTS assesses how fully and relevantly the task is answered, how ideas are supported, coherence and cohesion, vocabulary, and grammatical range and accuracy. The official Writing page confirms Task 2 requires at least 250 words, is a connected response, may use examples from personal experience, and carries twice the weighting of Task 1. The British Council’s current preparation guidance also recommends planning, relevance, developed main ideas, accurate language, and proofreading.

## 0:59–10:07 — improve by targeting the bottleneck

- Start with the required score and deadline. The teacher argues that study time should be aimed at the real score requirement rather than a vague wish to “improve everything.” ([0:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=59s))
- Use recent scores or a diagnostic to identify which skill or criterion is limiting progress. His suggestion to stop studying a module already at least half a band above target is a triage heuristic, not a universal rule; maintain the skill and recheck it.
- Get specific feedback on the weakness, practise repairing it, then verify the repair on new work. The video warns against doing many full tests before fixing a recurring problem. A sound course sequence is diagnose → learn → practise a focused repair → check transfer → add timed practice.
- Increase speed and test stamina after the method is reliable, rather than beginning with repeated full timed essays. His example staircase gradually shortens a writing task from an hour toward the test time.
- The video presents expert feedback as valuable because learners can misdiagnose the cause of a low score. Course materials should translate this into a repeatable rubric and peer/self-review routine, with teacher feedback where available.

## 10:07–48:50 — language and strategy cautions

- **Vocabulary:** choose words for meaning and fit, not rarity. Accurate common language is better than an advanced-looking word used with the wrong meaning, spelling, grammar, or collocation. The instructor urges learners to build vocabulary in context and use it only when they can control it. ([10:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=607s))
- **Cohesion:** linking expressions should show a real relationship between ideas. Do not add them mechanically or chase variety for its own sake. Repeating a simple correct link is preferable to using a more complex one incorrectly.
- **Grammar:** demonstrate a useful range while keeping structures accurate. Repair recurring errors (the video highlights articles, punctuation, agreement, and tense) before trying to force complex forms.
- **Repetition:** lexical variety can help, but replacing every repeated word with a risky synonym can create errors. Repeat a precise word when needed; vary it only when the replacement fits.
- **Opening and memorisation:** the teacher rejects canned hooks, stock background statements, and formulaic introductions, recommending a direct paraphrase and answer instead. This aligns with official preparation guidance to avoid memorised responses that fail to fit the task. Claims that any repeated phrase automatically triggers a particular penalty are stronger than official guidance supports. ([10:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=607s), [British Council Task 2 guidance](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/writing))
- **Examples and statistics:** support the particular claim with a plausible, relevant example. Do not insert fabricated precise percentages or impressive-sounding studies. The instructor prefers examples grounded in familiar experience and generalized only as far as the writer can honestly support. This is a useful credibility check, not a ban on evidence or numbers.
- **Idioms and register:** avoid forcing idioms or informal phrases into a formal essay. The useful principle is appropriate register and accurate use, not a universal prohibition on idiomatic language.
- **Length:** the instructor says very long essays can consume time and drift off topic; this is a practical time-management warning. IELTS’ published Task 2 requirement is a minimum of 250 words; the course’s suggested ranges are not an official maximum. ([40:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=2400s), [official IELTS Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing))

## 48:50–1:03:30 — the general answer-first method

1. Treat the essay as communication: make the answer easy for the reader to find.
2. Read the task verbs and identify every required part. For a “discuss both views and give your opinion” task, for example, track both views and the writer’s position separately.
3. For an opinion task, state the position and two supporting points early. For other task types, preview the required response rather than forcing an opinion formula onto them.
4. Build each body paragraph around one main point; explain how it answers the prompt and support it with a relevant example. Do not scatter several underdeveloped points through a paragraph.
5. Use the conclusion to recap the main answer and points. The instructor’s “essay acceleration” idea is that a useful introduction doubles as the essay plan, while the conclusion mirrors its key points.
6. When ideas feel difficult, choose the simplest relevant ideas that can be explained and supported quickly. He suggests checking whether a topic is real or overcomplicated by comparing it with authentic Cambridge/official practice questions. He also suggests reading a short daily news story on recurring themes; treat this as optional background-building, not a need to memorize facts.

The teacher warns that confused thinking creates unclear writing. A useful planning test is to ask: What is the prompt asking? What is my answer? What are my two strongest reasons or required points? Can I explain each one and give a credible example?

## 1:03:30–1:23:35 — introductions

The video contrasts two openings to a prompt about home schooling. Its preferred version does three jobs efficiently: (a) paraphrase the task accurately, (b) state the position, and (c) preview the main reasons. This also creates a short plan for the body paragraphs. ([1:03:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=3816s))

The weaker opening contains a generic hook, a stock “hotly debated topic” phrase, conspicuously rare vocabulary used incorrectly, a background sentence that does not answer the prompt, a mis-scoped substitute (“adolescents” for children generally), and a memorized “this essay will discuss…” formula. The lesson is to preserve scope and meaning while paraphrasing, rather than swapping in synonyms mechanically.

For an agree/disagree question, a clear “I agree” or “I disagree” is acceptable. Official IDP guidance also explicitly suggests using first person when needed to make the opinion clear. For a prompt asking for both views, the introduction must show how the essay will address both views and the writer’s position. A single rigid opening template should not be applied to every task type. ([IDP on Task 2 introductions](https://ielts.idp.com/qatar/prepare/article-writing-a-good-introduction-writing-task-2/en-gb))

## 1:23:35–1:38:20 — idea generation

The teacher defines a useful idea as one that is **relevant**, can be **developed**, and can be selected **quickly** under time pressure. He argues against open-ended brainstorming that generates a pile of unrelated ideas; this is his teaching preference, not a claim that all brainstorming is harmful.

Two named methods:

- **Coffee-shop method:** restate the formal prompt in plain everyday language and imagine explaining your answer to a friend. This can reduce “test mode” pressure and expose straightforward ideas.
- **Family Fortunes method:** ask what the most common, ordinary answer might be, rather than reaching for an unusual or impressive argument.

The methods are prompts for thinking, not answer generators to apply blindly. Check that a chosen point directly answers the exact question and can be developed without inventing support. Authentic questions and broad everyday themes (e.g., education, health, technology, environment, government) are useful practice; the teacher’s prediction about recurring topics is not a guarantee of test content. ([1:23:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=5017s))

## 1:38:20–1:56:47 — examples

The lesson compares examples for relevance, specificity, credibility, and fit with the paragraph’s main claim. A small personal story can be a starting point; make it understandable to the reader and avoid presenting one person’s experience as proof about everyone. A broader local or familiar example can work when the wider claim is plausible and accurate.

The video rejects fake precision (such as an unverified percentage), vague praise (“it is amazing”), examples about the general topic that miss the prompt’s exact point, and claims that overstate “all” or “every.” Its example-building sequence is: choose something real or familiar → connect it to the main claim → check that it is not too narrow or too personal → state only what can be supported. Do not “generalize” an anecdote by inventing a statistic or unsupported population claim. ([1:38:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=5900s))

## 2:53:05–3:38:49 — diagnosing and revising a weak essay

The worked prompt asks why some people think skilled professionals should serve the country where they trained, why others think they should work wherever they choose, and for the candidate’s opinion. The source’s lower-scoring draft demonstrates recurring failures:

- a stock opening unrelated to the prompt and spelling errors;
- an introduction that restates the views but never gives the requested position;
- several separate examples (doctors plus engineers) where a paragraph needs a central point that can be developed;
- a second paragraph about the difficulty of moving abroad, which does not answer whether workers should be free to move;
- an unclear example and a conclusion that introduces a recommendation instead of summarizing the requested answer;
- avoidable spelling, article, word-choice, and grammar errors alongside the task-response problems.

The revision method is to parse the prompt into its required parts before drafting, decide on the writer’s position, and make the introduction answer each part in plain terms. In the body, choose one central point per paragraph; explain it by repeatedly asking “so what?” until the reasoning is clear; then add a relevant, credible example. The “such as doctors and engineers” phrase in the prompt is treated as illustration of the broader group, not an instruction to discuss both professions separately. The conclusion recaps the two views and position rather than adding a new idea. ([2:53:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=10385s), [3:19:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=11957s))

The teacher argues that plain, well-controlled language can be stronger than elaborate phrasing and that limited slips can occur in high-band responses. His numerical claim that errors in more than half of sentences necessarily cap a response at Band 6 is not used as a rule; official marking is based on published descriptors, not that threshold. ([3:14:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=11649s), [official assessment criteria](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/results/how-is-ielts-assessed))

## 3:38:49–4:23:55 — opinion essay workshop

The tutor helps a learner answer a curfew prompt through a repeatable sequence:

1. Clarify the exact task and any unfamiliar word from context.
2. Identify the broad topic, the prompt’s narrower focus, its scope (here, teenagers, outdoors, at night, with an adult exception), and the command (“What is your opinion?”).
3. Choose a clear position and generate relevant points using ordinary reasons, another person’s perspective, or personal experience. Check whether the points are distinct rather than duplicates.
4. Put the position and two reasons into the introduction; use those same reasons to orient the two body paragraphs.
5. Start each paragraph with a clear topic sentence. Explain the claim as a logical chain, then support it with a credible example. Link the example back to the claim.
6. Conclude by restating the position and summarizing the reasons.

The feedback identifies an example of how to improve: replace an irrelevant general background sentence with a direct topic sentence, make the explanation actually prove the claim, avoid overclaiming (e.g., “the police doubled” if that cannot be supported), and correct grammar without swapping in words that sound unnatural. ([3:38:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzdG66VK75c&t=13129s))

## 4:23:55–4:40:42 — problem-and-solution workshop

The lesson uses fake social-media accounts. Learners first name a relevant problem, then explain why it matters and give an example; they do the same for a possible solution. The teacher’s preferred scaffold is: introduction that paraphrases the situation and signals the problem/solution; body paragraph 1 develops one problem; body paragraph 2 develops one solution; conclusion summarizes both. He says this is his chosen structure rather than the only possible form. Where a task asks for multiple problems or solutions, the response still has to cover the prompt adequately; one point per paragraph is a depth-first teaching choice, not a blanket limit.

For practice, write a short explanation of a problem and a short explanation of a matching solution before combining them into an essay. Ask whether a reader unfamiliar with the topic can follow the logic and whether the solution actually addresses the stated problem. The video also recommends authentic news reading as a way to build idea familiarity; treat any news details as practice material and verify facts before citing them.

## 4:40:42–4:47:23 — discuss-both-views workshop

Break the prompt into three requirements: why people support view A, why others support view B, and the candidate’s own view. The suggested plan states both views and the position in the introduction, gives one developed paragraph for each view, makes the writer’s agreement explicit, and summarizes the answer in the conclusion. The instructor favors choosing a clear position over spending time on a vague “both sides in every case” response. The specific examples and paragraph order can vary as long as both requested views and the candidate’s position are fully and clearly addressed.

## 4:47:23–5:11:05 — active ways to learn from sample essays

The instructor’s cautions: do not trust unverified “Band 9” examples, passively read without a task, assume the learner can judge a sample’s band, memorize/copy it, or paste a full sample into a test response. The claimed authenticity and band scores of his own linked sample bank are promotional claims and are not independently verified in these notes.

He presents ten active study techniques, though the captions jump from item 6 to item 8; **item 7 could not be identified**:

1. Cover the essay, generate your own ideas from the prompt, then compare relevance with the sample.
2. Study one sample’s main points, then independently write an explanation and example before comparing.
3. Map the essay structure across several examples of the same question family; infer a flexible plan rather than memorizing a script.
4. Mark cohesion devices, identify their purpose, and judge whether their use is accurate; successful essays may use them sparingly.
5. Find, categorize, and correct grammar errors in learner samples.
6. Notice sentence structures and why they were used; practise them as options, not as chunks to copy.
7. **Missing/unclear in the captions.**
8. Record unfamiliar words and collocations, infer meaning from context, verify them, and build a vocabulary notebook with usage/examples.
9. Find word-choice or meaning errors and unnecessary repetition; replace a word only with a genuine, accurate alternative.
10. Write a response to the same prompt and compare ideas, structure, and development, while remembering that many different good answers are possible.

## 5:11:05–6:09:03 — timed writing demonstration

The instructor works on a double question: whether now is the best time in history to live, and what other period would be interesting. He selects reasons he can develop, plans briefly, returns to the wording while drafting, and saves a separate proofreading pass. His choice of the 1960s is presented as an easy-to-write personal choice, not a model factual argument. The demonstration reinforces:

- identify every part of a double question;
- choose examples and ideas for clarity and developability, not to prove general knowledge;
- draft the argument first and check it separately for meaning, task coverage, grammar, spelling, and word choice;
- accept natural repetition when accurate; improve collocations and topic vocabulary only where the edit truly helps;
- use time to tighten a long draft and protect the second task.

The teacher writes about 308 words and says his preferred range is around 260–300, but the official Task 2 minimum is 250 words and there is no stated hard maximum. His advice is a personal timing/quality target, not the marking rule.

The video then includes a separate **General Training Task 1 informal letter** to a friend who has a university offer and needs accommodation advice. The demonstration selects an informal tone, answers the requested bullet points, uses the writer’s real experience as useful detail, and closes with a natural informal sign-off. The teacher prefers about 160–200 words in this example; the official minimum is 150 words. Treat the range as a personal drafting target, and keep all bullet points covered with an appropriate tone. ## Course design takeaways

- Teach students to annotate the task’s action, scope, and parts before they plan.
- Model one paragraph at a time: claim → explanation → example → link to the question.
- Separate idea relevance, development, language control, and timing in feedback so students know what to repair.
- Provide examples of effective simple language as well as examples of overcomplication and off-task writing.
- Use sample essays as active analysis material, then require a fresh response to a different prompt.
- Preserve question-family differences. “Answer first” is the shared principle; introduction wording and body organization must fit the actual task.
