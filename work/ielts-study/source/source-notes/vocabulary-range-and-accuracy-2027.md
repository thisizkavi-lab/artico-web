## Distilled teaching points

- **Develop range, but do not confuse it with rarity.** Familiar words often carry most of a response. Prepare words across topics and learn their meaning, collocation, and register before test day; on test day, choose the item that communicates the intended point accurately. A handful of memorized “Band 9 words” cannot compensate for narrow or inaccurate use. ([1:26–3:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=86s))
- **Keep repetition purposeful.** Repeating a precise topic word can be clearer than replacing it with a misleading synonym. Teach paraphrase to avoid unnecessary repetition and show flexibility, but do not impose a “never repeat any word” rule. ([5:39–8:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=339s))
- **Build topic vocabulary that does a job.** The video’s essay sample contains ordinary content words for recurring broad themes such as people, education, health, work, and diet. This reinforces the previous course’s method: read and listen widely, then collect words and phrases that help explain a specific idea—not a universal list that can be inserted into any prompt. ([8:33–10:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=513s))
- **Use cohesive devices when they help the reader.** A small set of familiar linkers can be enough. Add transitions only when they express the actual relationship between ideas; overuse can make prose mechanical. The target is clear progression and cohesive control, not the highest possible number of linking words. ([10:33–14:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=633s))
- **Treat sophisticated vocabulary as a precision tool.** Less common vocabulary is useful when it expresses a meaning more exactly and the writer can control it. The lesson’s “100% rule” is a test-day heuristic, not an official rule that learners should avoid all unfamiliar or ambitious language in practice. Learn and practise new language before the exam. ([16:18–20:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=978s))

## Companion word bank: teach distinctions, not substitutions

The 56-minute companion models a useful vocabulary lesson structure: introduce a word in context; identify its part of speech and meaning; compare near-synonyms; teach a natural collocation; then check a fresh example and a learner-produced sentence. The transferable course method is to make the limits of a word visible. The video itself cautions against memorizing the list and replacing words mechanically, and several of its own student-essay examples contain errors or awkward collocations, so examples need editing before reuse. ([0:00–2:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=0s), [13:00–16:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=780s), [28:00–33:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=1680s))

- **Compare boundaries, not just dictionary synonyms.** *Adolescents* refers to teenagers, not children of every age; *revenue* is not the same as profit; *address a problem* can mean discuss or deal with it without claiming it has been solved. Check the intended meaning before swapping one item for another. ([13:00–16:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=780s), [44:30–46:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=2670s))
- **Store useful word partners.** Teach a word with the grammatical pattern and collocation learners are likely to need, such as *prone to*, *allocate resources*, *raise awareness*, *carbon dioxide emissions*, and *nutritional deficiency*. Use these as examples to investigate, not a fixed quota of “Band 9 phrases.”
- **Keep cohesion distinct from lexical display.** The source correctly notes that *hence* expresses a logical relationship and belongs in a cohesion lesson as well as a vocabulary discussion. Use it only when the preceding idea supports the relationship; a familiar connector is better than a mismatched formal one. ([32:25–33:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=1945s))
- **Do not equate CEFR labels with IELTS scores.** The source presents a Text Inspector analysis that reports 3.2% C2 and 6.04% C1 words in its 100-essay set, then compares a small Cambridge 18 sample. The essays, prompts, band verification, and analysis settings are not published. Treat the figures and the claim that the listed words are especially common in Band 7–9 scripts as unverified. IELTS descriptors assess accurate and appropriate range; they do not define a C1/C2 percentage quota. ([52:00–56:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_s1rIKaoAyM&t=3120s); [IELTS Writing Band Descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

The transcript explicitly introduces these 59 headword entries (the on-page title says 69): *viable, schooling, renowned, prime, prone, officials, output, intellect, incentives, irrespective, fundamental, disclose, detrimental, adolescence, accountable, address, affluence, allocate, awareness, bullying, burden, capabilities, commonly, consumption, competence, corruption, downsides, drawbacks, deficiency, embrace, enhance, emissions, establishments, expenditure, exceed, harassment, hence, informative, infrastructure, insights, insufficient, innate, inappropriate, merit, mediocre, notable, numerous, peers, phenomenon, proportion, revenue, resent, sector, workforce, gifted, nutritional, thrive, unsafe,* and *unwind*. Preserve them as candidate items for contextual study, not as an authoritative list of the most frequent or highest-scoring IELTS vocabulary.

## Official-criteria check and evidence limits

IELTS Writing descriptors combine range with accurate and appropriate use. Higher-band descriptors include flexible, precise vocabulary and the skilful use of less common items when appropriate; cohesion should be effective without drawing undue attention. They do not set quotas for rare words, synonyms, or linking expressions. ([IELTS Writing Band Descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

The video reports its 100-essay sample had many more everyday than C1/C2 words, that its top 20 words made up about a third of all tokens, and that seven selected linking words accounted for more than 90% of linkers. The caption’s B2 proportion is garbled. The video does not provide the essays, prompts, sampling rules, band verification, tokenization details, or replication data. Treat these statistics as an illustration of the instructor’s teaching argument, not as the profile of IELTS scripts in general. Its claim that Band 9 essays used fewer linkers than Band 7 essays is likewise a reported finding without enough published detail to make it a course rule. ([3:40–5:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=220s); [10:33–14:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=633s); [14:55–20:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=895s))

The essay sample is described as real Band 7–9 VIP student work and the software as Cambridge’s. Neither the source data nor the analysis procedure is shown in this video. The “90/10 rule,” advertised at the start, is not clearly defined or demonstrated in the exported lesson. The closing discounted feedback offer is promotion, not evidence. ([0:00–0:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=0s); [14:55–15:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WC-8E9lqHhU&t=895s))

## Course application

Pair this note with the [contextual vocabulary-learning system](vocabulary-learning-system.md): use authentic input to build a broader repertoire; practise retrieving and using items in topic sentences and short answers; then edit for precision, collocation, and appropriate cohesion. In feedback, ask whether vocabulary fits and clarifies the intended meaning—not whether it looks advanced.
