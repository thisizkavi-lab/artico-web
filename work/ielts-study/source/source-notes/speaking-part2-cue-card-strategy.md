## Distilled method

1. **Read the whole card.** Identify the main topic, the points the card asks you to include, and any final instruction such as explaining why something matters. Do not plan only from the first bullet.
2. **Choose a relevant example you can describe.** A familiar person, event, object, place, or experience makes it easier to retrieve accurate detail. If the example is only an approximate fit, explain that briefly so the listener can follow. If a word on the card is unclear, ask the examiner what it means at the start of the preparation minute.
3. **Use the minute to make short notes.** Write phrases rather than full sentences, in English, and put them in speaking order. Note at least a useful cue for every task-card point; related points can share one detail. Add only a few memory prompts, not a script.
4. **Give one connected talk.** Open by naming the example and its connection to the topic. Address the card points in a natural order, joining related ones where useful. Develop ideas with a concrete detail, brief sequence, reason, effect, or personal reaction. Do not give every bullet an equal number of seconds, and avoid a disconnected list of facts. Keep the answer tied to the card and explain the aspect the card specifically asks about.
5. **Practise the planning skill before adding time pressure.** First take as long as needed to learn how to find a suitable example and organize notes. Then shorten planning toward one minute and practise speaking for one to two minutes. Record some attempts.
6. **Review against the four criteria.** Check whether ideas stay relevant and progress logically; whether vocabulary is accurate and appropriate; whether grammar has range and control; and whether pronunciation is easy to understand. Pick one recurring issue to work on in the next attempt.

## What to keep and what to qualify

- The useful insight is that cue-card points are planning support, not four equal-length mini speeches. A speaker can combine related points and develop the parts that support one coherent response; there is no equal-time rule.
- The video’s stronger claim that candidates do not need to speak about all four points is not adopted as default course advice. IELTS’s official format says the task card tells candidates what points they should include and that using those points and notes should help them structure a two-minute talk. Official preparation guidance recommends making notes on every point. Teach learners to address all card points, even briefly where appropriate, while avoiding mechanical equal timing. ([IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking); [How to use the preparation time in IELTS Speaking Part 2](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-use-the-preparation-time-in-ielts-speaking-part-2))
- The video’s example of a cue card supports flexible ordering and idea development, but a rigid bullet-by-bullet sequence is not inherently wrong if it produces a relevant, coherent answer. The teaching target is a clear, sufficiently developed long turn.
- The instructor warns against the past-present-future (PPF) formula because it may not fit a given cue card. Teach this as a warning against forcing an irrelevant structure: a timeline can still help when the task naturally calls for it.
- IELTS describes Part 2 as a long-turn task and says Speaking is assessed across the interview using four criteria. The transcript’s claim that Part 2 is extremely important because it alone lets the examiner evaluate all four criteria is overstated; there is no separate Part 2 band score. ([IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking); [IELTS Speaking band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf))
- Claims that the strategy has helped thousands, that learners fail mainly because of the wrong system, and that it will dramatically improve a score are promotional or anecdotal and are not independently verified. The Elon Musk sample includes time-sensitive factual assertions; use it only to illustrate connected development, not as a current factual source or a guaranteed Band 9 model.

## Practice routine for an original course

Use current, authentic practice cards. For each one, have the learner: (a) mark the topic and every requested point; (b) choose an example; (c) create ordered phrase notes covering the points; (d) speak for up to two minutes without reading sentences; and (e) review one criterion-specific improvement. Begin with generous planning time, then move toward test timing. Change the card and repeat so the learner practises the method rather than memorizing a response.
