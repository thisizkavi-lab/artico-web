# CH83 — Seeing a Healthcare Provider

**Outcome:** Report symptoms and concerns accurately, ask about an examination or care plan, and check the next step.  

## Study

A useful report explains **where**, **when it started**, **what it feels like**, and **whether it changes**. You can use short sentences. Not knowing the cause is different from not knowing what you experience.

### Give a report and answer follow-up questions

**Patient:** I've had a cough for a week, and it's getting worse.  
**Clinician:** Have you noticed any other symptoms?  
**Patient:** I've also felt tired. I don't know whether I've had a fever.  
**Clinician:** Tell me about any medicines you're using.  
**Patient:** I brought the names with me. Could you look at the list?  
**Clinician:** Yes. I'd also like to examine you.  
**Patient:** Could you explain what you'll do first?  

“Since Monday” names a starting point; “for a week” names a duration. “It comes and goes” means it is not constant. “It hurts when I move my arm” connects pain with an action. “It's getting better / worse” describes a change.

More specific reports: “I've been throwing up since last night.” “My shoulder hurts when I lift it.” “My child has a fever.” “I found a lump in my breast.” **Lump** means a raised or solid area you noticed; this report does not decide its cause. **Under the weather** is a broad conversational phrase for feeling unwell. For a clinical question, add concrete symptoms rather than relying on the idiom alone.

### Discuss examination and care

**Clinician:** I'd like to check your temperature and examine your shoulder.  
**Patient:** Please tell me before you touch the painful area.  
**Clinician:** Of course. Let me know what you feel.  
**Patient:** I will. Could I have an interpreter for the explanation afterward?  

A **checkup** is a health assessment. A **shot / injection** introduces a substance using a needle. **Referral** means directing you to another professional or service. **Tests** investigate a question; being referred for a test does not, by itself, name a diagnosis.

You may hear “I'd like to change the dressing” or “I'd like to order a blood test.” A **dressing** covers a wound. Ask what the proposed procedure involves rather than guessing.

### Understand the explanation and follow-up

**Patient:** What do you think is causing this?  
**Clinician:** We need to discuss what we know and what we still need to check.  
**Patient:** What are the next steps? Are there other options?  
**Clinician:** I'll explain the plan and give you written information.  
**Patient:** How will I get any test results, and who should I contact if things change?  

Questions about antibiotics, rest, food, fluids, or other care need answers for your situation. This chapter does not supply a diagnosis or a treatment recipe. “Do I need antibiotics?” is a question, not an assumption that they are needed.

For a repeat prescription: “I'm running low on my regular medicine. Can we discuss the refill?” For a flu vaccination: “I'd like to ask about getting a flu shot.” These are requests to discuss care, not eligibility claims.

### Confirm without pretending to understand

**Patient:** I want to make sure I understood. You want me to arrange the test, then contact this office for the results—is that right?  
**Clinician:** Let me clarify the sequence for your plan.  
**Patient:** Please write down who I should contact and when.  

You can be direct: “I don't understand that term.” “I still have a question.” You do not have to say everything is clear to end the visit politely.

## Practice

Fictional report: shoulder pain since Monday, worse when lifting the arm, cause unknown, medicine list available. No treatment or results plan has been selected.

1. Give the report. Partner asks where, when, and what changes the pain. Answer without inventing a diagnosis.
2. Partner proposes an examination. Ask what it involves and request that they tell you before touching the sore area.
3. Partner says a test may be needed. Ask why, how results will be communicated, and who to contact if the situation changes.
4. Try another role: a breast lump noticed yesterday. State the observation without naming its cause.

Solo: read the clinician's prompts and speak your patient turns; then summarize your main concern without the role card. Change one timing or symptom detail later.

**Possibilities:** “Since Monday, especially when I lift my arm.” “What are you checking with that test?” “I noticed a lump yesterday and want to have it assessed.” Check that the symptom, uncertainty, question, and unresolved next step remain clear.  

*Source note: Reporting and clarification principles informed by [NIH communication guidance](https://medlineplus.gov/talkingwithyourdoctor.html); all patients and exchanges are invented.*
