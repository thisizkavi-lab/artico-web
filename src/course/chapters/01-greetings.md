# CH01 — Greetings

**Your goal:** Open an interaction, answer a short check-in, and greet someone again after time apart.  

Choose one greeting and one check-in first. Add more choices when you want to express a different feeling or respond to a different situation. These are text-based speaking activities; no audio is supplied.

## Study: Start the conversation

| Expression | Meaning and a possible use |
| --- | --- |
| **Hi.** | A short greeting to open contact. |
| **Hello.** | A greeting that can also begin a more formal encounter; it is not formal-only. |
| **Hey.** | An informal greeting. Our first example uses it with someone you know. |
| **Good morning.** | A morning greeting. |

**At work, meeting a familiar colleague**

**Alex:** Good morning.  
**Casey:** Morning.  

**Morning** is a shortened greeting. You can also answer with the full **Good morning**. In the afternoon, use **Good afternoon**; in the evening, **Good evening**. To address a group, try **Hi, everyone** or **Good evening, everyone**. Group practice is optional.

Choose with the relationship, situation and feeling you want to convey in mind. A workplace does not automatically require one greeting. **Hey** can also attract attention, so the surrounding words and delivery matter. **Good night** usually closes contact or comes before sleep; Chapter 10 develops closings.

## Study: Check in and answer

**How are you?**, **How are you doing?** and **How's it going?** can be short social check-ins or genuine questions about someone's situation. What happens next helps show the intention.

| Reply | What it can communicate |
| --- | --- |
| **Good, thanks.** | A short positive answer. |
| **Pretty good.** | Fairly good. |
| **Not bad.** | A positive or reasonably satisfied answer, depending on delivery. |
| **I'm okay.** | You are all right, managing, or reassuring the other person. Context matters. |
| **Not great.** | Things are not going particularly well. |

This is not a fixed scale of emotions. **Fine, thanks** is another valid choice. Add **How about you?** to return the question. In a clear short exchange, **You?** can do the same job.

**Familiar acquaintances**

**Alex:** Hey. How's it going?  
**Casey:** Pretty good. You?  
**Alex:** Not bad.  

**A different answer**

**Alex:** Hello. How are you?  
**Casey:** Good, thanks. How about you?  
**Alex:** I'm okay.  

You do not have to say everything is good. **Not great** is a possible honest answer. Someone who stops and makes time for you may invite more explanation. You can also keep your answer short without sharing private details. This chapter practices the short response, rather than a complete supportive conversation.

## Study: Meet again

**Good to see you** expresses pleasure at seeing someone. **It's been a while** acknowledges time apart. **How have you been?** asks about the period since earlier contact. Learn it as a usable phrase; you do not need to explain present-perfect grammar to use it here.

**Two acquaintances reconnect**

**Alex:** Hi! Good to see you.  
**Casey:** You too. It's been a while.  
**Alex:** How have you been?  
**Casey:** I've been okay. How about you?  
**Alex:** I've been good.  

In this example, Casey gives a restrained account of that period. **I've been okay** can have other shades of meaning in a different situation. **How've you been?** is a contracted form of the question.

**Nice to meet you** is useful in first-meeting introductions, developed in Chapter 2. The reunion example above is a teaching focus, not a rule that **Good to see you** is impossible at a first encounter.

## Optional: Casual choices

**What's up?** can greet a friend:

**Alex:** Hey, what's up?  
**Casey:** Not much. You?  
**Alex:** Not much.  

Here **Not much** means there is little new to report. It is one possible reply. **What's up?** can also ask what is happening or what is wrong:

**Alex:** Hey, what's up?  
**Casey:** I lost my phone.  

The second scene is a real question about a problem. The phone sentence explains the example; you do not have to memorize it.

**Yo** also has an informal greeting use. Try **Yo — Hey, what's up?** in a familiar-friend scene if that style expresses what you want. It is not a greeting prescribed for every person or setting.

## Practice: Take your turn

Read the other person's line and speak your response. On your own, rehearse both roles. Look at Study when needed, then try again with the model hidden.

1. A colleague says **Good morning**. Return the greeting.
2. Open a check-in with someone you know. They answer **Pretty good. You?** Give a response that fits you or your chosen role.
3. Your role is having a difficult day and wants to say so briefly. Answer **How are you?**
4. An acquaintance says **Hi! Good to see you** after time apart. Respond and ask about that period. They answer **I've been okay. How about you?** Continue.
5. Greet an unfamiliar work contact in the afternoon, then a close colleague. Explain how your choices fit the relationship. The same greeting can work in both scenes.
6. You ask **How's it going?** The reply is **Not great**. Explain what you know and what you cannot know from this answer alone.
7. Optional: greet a friend with **Yo** or **Hey, what's up?** and rehearse a coherent response.
8. Optional: open an evening group meeting.

### Sample possibilities

- 1: **Morning** or **Good morning**.
- 2: **Hi. How are you?** followed by **Good, thanks**, **I'm okay**, or another fitting reply.
- 3: **Not great**. **I'm okay** might reassure someone instead; do not treat it as simply a weaker negative answer.
- 4: **You too. How have you been?** Then **I've been good** or **I've been okay**.
- 5: **Hello / Good afternoon / Hi**; with the colleague, **Hey / Hi / Good afternoon** are possibilities.
- 6: They report things are not going well. The reason is not given.
- 7: **Yo — Hey**; or **Hey, what's up? — Not much. You?**
- 8: **Good evening, everyone** or **Hello, everyone**.

Later, retry one exchange with a changed relationship or response. Check whether you can open contact, communicate your state, and make the reply fit. Exact imitation of one voice or accent is not the only goal. Audio-based repetition and shadowing will require actual recordings.
