# CH90 — Familiar Phone Calls

**Outcome:** Start a familiar call, check availability, coordinate a plan, and handle connection or battery problems.  

## Study

A familiar call can be warm, quick, serious, or inconvenient. You can check whether it is a good time before launching into a story: “Can you talk for a minute?” “Is now a bad time?” The other person can say yes, no, or offer a later time.

### Make contact and state why

**Caller:** Hey, it's Jamie. Got a minute?  
**Friend:** Yeah. What's up?  
**Caller:** I'm checking about tonight. Are we still meeting at six?  
**Friend:** Yes, at the cafe. I'm outside the library right now.  
**Caller:** Great. I'll see you at six, then.  

Here *What's up?* asks why the caller is calling as well as maintaining a familiar tone. “Good to hear from you” expresses pleasure at the contact. “You okay?” can be a genuine concern; if the call sounds worried, answer what is being asked rather than automatically using a routine check-in reply.

### Set a limit without inventing an excuse

**Caller:** Have you got time for a chat?  
**Friend:** Not right now. I'm in the middle of something.  
**Caller:** No problem. Would later work?  
**Friend:** I can call after seven.  
**Caller:** Okay. Message me if the plan changes.  

“I'm busy” is a direct explanation. “I can't talk now, but I'll call you back” offers a next step. “I don't have the energy for a long conversation tonight” can be appropriate with someone you know well. You do not have to explain everything or claim enthusiasm you do not feel.

Before speakerphone: “I'm going to put you on speaker—is that okay? Chris is here too.” This explains the proposed audience. The other person might reply “I'd rather speak privately.” This is a conversational arrangement, not a universal legal recording rule.

### Connection and device problems

**Caller:** Can you hear me?  
**Friend:** You're breaking up. I only caught the last part.  
**Caller:** I'll try again. Are we meeting at six?  
**Friend:** Yes, six. If the connection drops, text me.  

**Breaking up** here means the sound keeps being interrupted, not ending a relationship. **Drop** can describe a call ending unexpectedly. “My battery's at five percent” reports remaining charge. “My phone died” commonly means it lost power in this situation. “I'm out of mobile data” concerns data service; do not assume it explains every kind of calling or texting failure.

“My screen is frozen” means it is not responding. “I cracked the screen” reports damage. “I've forgotten my PIN” or “I'm locked out” reports an access problem. A **PIN** is a personal identification number used for access. Ask the relevant support provider about the next step rather than giving a friend an invented recovery procedure.

### Close clearly

**Caller:** I have to go—my bus is here.  
**Friend:** Okay. See you at six.  
**Caller:** See you then.  

“Catch you later” is a familiar closing. “I'll text you the address” adds an action; follow it with the address in a real exchange rather than leaving a plan incomplete.

## Practice

Fictional plan: cafe at six; friend can return a call after seven for a separate chat; battery at five percent; connection interrupts sound.

1. Call to confirm the cafe plan. Partner asks “What's up?” State your purpose and confirm the time.
2. Partner cannot chat now. Offer a later call or accept their after-seven suggestion. Do not confuse that call with the six-o'clock meeting.
3. Partner says you're breaking up. Restate the key time and arrange a text if the call drops.
4. End because your bus arrives. Keep the closing consistent with the confirmed plan.

Solo: read the friend's prompts and speak your caller turns, then rehearse the opposite role. Later change the availability rather than only the name.

**Possibilities:** “Just checking we're still on for six.” “After seven works for a call.” “If it drops, I'll text the details.” Check purpose, availability, plan, and backup communication. These written exercises do not test sound quality or listening ability.
