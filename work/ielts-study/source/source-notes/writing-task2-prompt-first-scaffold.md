## Introduction-building sequence

1. **Analyze the prompt before writing.** Separate the broad subject, the specific issue within it, and the instruction words that define the response. In the robot example, the broad area is technology, the focus is tasks moving from people to robots at home and work, and the prompt asks why this is happening and whether it is positive or negative. ([1:03–3:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=63s))
2. **Choose a position when the prompt calls for one.** Pick a position you can support with clear, relevant reasons. The instructor recommends choosing the side with ideas that are easiest to explain, rather than choosing a side first and struggling to defend it. Do not add an opinion if the task does not ask for one. ([4:10–6:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=250s))
3. **Generate and screen a small set of ideas.** Ask the prompt directly (“Why is this happening?”), use a plain-language reframe or a common-view prompt if helpful, and keep ideas that are clear, relevant, and developable. Build a short reason or example around the idea before committing to it. Avoid generating a large list that still needs to be sorted. ([6:07–14:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=367s); [idea-generation lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA))
4. **Make a brief plan.** Note the required answers, your position, and the few reasons the essay will develop. The video suggests that a clear introduction can help map the rest of the essay; use the notes as a flexible guide, not a substitute for planning the body when the prompt is complex. ([14:26–16:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=866s))
5. **Write the introduction only after that preparation.** For a double question, the lesson’s scaffold is: paraphrase the prompt accurately, briefly answer the first part, and briefly answer the second part. Adapt the content to the actual prompt type. Then use the “stranger test”: could a reader who sees the task and introduction understand the response and position? ([19:04–20:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1144s); [26:49–29:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1609s))
6. **Keep paraphrase accurate.** Change wording or sentence structure only when the new wording preserves meaning, fits the grammar, and suits the context. Do not replace a precise term just to avoid repeating it: the lesson contrasts *robots* with *robotics*, and *country* with *kingdom*. ([20:01–26:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1201s))

## Distilled routine

1. **Decode the task.** Mark the topic, scope, and each instruction that needs a response. Decide what position or answer the essay will give.
2. **Generate and screen main points.** Choose a manageable set of ideas that directly answers the task and that you can explain. Check that each idea is relevant, can be developed with a reason or example, and comes to mind quickly enough for timed writing. A straightforward point is useful when it fits those checks; complicated ideas are not automatically wrong. ([3:17–6:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=197s))
3. **Draft a clear opening.** Paraphrase the prompt only where you can keep its meaning accurate. State the answer or position clearly and preview the main reasons or views so the reader can follow the plan.
4. **Develop the body.** Give each paragraph a clear central point, explain how it supports the answer, and add a relevant example or detail. For a “discuss both views” question, cover both views and make the requested opinion clear. Adapt paragraphing and coverage to the actual prompt.
5. **Write a consistent conclusion.** Restate the answer and summarize the main reasoning already developed. Do not introduce a new argument that the essay has not supported.
6. **Practise the process.** First build the habit without a strict time limit. Then answer a range of prompts under progressively more realistic timing, including topics where the learner has little prior interest or knowledge. Review task coverage, relevance, development, organization, vocabulary, and grammar.

The video’s main point that relevance and clarity matter more than impressive-sounding ideas fits the Task Response criterion. Its point → explanation → example routine is a practical scaffold for supporting ideas, not a required formula. IELTS assesses how fully the response addresses the task and how relevant, extended, and supported the ideas are; it also assesses coherence and cohesion, lexical resource, and grammatical range and accuracy. A clear position alone is not enough for a high band. ([IELTS Writing Task 2 band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf); [IELTS Writing test preparation resources](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/writing-test-resources))

## Quick idea generation when a prompt feels unfamiliar

1. **Keep the prompt’s scope.** Underline the topic, the specific angle, and every instruction. Do not let an example inside the prompt replace the actual topic. In the video’s tourism prompt, the essay is about visiting places with difficult conditions; the Sahara is an example, not the whole subject. ([12:10–13:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=730s); [16:35–16:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=995s))
2. **Say the question in ordinary language.** The instructor’s “coffee shop” prompt is to imagine explaining the issue to a friend. Rephrasing “advantages and disadvantages” as “what is good and bad about this for the people involved?” may make the task easier to approach, provided the simplified version keeps the same scope and instructions. ([8:55–13:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=535s))
3. **Start with an ordinary, answerable point.** The “Family Fortunes” prompt asks what a typical member of the public might say first. Use it to get one accessible candidate idea, then check whether it actually answers this question. It is a prompt for generating ideas, not a test rule or a reason to choose an unsupported generalization. ([13:20–15:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=800s))
4. **Test each candidate before using it.** Ask: Does it answer the exact question? Can I explain why it matters and support it with a relevant example or evidence? Can I develop it without spending too long searching for an idea? A short, supported argument is more useful than a list of unrelated points. ([3:17–6:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=197s); [IELTS Writing Task 2 format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing))
5. **Practise the process under gradually increasing pressure.** The video describes anxiety as “test mode” and recommends normalizing discussion. Treat that as a teaching observation: rehearse with low-stakes spoken discussion, then rephrase and plan a prompt in timed writing. It can help learners practise the task routine, but the video does not establish a treatment or guarantee for test anxiety.

The speed check is a practical time-management aid, not a separate IELTS scoring criterion. The official Task Response criteria focus on answering the prompt and developing relevant ideas with support. ([IELTS Writing Task 2 band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

## Claims and teaching limits

- The instructor describes the method as universal and says it guarantees a high score and completion on time. Those outcomes are not guaranteed by a strategy. The student’s reported increase from Band 6.5 to 7.5 and the claim of a rapid score change are anecdotal and not independently verified.
- The source says unknown topics do not occur in real tests because the learner had seen topics in Cambridge practice books. That is not a safe universal assumption. Prepare learners to respond to unfamiliar prompts with a relevant, supportable idea rather than to depend on predicted content or assumed familiarity. The Task Response descriptor assesses relevance, extension, and support. This implies there is no separate specialist-knowledge criterion; a response still needs relevance and development.
- The instructor discourages brainstorming. That is his practice preference, not IELTS policy. Teach learners to generate ideas in whatever way helps them produce relevant, supportable points without losing time.
- The introduction video’s five stages and broad-topic → specific-focus → instruction model are teaching scaffolds, not prescribed IELTS steps. Its three-part introduction (paraphrase plus brief answers to two questions) applies to the demonstrated double-question prompt; adapt the introduction to the task rather than forcing every prompt into that formula. The suggested two-to-three sentence length is an example, not a word or sentence quota. ([0:00–0:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=0s), [19:04–19:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1144s))
- The video warns against hooks, quotations, proverbs, memorized chunks, and decorative vocabulary. Preserve the aim of a direct, relevant opening; do not claim that examiners literally “delete” these phrases or that introductions must use only simple language. Official criteria reward relevant task response and accurate, appropriate vocabulary, including range; they do not require a hook or a memorized phrase. Its statement that vocabulary is 25% of the “total score” is imprecise: the four criteria are equally weighted within each task, while Task 2 is weighted twice as heavily as Task 1. ([16:42–19:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1002s), [25:59–26:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=1559s); [official Writing scoring](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail); [Writing Band Descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))
- The instructor’s claim that one introduction system makes misunderstanding “pretty much impossible,” the survey-based stress statements, and the promise of finishing on time are not guarantees. The workbook, VIP service, and student outcomes are promotional material, not evidence that the method guarantees a band. ([3:33–4:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=213s), [7:28–8:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=448s), [15:04–15:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uyf17MMucCg&t=904s))
- The companion lesson says that most online IELTS questions are fake, that almost all reported test questions are inaccurate, and that its listed common topics recur frequently. It shows no dataset or method for these claims. Prefer authenticated sample material for exam-format practice, but do not assume every other practice question is false or use past-topic lists to predict a live test. Its topic list (education, technology, health, environment, and government) can be used as a general reading and discussion menu, not a forecast. ([0:23–2:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=23s))
- “Stop brainstorming” overstates the lesson’s useful point. A large, unfiltered idea list can waste time; a brief, structured brainstorm is fine if it produces a relevant point the learner can explain and support. The coffee-shop and Family Fortunes methods are optional classroom prompts, not exclusive techniques or official rules. ([6:28–8:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=388s), [13:20–15:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=800s))
- The claim that Task 2 topics are always simple enough for anyone with basic education, and that learners will have no problem understanding or generating ideas after reading news, is too broad. Prepare learners to handle unfamiliar topics by staying relevant and supporting their reasoning; this follows the Task Response requirements but does not promise familiarity. The video’s Cambridge 12 example and its “thousands of past questions” analysis are not independently verified. ([0:41–3:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E5S1wJJ9rSA&t=41s))
- The lesson frames an essay’s only purpose as transferring an opinion to the examiner. Keep its reminder to answer the question, but do not let that slogan erase the other assessed features: organization, cohesion, vocabulary, and grammar.
- Introductory paraphrase, two body points, a point → explanation → example paragraph, and a restated conclusion are useful beginner structures. They should be adapted to question requirements and learner needs rather than memorized as the only acceptable essay shape.
- Claims that examiners “have no choice” but to give a high score to a simple, clear essay are false as scoring guidance. The essay must satisfy all relevant criteria; no one feature guarantees a band.

## Learner example: plan ideas before drafting

The learner in the [Writing Academy interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-x6U2el8-Lk) says a previous course’s advice to generate two main ideas and two supporting points for each body paragraph left her struggling to produce enough material within 40 minutes. She reports that a prompt-first plan helped her finish and leave time to review: analyze the question, select relevant ideas, develop them with an explanation and example, then note any vocabulary that fits. She describes spending about eight minutes on planning; the instructor suggests five to ten minutes. Treat those timings as starting examples, not fixed requirements. Set the planning budget from the learner’s pace and protect enough drafting and checking time within the full test. Her later 8.5 result, preparation history, and claim that the promoted course caused her improvement are self-reported, and the interview does not verify a general score effect. ([3:00–5:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-x6U2el8-Lk&t=180s); [9:00–11:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-x6U2el8-Lk&t=540s))

## Course application

Use the opening as a short planning bridge: prompt → answer/position → one or two relevant main points. When idea generation stalls, rephrase the prompt in ordinary language, try a familiar everyday example, and test each point for relevance and support before drafting. Ask the learner to write a supported body paragraph from each selected point and compare the conclusion with the opening. After the scaffold is familiar, vary prompt types and subjects so learners practise adapting while maintaining task coverage and logical progression.
