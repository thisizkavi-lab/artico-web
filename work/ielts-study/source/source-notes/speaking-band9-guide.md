## Useful response routines

### Part 1: answer and add a little

Answer the question immediately, then add one relevant reason, detail, brief example, or personal experience when it helps. Stop when the question has been answered. Avoid a rehearsed personal speech, a long list of facts about the topic, and impressive-sounding material that does not answer the prompt. Practise with varied familiar questions so the learner can use flexible language rather than memorizing full responses. ([1:08–8:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DTyINOeY-Y&t=68s))

### Part 2: sustain a connected talk

Use the one-minute preparation period to identify a clear angle and jot down a sequence of supporting points. Stay anchored to the main topic, connect details into one talk, and keep speaking until the examiner stops you or time ends. Add a related memory, description, reason, feeling, or future plan when it naturally supports the topic. The cue-card points help organize the response; aim to cover them, but do not divide the two minutes into equal slots for every bullet. If a point feels difficult, make a brief relevant attempt and develop a connected detail instead of abandoning the topic. ([8:45–15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DTyINOeY-Y&t=525s))

### Part 3: answer, explain, support, extend

For a broader question, first give a clear answer or position. Explain why, support the reasoning with a relevant example or experience, and then add a qualification, alternative, or further implication when it develops the answer. If the topic is unfamiliar, do not go silent or pretend to have specialized facts: state what you can reasonably infer, qualify it, and build a general example. Ask the examiner to repeat or clarify a question if needed; do not rely on a promise that the examiner will switch topics. ([15:13–20:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DTyINOeY-Y&t=913s))

Across all three parts, practise natural, intelligible responses that stay relevant and show flexible language. Use the video’s sample answers as objects for noticing answer relevance, development, fluency, vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation—not as scripts to reproduce.

## Companion: common lower-band habits across the three parts

The second lesson contrasts rehearsed or overlong Part 1 answers, rigid cue-card recitation in Part 2, and underdeveloped or abandoned answers in Part 3 with more responsive language use. Convert that contrast into a practice model rather than promising that these habits identify a candidate's band. ([0:00–5:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8inpUXQPI8&t=0s), [5:40–10:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8inpUXQPI8&t=340s), [11:00–17:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8inpUXQPI8&t=660s))

- **Part 1:** discourage prepared monologues, lists of unrelated facts, and needlessly formal delivery. Answer the question first, then add a useful reason, detail, or example and stop when the answer is complete. Familiar topic types are useful for practice, but exact questions are not guaranteed; practise flexible responses rather than scripts.
- **Part 2:** avoid turning the four prompts into four disconnected mini-answers or imposing a past → present → future formula. Pick a clear angle, plan a sequence, and use the task-card points to organise and develop one coherent talk. Do not teach the companion's suggestion that points can simply be ignored: the official task format says the card indicates points candidates should include. There is no need to give every point equal time. ([IELTS Part 2 format](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking); [one-minute preparation guidance](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-use-the-preparation-time-in-ielts-speaking-part-2))
- **Part 3:** prepare learners to handle a wider range of ideas by giving a direct response, explaining the reasoning, and adding a relevant example, qualification, or implication. If knowledge is limited, give a clearly framed general or hypothetical view instead of going silent or bluffing facts. The video calls unexpected topics an IELTS “trick”; official format guidance describes Part 3 as a more general, abstract discussion related to the Part 2 topic, not a trap or a signal of the examiner's private band estimate. ([IELTS Part 3 format](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Vocabulary and grammar:** replace memorized lists of idioms or set phrases with learning that supports flexible, accurate use. Learners do not need to sound like native speakers; IELTS assesses communication through four equally weighted criteria, including intelligibility, vocabulary, and grammar. ([IELTS Speaking criteria](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail))

IELTS also advises against over-rehearsed responses because they may not show a candidate's real ability to communicate. The companion video's contrast between Bands 5–6.5 and 7–9 is a teaching illustration, not a diagnostic rubric: score judgments must use the official criteria across the whole performance. ([IELTS guidance on memorized answers](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/dont-overdo-it-how-to-ace-your-ielts-speaking-test))

## Companion: avoid “test mode” and build responsive answers

The 24-minute [Get Band 9 After Using These Speaking Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Emd65BXMS9M) calls it “test mode” when a learner replaces ordinary conversation with anxious self-monitoring: counting words or sentences, worrying about answer length, or trying to perform an impressive memorized response. Its most useful Part 1 routine is simple: answer the exact question, then add a relevant reason, detail, example, or experience if it helps. Do not set a sentence quota. An interruption by the examiner can be ordinary turn management in a short interview; do not train learners to read it as a mark of failure. ([0:00–10:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Emd65BXMS9M&t=0s))

The video applies flexible development to Part 2 and Part 3. For Part 2, stay with the central topic and build a connected talk rather than reciting four disconnected bullets; its advice to use only cue-card points that feel comfortable is corrected above, because official guidance says the points identify what to include. For Part 3, answer directly, explain why, add a relevant example, and extend the reasoning with another implication or qualification when it helps. If the topic is unfamiliar, acknowledge that briefly and offer a reasonable general response rather than going silent. These are optional supports for relevance and development, not fixed response formulas. ([10:20–16:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Emd65BXMS9M&t=620s); [16:50–24:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Emd65BXMS9M&t=1010s))

Do not repeat this lesson’s claims that memorizing is cheating or can automatically earn Band 0, that a hard Part 3 question signals a high examiner estimate, or that the examiner is likely to switch to another topic after a candidate admits limited knowledge. Those claims are unsupported as test policy. Assess performance against the four official criteria rather than inferring a band from a demonstration label. A relevant response gives the examiner language to assess; silence does not, while a longer answer is not automatically stronger. The teacher’s band labels, guarantees, score changes, and promotional claims are not independent evidence. ## Companion: keep language controlled and task-appropriate

The 18-minute [Get BAND 9 Using This IELTS Speaking Strategy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7lqSx_ucv4A) reinforces direct Part 1 answers with a small amount of relevant detail, more developed Part 3 responses, natural pacing, and vocabulary chosen for the topic. Its statement that Part 1 questions cannot be answered “wrong” because they are about the candidate should mean that the test is not checking factual knowledge; relevance, coherence, and language performance still matter. The video’s “100% of words must be understood” threshold is not an official score rule: the Band 8 descriptor says a speaker can be easily understood throughout and use a wide range of phonological features; it does not set a percentage. ([6:50–10:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7lqSx_ucv4A&t=410s); [official Speaking band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf))

Use simple, accurate language when it expresses the idea well, but do not teach a “simple only” strategy or a fixed 95–99% simple-word ratio. The video's recommendation to ignore bullets is not adopted. The speaker says he took IELTS and received Band 9, but the self-reported result is not independently verified. Its “the less you try, the higher your score” promise, vocabulary ratios, and the claimed analysis behind a high-scoring-speaker word list are not independently inspectable. Do not teach deliberate mistakes, too little preparation, or an exact vocabulary balance as a route to a band score. ## Applied mock test: reduce word-search friction

[IELTS Speaking: Why Perfect English Hurts Your Score](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5zmVCfCb7o) records a learner answering Parts 1–3, receiving feedback, and trying a second round. The useful diagnosis is specific: the learner is fluent in ordinary conversation but sometimes stops to find a more exact or sophisticated word, second-guesses familiar ideas, and becomes less fluent under the performance pressure of the test. The title's broad claim is not supported; strong English does not itself lower a score. The case illustrates how over-monitoring can interrupt an individual learner's fluency. ([0:00–5:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5zmVCfCb7o&t=0s), [10:45–13:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5zmVCfCb7o&t=645s))

- **Paraphrase instead of freezing.** When an exact word does not arrive, preserve the meaning with a simpler word, a short explanation, or an example, then continue. This is a useful repair for fluency and uses the paraphrasing ability named in the official lexical-resource guidance; it is not a reason to avoid learning precise vocabulary. ([20:00–22:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5zmVCfCb7o&t=1200s); [IELTS Speaking format and criteria](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Choose an easy, relevant example.** For a broad personal topic, select a genuine experience or a simple position that can be explained clearly. The test is not an entertainment contest: relevance and coherent development matter more than an elaborate story. In Part 2, the example still needs to answer the cue-card topic and points.
- **Keep the conversation frame as an optional calming exercise.** Rehearse answering a mock examiner in a relaxed, cooperative way if that reduces self-monitoring. This is a learner-specific practice aid, not official scoring guidance; body language and the examiner's friendliness are not separate assessment criteria.
- **If a Part 3 topic is unfamiliar, acknowledge the limit and still respond.** Offer a reasonable general view, explanation, or qualified example. Saying only “I don't know” and stopping gives little language for the examiner to assess. The video suggests that admitting unfamiliarity may lead to a different question; examiner redirection is not guaranteed, so prepare to continue with the prompt. ([18:00–23:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5zmVCfCb7o&t=1080s); [IELTS Speaking format](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))

The instructor calls the learner's pronunciation, grammar, and vocabulary perfect, predicts a Band 9, and later says the learner is almost guaranteed that result. These are the instructor's judgments in a promotional video, not a reported official IELTS score. Use the sample to discuss a concrete fluency repair, not as proof that a speaking routine guarantees Band 9. IELTS assesses the whole performance against four criteria, each carrying equal weight. ([IELTS scoring in detail](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail))

## Official criteria and corrections

IELTS Speaking is a structured, recorded interview with three parts. Examiners assess Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation across the performance. A “good first impression,” eye contact, or the examiner’s friendliness is not a separate scoring criterion. ([IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking); [Speaking band descriptors](https://cdn.ielts.org/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf))

- **Memorized responses:** IELTS warns that rehearsed answers may fail to show the candidate’s real speaking ability and can affect the score. Teach flexible preparation and practice, not scripted answers. The video’s claim that memorization is “cheating” or can automatically earn zero is too absolute; do not repeat it as policy. ([IELTS guidance on memorized answers](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/dont-overdo-it-how-to-ace-your-ielts-speaking-test))
- **Part 2 bullets:** the cue card tells candidates which points to include. Teach learners to address the points and develop them with relevant detail; there is no need to spend equal time on each. The video’s advice that stronger candidates can simply skip bullets is not adopted. ([IELTS Speaking format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-speaking))
- **Difficult Part 3 questions:** the instructor says a difficult question is a positive signal that the examiner thinks the candidate may be Band 7–9. The video provides no evidence for this. Question difficulty alone does not establish the examiner’s view or the candidate’s score.
- **Unfamiliar topics:** IELTS guidance says the examiner is assessing language, not the candidate’s opinion or specialist knowledge. Candidates may give a reasonable general or hypothetical response; there is no guaranteed examiner redirection. ([IELTS Speaking myths and difficult questions](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/ielts-speaking-tips-common-myths-and-how-to-overcome-them))
- **No fixed answer length:** short answers may limit evidence of a candidate’s ability, but do not diagnose every short answer as fatigue or prescribe a word count. Match development to the question and the part of the test.
- The instructor’s students, band examples, pass-rate framing, and “guaranteed” results are not independently verified. The closing invitation to a free question bank also promotes the creator’s materials; the instructional methods stand on their own. ([0:00–0:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DTyINOeY-Y&t=0s), [20:46–21:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DTyINOeY-Y&t=1246s))

## Companion: choose an opening that fits the question

The 15-minute [How to Start IELTS Speaking Answers Like a Band 9 Student](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WP2LcgMq-_8) quizzes learners on natural ways to begin answers: direct preference or opinion phrases; a recall phrase for past experience; *would* for hypothetical questions; *it depends* when the answer genuinely varies by person or context; a simple direct reply for familiar Part 1 questions; a brief bridge into a developed Part 3 explanation; and a short lead-in before a personal example. For unfamiliar questions, try a reasonable, qualified response instead of going silent. The useful principle is **match the first clause to the question’s function, answer promptly, then develop only as needed**. ## Companion: three flexible answer-building frameworks

The 19-minute [The 3 Speaking Tricks That Get You Band 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o) demonstrates three frameworks. **PEL** begins with a point of view, elaborates it in whichever way best helps the listener understand (reason, explanation, example, experience), then adds a further detail, contrast, or consequence if useful. **1-2-3** suits a genuinely two-sided question: acknowledge the issue, explain the relevant sides, then give a context-specific position or qualification. **AAA** means answer the question, add helpful detail (such as who, what, when, where, or why), and add a relevant anecdote or example when useful. Treat these as optional planning supports; do not force a two-sided response or story into every answer, and let the prompt and test part determine appropriate length. ([1:00–6:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o&t=60s); [7:00–12:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o&t=420s); [13:00–17:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o&t=780s))

These frameworks overlap the answer → explain → support methods above and the Speaking course's Part 1/2/3 routines. Use them to reduce idea overload and keep the answer relevant, not to memorize sentence scripts. If a story is invented for practice, label it as hypothetical rather than presenting false autobiography; the transcript's idea that an anecdote can come from a story heard elsewhere should not become a reason to fabricate personal history. The video promises five frameworks but only explains PEL, 1-2-3, and AAA in the reviewed transcript; it mentions two bonus frameworks in a PDF without naming or teaching them, so this note does not infer their content. The claimed student results, “Band 9” guarantee, and promotional PDF/course claims are unverified. ([0:00–1:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o&t=0s); [17:50–18:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ii2fVU4CJ9o&t=1070s))


## Companion: use ChatGPT for practice, not score certification

In the 16-minute [Can ChatGPT Get A Band 9?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4uRhRB1ENUs), the model produces sample answers, conducts a mock Speaking sequence, and comments on the instructor's performance. This demonstration is not a benchmark of every current AI system, but it is strong reason to treat model-generated bands as uncalibrated opinions rather than assessment. ([0:00–3:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4uRhRB1ENUs&t=0s); [4:00–7:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4uRhRB1ENUs&t=240s); [8:00–15:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4uRhRB1ENUs&t=480s))

For course practice, AI can provide varied prompts, ask them sequentially, role-play a conversation, or suggest one area to review from a learner's recorded answer. Require evidence from the response, check whether the feedback matches the official criteria, and get calibrated human review for important score or readiness decisions. Keep generated examples appropriately short for Part 1, natural, and free of invented personal details. IELTS reports a Speaking score based on the average of four equally weighted criteria; the public descriptors use whole-band criterion descriptions, so the model's 8.5 sub-scores are not an official criterion scale. ([IELTS scoring in detail](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail); [IELTS Speaking band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf))
