# Why Language?

## Start from nothing

Before asking why English matters, we should ask a more basic question: why does language matter at all?

From a cold physical point of view, speech is strange. A human pushes air out of the lungs. The throat vibrates. The tongue and lips change the shape of the air. Another person receives those vibrations through the ear, and something happens inside the mind.

The sound itself contains no natural meaning. There is nothing inside the sound *water* that is wet. Japanese uses *mizu*. Other languages use other sounds. The connection between the noise and the thing is not written into the universe. People made it, inherited it, and continue to use it together.

From this distance, language can look absurd: temporary creatures making arbitrary noises at one another on a small planet.

And English is not metaphysically special. It is not wiser, more beautiful, or more human than Japanese—or any other language. If you have no reason to use English, learning it is not a moral duty. You are allowed not to care.

> [!abstract] Zoom out
> There may be no cosmic command telling you to learn a language. That does not mean there can be no human reason.

## You are alive among other people

We do not begin life alone, and almost nothing we do remains completely private.

Food is grown, transported, sold, and prepared by people. Homes are designed and built by people. Work is done with people or for people. Knowledge arrives from people who lived before us. Even when you sit alone and read, another mind has left marks for you across distance and time.

Our lives move through networks of other lives. We depend on them, resist them, cooperate with them, misunderstand them, love them, and sometimes need to ask them for help.

Communication is therefore not decoration. It is one of the ways a private life becomes able to act in a shared world.

Language is not the only form of communication. A face, a gesture, a drawing, music, touch, silence, and action can all carry meaning. But human language has an unusual power: with a limited set of sounds and signs, we can create an open-ended number of messages. We can speak about yesterday, next year, another country, an imaginary world, a promise, a law, a joke, or a person who is no longer alive. We can even use language to question language.

That is what is special—not the noise by itself, but what people can build with it together.

## The distance between two minds

Every person experiences life from inside one body and one point of view. You cannot directly enter another person’s pain, memory, fear, or hope. They cannot directly enter yours.

Language does not remove this distance. Words are imperfect. A sentence can be misunderstood. A feeling can be larger than the vocabulary available to describe it. Translation can preserve one part of an idea and lose another.

Still, language gives us a bridge that can be repaired while we are crossing it.

We can say: “That is not quite what I meant.” We can ask: “Do you mean this?” We can try another example, change our tone, tell a story, or begin again. Communication is not the perfect transfer of a thought. It is the joint work of moving closer to understanding.

> [!example] Zoom in
> A word has no meaning because of its sound alone. It has meaning because people use it in a situation, expect something from it, and respond to it.

This is why language is more than vocabulary. Meaning also lives in timing, relationship, tone, rhythm, shared history, and what is left unsaid.

## Language creates a shared world

Human beings do more than exchange information. We coordinate.

We make plans with people who are not present. We preserve instructions for people who are not yet born. We build schools, companies, communities, laws, rituals, scientific disciplines, and stories that outlive any one speaker.

Many things that organise human life are not physical objects. A promise cannot be placed on a table. Neither can ownership, a job title, a deadline, a reputation, or trust. Yet these things have real consequences because people recognise them and act around them.

Language helps make that recognition possible. It turns private intention into something other people can answer.

The meaning is not hidden inside the sound. The meaning exists in the relationship between a speaker, a listener, a situation, and a history of use.

## Why learn another language?

Your first language already gives you an enormous world. Learning another language does not make you more intelligent or more valuable as a human being.

It changes your range.

A new language lets you address people who were previously outside your reach. It lets you receive humor, emotion, disagreement, and personality without asking translation to carry everything. It gives you access to another set of habits for showing respect, building closeness, organising a story, or making a request.

It can also loosen the idea that your usual way of saying something is the only natural way. The new language does not replace the first self. It gives that self another set of movements.

But this is a possibility, not an obligation. The honest question is not, “Should everyone learn English?” The honest question is:

> Is there a person, place, idea, opportunity, or future that becomes more reachable if I learn it?

If the answer is no, English may not deserve your time. If the answer is yes, the reason belongs to you.

## Why English?

English is important today for historical and practical reasons, not because it is inherently superior.

Its global reach grew through British imperial power and trade, then through the economic, political, scientific, technological, and cultural influence of the United States and other English-speaking institutions. Once many people use one language across borders, more people learn it because other people already use it. The network reinforces itself.

That history contains invention, exchange, inequality, coercion, and accident. We do not need to turn it into a heroic story to recognise the present result: English is now widely used as a shared language among people who often do not speak it as their first language.

It appears in international work, research, software, aviation, travel, entertainment, online communities, and many of the tools shaping contemporary life. English is not the world. But it is one of the largest doors currently available between different parts of the world.

Learning it does not require believing that English culture is better. It means deciding that access to this network is useful to you.

## The Japanese learner and the weight of English

In Japan, English can carry too much symbolic weight.

It may represent school ranking, entrance examinations, intelligence, international status, career value, or the image of a person who is somehow more sophisticated. When a language is placed on a pedestal, every mistake can feel like evidence that the learner does not belong there.

But speaking is not an examination conducted in silence. It happens in time, with another person. The speaker listens, predicts, responds, hesitates, repairs, changes direction, and continues.

Grammar and literacy matter. Reading and writing are also forms of communication, not dead or lesser skills. But knowing rules is not the same as being able to participate in a live exchange. Real-time conversation adds timing, sound, memory, emotion, and social judgment.

English therefore needs to come down from the pedestal and enter the body. It must become something the ear can recognise, the mouth can attempt, and the person can use before it is perfect.

> [!warning] Watch this
> Difficulty is not proof that English belongs only to “smart” people. It often means that knowledge, listening, mouth movement, timing, and confidence have not yet been trained together.

## From nihilism to chosen meaning

Suppose the universe gives us no final instruction. Suppose no language matters from the point of view of distant stars.

Then meaning is not something we passively discover already completed. It is something living beings create through attention, choice, responsibility, and relationship.

A conversation can matter because two people make it matter. A promise can matter because someone keeps it. A sentence can change a life because it reaches the right person at the right time.

Language begins as air and vibration. Human beings turn it into warning, invitation, memory, tenderness, instruction, protest, humor, and art.

The absence of cosmic meaning does not erase these human meanings. It makes us responsible for them.

## Artico’s answer

So what is the point of learning English?

There is no single answer for everyone.

For one person, it is work. For another, travel. For another, friendship, love, art, technology, study, migration, independence, or simple curiosity. The deepest reason may be freedom: not freedom from every limitation, but a larger ability to understand, respond, choose, and connect.

Artico does not treat English as proof of intelligence. It treats language as an embodied, cognitive, and social practice.

We will explain the big picture and the small details. We will study why an expression works, where it belongs, and what deserves attention. Then we will listen, imitate, repeat, change it, and use it. Theory gives direction. Practice gives the language a body.

The first goal is not perfection or a borrowed personality. It is social fluency: the ability to enter an interaction, understand enough, express yourself, repair a problem, and remain connected.

Your personal voice comes later—and it cannot be handed to you by a teacher. First learn the shared forms well enough to participate. Then bend them, combine them, and make them yours.

> [!important] The point
> English has no sacred value. Your life has no obligation to revolve around it. But if English connects you to a part of life you want to reach, learning it becomes a practical act of freedom.

## Where we go next

Now that we have asked why language matters, we can ask how it is learned.

We will begin by removing one misunderstanding: a language is not learned only by collecting information about it. Understanding matters, but understanding must eventually become perception, movement, timing, and action.

That is the path from theory to sound, from sound to practice, and from practice to another person.
