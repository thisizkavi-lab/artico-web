# CH25 — Cooking and Eating

**Your goal:** Follow and clarify cooking steps, share tasks, and talk with a host about food and preferences.  

## Study: Understand the action

Cooking instructions often begin with the action: **Chop the onion**, **Add the oil**, **Stir the sauce**. You do not need a separate subject in each instruction.

| Action | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| Peel | Remove an outer skin or covering |
| Slice / chop / dice | Cut into slices / pieces / small cubes |
| Grate | Rub against a grater to make small shreds or pieces |
| Weigh / measure | Find an amount by weight / use a stated quantity |
| Add / pour | Put in another ingredient / make liquid flow into a container |
| Mix / stir / whisk | Combine ingredients / move a spoon or similar tool through them / beat or mix with a whisk |

**A tablespoon** and **a teaspoon** name measured quantities as well as spoon types. **Two tablespoons of oil** specifies an amount. **A cup of tomatoes** uses a measuring unit; it does not mean any drinking cup filled any way. If the recipe is unclear, ask **Which size spoon do you mean?** or **Do you mean a measuring cup?**

For heat instructions, **preheat the oven** means bring it to the stated temperature before putting the food in. **Boil** and **simmer** are different: boiling has vigorous bubbling; simmering is gentler cooking in liquid below a full boil. **Turn the heat down** asks you to reduce it. **Set a timer for ten minutes** gives a duration; **Check it in ten minutes** gives a time to look, not a guarantee that it will be ready.

Other methods from Chapter 20 include **fry** in hot fat, **grill** using direct heat, **bake** in an oven, and **roast** using dry heat, often in an oven. These words describe a method; the food and recipe supply the temperature, duration and other details.

## Study: Follow a short recipe together

This simple practice recipe gives all the amounts and steps needed for the exchange. No heating is involved.

**Tomato-and-cucumber salad — two servings**

- Two tomatoes and one cucumber
- Two tablespoons of olive oil
- One tablespoon of lemon juice
- One small pinch of salt, if wanted

**Steps**

1. Wash the tomatoes and cucumber, then chop them into small pieces.
2. Put the oil and lemon juice in a small bowl. Whisk them together.
3. Put the vegetables in a larger bowl. Pour the dressing over them and mix.
4. Add the optional salt, taste, and serve.

**Cook:** Could you chop the tomatoes and cucumber while I make the dressing?  
**Helper:** Sure. How small do you want the pieces?  
**Cook:** Small enough to eat in one bite.  
**Helper:** Okay. Does the lemon juice go in with the oil?  
**Cook:** Yes. Then whisk them together.  
**Helper:** Got it.  

**While** puts two actions at the same time. **Then** puts the next action afterward. **Before** and **after** change the order: **Add the dressing after chopping the vegetables**.

If you lose your place, ask **What's next?** If you did something different, say **I added the lemon juice to the vegetables already. What should we do now?** For this practice salad, the cook answers **That's okay. Add the oil and mix everything together**. That response concerns this recipe, not every cooking mistake.

## Study: Share the work

**Who's cooking tonight?** opens a planning conversation. **It's my turn**, **I'll make the salad**, **Could you set the table?**, and **Can you handle the dishes afterward?** divide tasks.

**Housemate:** I'll make dinner. Can you set the table?  
**You:** Yes, but I can't do the dishes tonight. I'm leaving at seven.  
**Housemate:** Okay. I'll do those.  
**You:** Thanks. I'll put the plates and forks out now.  

**Handle** here means take responsibility for a task. Saying what you cannot do makes the arrangement clear; it is not an acceptance of every job.

## Study: Eat with other people

**Host:** Is there anything you don't eat?  
**Guest:** I don't eat pork. Also, I don't like bell peppers.  
**Host:** Thanks for telling me. I'll explain what's in the food.  

**I don't eat…** states a restriction without requiring a reason. **I don't like…** states a preference. **I'm allergic to shellfish** states an allergy and calls for ingredient and preparation questions, as practiced in Chapter 24. Do not replace it with **I don't really like shellfish** if the intended meaning is allergy.

Useful labels include **vegetarian**, **vegan**, and **pescatarian**—someone whose diet includes fish but not meat from other animals. Ask about the specific ingredients rather than infer every choice from a label.

**Help yourself** invites guests to take food. **Would you like some more?** offers another portion. Accept with **Yes, a little more, please** or decline with **No, thanks. I'm full**. **This is delicious** and familiar **This is really yummy** express enjoyment. You can also say **It's a bit too spicy for me** without pretending you enjoyed the same thing as everyone else.

## Practice: Prepare, cooperate, and respond

Speak both roles alone. Use the salad recipe and housemate scene above.

1. Give the recipe's four steps using **first**, **then**, and **after that**. The helper asks where the lemon juice goes; answer from the recipe.
2. Divide the salad work: one person chops, and the other makes the dressing. Ask how small the pieces should be.
3. Change a detail: the lemon juice is already on the vegetables. Report it and give the supplied adjustment for this salad.
4. You can set the table but are leaving at seven. Agree to that task and explain why you cannot do the dishes. The cook takes responsibility for them.
5. As host, ask about food restrictions. As guest, state a pork restriction and a bell-pepper dislike. Change the intention: state a shellfish allergy and ask about ingredients and preparation.
6. Hide the models. Explain the recipe without reading its sentences, assign two tasks, then accept or decline an extra portion. Repeat with a different response to the food.

### Sample possibilities

1. **First, wash and chop the vegetables. Then whisk the oil and lemon juice together. After that, mix the dressing with the vegetables. Finally, add salt if wanted, taste, and serve.**
2. **Could you chop the vegetables while I make the dressing? — How small? — Small enough for one bite.**
3. **I already added the lemon juice. — Add the oil and mix everything together.**
4. **I can set the table, but I can't do the dishes. I'm leaving at seven.**
5. **I don't eat pork, and I don't like bell peppers.** For the changed need: **I'm allergic to shellfish. Can you check the ingredients and how the food is prepared?**
6. **A little more, please** or **No, thanks. I'm full**.

**Check:** Were the amount and action clear? Did your sequence match the recipe? Did each person know which task they accepted? Did a restriction, preference and allergy keep their different meanings?
