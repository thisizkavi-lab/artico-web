# CH71 — At the Auto Repair Shop

**Outcome:** Schedule service, describe a car problem, ask for an explanation, and agree on the next step.  

## Study

A **repair shop** works on vehicles. **Maintenance** is work intended to keep something functioning; a **repair** addresses a problem. An **estimate** is a proposed cost, not the same word as the final bill. This lesson teaches communication with a professional, not diagnosis or repair instructions.

### Schedule and describe

**Customer:** I'd like to schedule maintenance for my car.  
**Employee:** What work are you looking for?  
**Customer:** An oil change. I'd also like someone to check a noise.  
**Employee:** When do you hear it?  
**Customer:** When I turn left. It started yesterday.  
**Employee:** We have an opening Thursday at nine.  
**Customer:** That works. About when could I pick the car up?  
**Employee:** We can estimate the pickup time after the inspection.  

Use “The engine sounds different,” “The warning light keeps flashing,” or “The car won't start.” These describe observations. “I think the engine is overheating” states a suspicion. “I don't know what the light means” is a useful admission, not a failure.

### Parts for explaining a fault

**Windshield** is the front glass; **hood** covers the engine area; **trunk** is the rear storage area. **Steering wheel** is the control you turn to steer. **Headlight**, **bumper**, **engine**, **radiator**, **wheel**, and **tire** are useful labels. You do not need to know every part to report a problem.

Examples: “There's a crack in the windshield.” “The left headlight isn't working.” “The tire keeps losing air.” “The steering wheel is hard to turn.” Describe what happens rather than silently choosing a cause.

### Understand the proposed work

Use this invented shop quote after the inspection. The originally requested oil change and noise check cost **$80: $30 for parts and $50 for labor**. The turning noise has not yet been explained. The mechanic separately reports that the left headlight bulb is not working and proposes replacing it for **$35: $15 for the bulb and $20 for labor**. The quoted total with that additional work is **$115**. These amounts are scene facts, not typical prices.

**Mechanic:** We recommend replacing the left headlight bulb. This is separate from checking the turning noise.  
**Customer:** Could you put that in plain language?  
**Mechanic:** We'd replace the bulb with a new one. We haven't identified the cause of the noise yet.  
**Customer:** What's included in the estimate? Are parts and labor separate?  
**Mechanic:** The original work is eighty dollars. The bulb replacement adds thirty-five: fifteen for the bulb and twenty for labor.  
**Customer:** So the total would be a hundred fifteen?  
**Mechanic:** Yes, for the work listed here.  
**Customer:** Okay. I agree to that work for a hundred fifteen. Please call me before doing anything else.  

You can also say “Please do only the original work for eighty dollars” or “I'd like to review this before agreeing.” The mechanic can answer “Okay, I'll record that decision.” Asking about a proposal does not itself authorize it.

**Parts** are the physical components; **labor** is the work. “What happens if I wait?” asks the professional to explain consequences; this book supplies no technical answer. “Is the car safe to drive?” asks for professional advice rather than presuming safety.

### Respond to an unexpected bill

For this checkout scene, the customer approved the listed **$115** of work and no extras. The bill is **$125**: the listed work plus a **$10 cleaner charge** that was not agreed to.

**Customer:** The bill is a hundred twenty-five, but I approved a hundred fifteen. Could you explain the extra ten dollars?  
**Employee:** That's a cleaner charge added to the bill.  
**Customer:** I didn't agree to that charge. Could we review it with the manager?  
**Employee:** I'll ask the manager to review the charge with you.  

This reports the customer's position; it does not establish a legal entitlement. Softer “Could we go through this together?” and firmer “I need an explanation before we settle this” express different intentions.

## Practice

Use the fictional appointment facts: an oil change requested; noise when turning left, starting yesterday; Thursday at nine available. For the later quote and bill scenes, use the amounts and work details above. The checkout scene assumes the $115 approval regardless of the choice you make while practicing the quote scene.

1. Book the appointment. Partner asks about the noise and offers the time. Give the observed facts and ask about pickup.
2. Partner proposes the left headlight bulb replacement and supplies the $80 original work, $35 addition, and $115 total. Ask what the job is and how parts and labor are charged. Choose to approve it, keep only the original work, or review before deciding. Partner records that choice; no explanation of the turning noise is available yet.
3. Use the checkout case: you approved $115, but the bill is $125. Ask about the $10 difference. Partner identifies the unapproved cleaner charge and offers manager review. State your position and ask for the next step. Do not invent a technical cause, refund or legal guarantee.

Solo: rehearse both roles and hide your customer lines. Later substitute a cracked windshield or a tire losing air.

**Possibilities:** “It started yesterday. I hear it when I turn left.” “Does the thirty-five include both the bulb and labor?” “Please do only the original work for eighty dollars.” “I approved a hundred fifteen, not a hundred twenty-five. What's the extra ten dollars for?” Check that the employee understands the symptom, scope of permission, and question needing an answer.
