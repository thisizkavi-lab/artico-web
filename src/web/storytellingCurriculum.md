# SPOKEN STORYTELLING
## A practical course in finding, shaping, and telling stories aloud

**Course type:** Self-study course  
**Main skill:** Spoken storytelling in everyday English  
**Level:** Beginner to advanced  
**Format:** Learn a concept, study an example, practise it, and use it in a real situation  
**Course principle:** Clear communication is more important than dramatic performance.

---

# HOW TO USE THIS COURSE

This course teaches you how to tell stories aloud. It does not train you to become a novelist, screenwriter, actor, or stand-up comedian. You can learn from those fields, but the main goal is different:

**You should be able to tell another person what happened in a way that is clear, natural, and worth listening to.**

You will practise stories for:

- A conversation with a friend.
- A new social situation.
- A class or meeting.
- A presentation.
- An interview.
- An explanation.
- A personal account.
- An informal speech.
- A situation in which you must speak without preparation.

You do not need an unusual life. You do not need to be funny. You do not need to use a special voice. You need to notice events, understand what matters, organize your thoughts, and express them clearly.

## The learning cycle

Use this cycle in every lesson:

1. **Understand:** Learn one skill.
2. **Observe:** Find the skill in an example.
3. **Practise:** Use the skill in a short exercise.
4. **Apply:** Use it in a real conversation or recording.
5. **Review:** Identify one thing to keep and one thing to improve.

Do not try to improve every part of your storytelling at the same time. Focus on one skill in each practice session.

## Course outcomes

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

1. Explain what a story is, name different ways to tell stories, and know when storytelling is useful.
2. Find story material in ordinary experiences.
3. Identify the main point of a story.
4. Choose details that help the listener understand.
5. Organize events in a clear order.
6. Create interest without adding false drama.
7. Begin and end a story effectively.
8. Speak in a natural and understandable way.
9. Adjust a story for different listeners and time limits.
10. Tell a short story without a full script.
11. Use stories to support an explanation or presentation.
12. Review your own performance and improve it.

## A note about the word "good"

A good story is not always exciting. A good story is suitable for its purpose and audience.

A quiet story can be effective. A funny story can be confusing. A dramatic story can be dishonest. A simple story can communicate an important idea.

In this course, we judge a story by its clarity, relevance, structure, delivery, and effect on the listener. We do not judge it by how loud, emotional, or theatrical the speaker is.

# PART 1 — FOUNDATIONS

# LESSON 1 — WHAT IS STORYTELLING?

## Learning goal

Understand what a story is, how people can tell stories in different ways, and why this course focuses on spoken storytelling.

## 1.1 A simple definition

A **story** is an account of connected events in which something happens, changes, or is revealed.

**Storytelling** is the act of selecting, arranging, and presenting those events so that another person can understand what happened and why it matters.

This definition has three important parts:

- **Selecting:** You choose which events and details to include.
- **Arranging:** You decide the order in which the listener receives the information.
- **Presenting:** You use words, voice, timing, and expression to communicate the account.

You do not tell every detail of an experience. You choose the details that help the listener follow the event.

## 1.2 A story is more than a list of events

Consider these two accounts.

**Account A**

> Yesterday I went to the station. I bought a ticket. I got on the train. I arrived at work.

This is a list of events. It has a time order, but there is no clear reason to listen beyond the information itself.

**Account B**

> Yesterday I nearly missed my train. I arrived at the station just as the doors were closing. I ran, but I was on the wrong platform. I thought I had lost my chance. Then a station worker pointed to another train on the opposite side. It was the same service, and I got on just in time.

This is more story-like. There is a situation, a problem, a change, and an outcome.

The second account gives the listener a reason to follow the events.

However, not every story needs a serious problem. A small discovery, an unexpected meeting, a funny mistake, or a change in understanding can also form a story.

## 1.3 What does the listener need?

A listener usually needs to understand:

1. What was happening?
2. Who was involved?
3. What changed?
4. What happened next?
5. Why is the speaker telling me this?

The answers do not need to appear in this exact order. They are questions that help you check whether the story makes sense.

## 1.4 Storytelling is not the same as acting

You can tell a story in your normal voice. You can use a small pause or change your tone when it helps. You do not need to perform every sentence.

Natural delivery often works better than forced drama.

The listener should pay attention to the event, not spend all their attention watching the speaker perform.

## 1.5 Different ways to tell a story

A story does not have to be spoken. People can tell stories through different **channels**.

A **channel** is the way a story reaches an audience. A person may use words, images, sounds, actions, or a combination of these.

| Channel | How the story is told | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Spoken** | A person tells events aloud. | Telling a friend what happened yesterday; giving a live talk. |
| **Written** | A person uses written words. | A short story, a diary entry, a personal essay, or a memoir. |
| **Visual** | Images show or suggest events and changes. | A comic strip, a sequence of drawings, or a wordless picture book. |
| **Live performance** | People use actions, movement, and sometimes speech. | A play, a dance performance, or a mime act. |
| **Film and video** | Moving images, editing, sound, and often speech work together. | A film, a documentary, or a short video. |
| **Audio** | Voices and sounds carry the story without requiring images. | A storytelling podcast or a radio drama. |
| **Interactive** | The audience can make choices that affect the experience. | An interactive story or a story-based game. |

These categories can overlap. A video can contain spoken storytelling. A comic can contain written dialogue. A live storyteller may use gestures or pictures. The table shows common ways to tell stories, not strict boundaries.

**Not everything in these channels is a story.** An essay may explain an idea without telling a story. A drawing may describe an object without showing an event. A documentary may combine stories, explanations, and evidence.

### One event, three channels

Imagine this event: A student arrives at class and discovers that the exam is tomorrow, not today.

**Spoken version**

> I rushed to campus because I thought my exam was today. I reached the classroom, opened the door, and found it empty. Then I checked my calendar. The exam was tomorrow.

The speaker can use their voice, pauses, and facial expression. The listener hears the account as it happens.

**Written version**

> I reached the classroom out of breath. The lights were off. I checked my calendar and saw the date: tomorrow. I had spent the whole morning preparing for the wrong day.

The writer can choose precise words. The reader can stop and read a sentence again.

**Visual version**

Picture three drawings: the student running toward campus; an empty classroom; the student's phone showing tomorrow's date.

The images can communicate the event without a spoken explanation. The viewer must understand the order of the images.

The **event** stays the same. The **tools** change.

## 1.6 Why this course focuses on spoken storytelling

When people speak about storytelling in everyday conversations or public speaking, they often mean **telling a story aloud**. That is our main skill in this course.

You will practise telling stories to a friend, in a group, during a presentation, or into a microphone or camera. You will learn to choose events, arrange them, speak clearly, and respond to listeners.

Writing, reading, drawing, films, and performances can help you understand stories. But you will not have to learn how to write a novel, draw a comic, or produce a film.

**The course teaches you to tell stories with your own words and voice.** The other channels help us see what storytelling can be.

### Practice — Same story, different channels

1. Choose a small event from your life.
2. Tell it aloud in 30 seconds.
3. Write it in three or four sentences.
4. Imagine three drawings that could tell the same event. Describe what each drawing would show. You do not need to draw them.
5. Compare the three versions.

Ask yourself:

- What stayed the same?
- What changed when I used words, writing, or images?
- Which details did I need to explain aloud?

**Check:** You can name at least four ways to tell a story. You can also explain why this course teaches spoken storytelling rather than all forms of storytelling.

## Example: a small story

> Last week, I went to a small bookshop near my apartment. I only wanted to buy a notebook. The owner asked what I was studying, and we started talking about science books. He showed me a book that he had kept behind the counter for years. I had never heard of it. I went home with three books instead of one.

This account is not dramatic. But it contains a clear event, a change in the speaker's plan, and a small result.

## Practice 1 — Story or list?

Read each example. Decide whether it is mainly a list, description, explanation, or story.

A. "The room was small, bright, and very quiet."

B. "I entered the room, saw my old teacher, and realized that I had walked into the wrong meeting."

C. "A thermometer measures temperature."

D. "First I called the office. Then I sent an email. Finally, I received a reply."

**Suggested answers**

- A: Description.
- B: Story.
- C: Explanation.
- D: A sequence of actions. It can become a story if the sequence contains a meaningful event or change.

## Practice 2 — Tell a small story

Tell someone about a small event from the last seven days.

Use these questions:

- Where were you?
- What were you doing?
- What happened?
- What changed?
- Why do you remember it?

Speak for 30–60 seconds. Do not write a full script first.

## Check your understanding

You understand this lesson if you can explain why storytelling involves selection, order, and presentation.

# LESSON 2 — STORY, DESCRIPTION, EXPLANATION, AND ARGUMENT

## Learning goal

Choose the right form of communication for your purpose.

Storytelling is useful, but it is not the best method for every situation.

## 2.1 Five related forms

| Form | Main purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Story | Show connected events | "I missed the bus, so I took a different route." |
| Description | Show what something is like | "The bus was crowded and noisy." |
| Explanation | Help someone understand how or why something works | "The bus uses an electric motor." |
| Argument | Give reasons for a position | "The city should improve bus services." |
| Instruction | Tell someone what to do | "Tap your card before you enter." |

These forms can appear together.

For example, a science teacher may describe a cell, explain how it works, tell a short story about a discovery, and argue that a new experiment is useful.

The important skill is not to turn everything into a story. It is to choose the form that helps the listener.

## 2.2 What is an anecdote?

An **anecdote** is a short account of a particular event. People often use anecdotes in conversation to make a point, share an experience, or entertain someone.

Example:

> I once tried to cook rice without measuring the water. It turned into something closer to soup.

An anecdote can be a story. The terms are not identical. "Anecdote" describes a short type of account. "Story" describes a narrative form.

## 2.3 What is narrative?

A **narrative** is an organized account of events. The term is often used in research and literary studies.

A narrative can be spoken, written, filmed, or shown through images.

**Narration** is the act of telling or reporting events. It is one part of storytelling.

## 2.4 When should you use a story?

Use a story when the listener needs to understand an experience, a change, a choice, or a result.

Use a direct explanation when the listener mainly needs facts or instructions.

Use an argument when the listener needs to assess reasons and evidence.

Use a description when the listener needs to picture a person, place, object, or situation.

## Example: four ways to communicate one subject

Subject: a crowded train.

**Description**

> The train was full. People were standing close together, and there was very little space near the doors.

**Explanation**

> The train becomes crowded because many passengers travel at the same time, especially during the morning commute.

**Argument**

> The city should increase train frequency during peak hours because the current service does not meet demand.

**Story**

> I got on the train at 8:15 yesterday. It was so crowded that I could not reach the door. I missed my stop and had to walk back.

Each form has a different job.

## Practice — Choose the right form

For each situation, choose the best starting form.

1. You need to explain how to use a ticket machine.
2. You want to tell a friend about a strange event.
3. You want to show what a mountain village looks like.
4. You want to persuade a school to change its timetable.
5. You want to explain why a plant grows toward light.

**Suggested answers:** 1. Instruction; 2. Story; 3. Description; 4. Argument; 5. Explanation. A short story may support answers 4 and 5, but it should not replace the main explanation or evidence.

## Practice — Change the form

Take one event from your life. Explain it in three ways:

- As a story.
- As a short description.
- As a direct explanation of what you learned.

Notice how the purpose changes the information you include.

# LESSON 3 — PURPOSE AND AUDIENCE

## Learning goal

Know why you are telling a story and what the listener needs.

A story does not exist in isolation. You tell it to someone, in a situation, for a reason.

## 3.1 Identify your purpose

Before you tell a story, complete this sentence:

**"I am telling this story because I want the listener to..."**

Possible answers:

- Understand what happened.
- Laugh.
- Understand how I felt.
- See why I made a decision.
- Learn something.
- Understand an idea.
- Feel connected to my experience.
- Consider a different point of view.

You may have more than one purpose. Choose the main one.

## 3.2 Think about the listener

Ask three questions:

1. What does this person already know?
2. What information do they need?
3. How much time and interest do they have?

You do not need to explain information that the listener already knows.

You also need to explain information that is necessary to understand the event.

## 3.3 The same story changes with the audience

Imagine that you lost your phone on a train and later found it.

**To a close friend**

> I did something stupid yesterday. I left my phone on the train. I only noticed when I got home.

**To a colleague**

> I left my phone on the train yesterday. I noticed after I arrived home, so I contacted the station and collected it later.

**To a group**

> Yesterday I learned why I should check my seat before leaving a train. I left my phone behind, and I did not notice until I got home.

The event is the same. The amount of context and the reason for telling it are different.

## 3.4 Respect the listener's attention

A listener does not owe you unlimited time.

If the main point can be communicated in 30 seconds, do not use five minutes without a reason.

A long story can be useful when the details matter. A short story is better when the situation is simple.

## Practice — Audience change

Choose one experience.

Tell it to:

1. A close friend.
2. A person you have just met.
3. A group of people who need to learn something from it.

Keep the event the same. Change the information and wording.

## Self-check

- Did I know why I was telling the story?
- Did I give enough context?
- Did I explain things the listener already knew?
- Did I use more time than the story needed?

# PART 2 — FINDING STORY MATERIAL

# LESSON 4 — FIND STORIES IN ORDINARY LIFE

## Learning goal

Notice events that can become useful stories.

Many people say, "I do not have any stories." Often, the problem is not a lack of experience. The problem is that they do not notice which moments can be told.

A story does not need to be unusual. It needs a clear event and a reason to tell it.

## 4.1 Look for a change

A useful starting point is a moment when something changed.

Look for:

- An expectation that was wrong.
- A plan that changed.
- A mistake.
- A new discovery.
- A decision.
- A difficult choice.
- A meeting.
- A misunderstanding.
- A small success.
- A failure.
- A moment of embarrassment.
- A moment of kindness.
- A question that changed your view.
- Something that seemed ordinary but later became important.

## 4.2 A story can begin with a small moment

Consider:

> I went to a café.

This is not yet a story.

Now add a change:

> I went to a café to study, but I spent the whole afternoon helping a stranger find a lost dog.

Now the event has a direction.

The story may become interesting because of the unexpected change, the people involved, or what the speaker learned.

## 4.3 Do not confuse importance with tellability

An event can be important to you but difficult for another person to follow.

An ordinary event can be interesting if you present it clearly.

Ask:

- Is there a specific event?
- Is there a change?
- Is there a choice or discovery?
- Is there something the listener can understand or recognize?
- Can I explain why I want to share it?

## 4.4 Build a story bank

A **story bank** is a private collection of events that you may want to tell later.

Create a simple table:

| Date | Event | Possible point | Useful situation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Missed a train | Small mistakes can change a day | Casual conversation |
| Tuesday | Helped a new student | A small action can reduce stress | Presentation |
| Friday | Tried a new food | First impressions can be wrong | Conversation |

Write short notes. Do not write full stories yet.

## Practice — Seven-day observation

For seven days, record one moment each day.

Use this format:

- What happened?
- What changed?
- Why did I notice it?
- Who might find it useful or interesting?

At the end of the week, select two events that you can explain in under one minute.

## Common mistake

Do not wait for a major life event. Small moments often provide better material for everyday conversation.

# LESSON 5 — FIND THE POINT OF THE STORY

## Learning goal

Identify the main reason for telling an event.

A story can contain many events. The speaker must decide which event matters most.

The **point** is the main reason the listener should hear the story.

It does not have to be a moral or a life lesson.

A point can be:

- Something unexpected happened.
- I made a mistake.
- I changed my mind.
- I met someone interesting.
- I learned how something works.
- I saw a familiar situation in a new way.
- I want to share a funny moment.

## 5.1 One story, one main point

Imagine that you tell a story about a trip.

You include:

- The weather.
- The hotel.
- The food.
- The train.
- A conversation.
- A lost bag.
- A museum.
- A new friend.

All these details may be true. But if the story has no main point, it becomes a travel report.

Choose the part that gives the account its direction.

For example:

> The most interesting part of the trip was not the place. It was the stranger who helped me when I lost my bag.

Now the listener knows what to follow.

## 5.2 Use the one-sentence test

Complete this sentence:

**"The main thing about this story is..."**

Examples:

- "...I thought I was lost, but I had been looking at the wrong map."
- "...a small mistake caused a much bigger problem."
- "...I changed my opinion after talking to someone."
- "...I learned that I had misunderstood the instructions."

If you cannot complete the sentence, you may not yet know what the story is about.

## 5.3 Do not force a lesson

Not every story must teach something.

Sometimes the point is simply that an event was funny, strange, surprising, or worth sharing.

Do not add a false moral because you think every story needs one.

## Practice — Three possible points

Choose one event from your story bank.

Write three possible points for it.

Then choose the point that best fits your intended listener.

Tell the story in 45 seconds using that point.

## Improvement task

Tell the same event again, but change the point. Notice how the story changes.

# LESSON 6 — SELECT AND STORE STORY MATERIAL

## Learning goal

Choose useful details and build a collection of stories that you can use again.

## 6.1 Separate the event from your memory of it

Memory is not a perfect recording.

You may remember the main event but not the exact words, order, or time.

When you tell a personal story:

- State what you remember clearly.
- Do not invent dialogue and present it as exact.
- Say "something like" if you are giving the general meaning.
- Do not add details only because they make the story more exciting.

A story can be clear without pretending that memory is perfect.

## 6.2 Record the useful parts

For each story, record:

1. A short title.
2. The main point.
3. The people involved.
4. The place and time.
5. The main event.
6. The outcome.
7. The detail you must not forget.
8. The situations in which you could tell it.

Example:

**Title:** The wrong platform

- Point: I nearly missed a train because I trusted the wrong sign.
- People: Me and a station worker.
- Place: A large train station.
- Main event: I ran to the wrong platform.
- Outcome: A worker directed me to the correct train.
- Key detail: I had less than one minute.
- Useful situation: Talking about mistakes, travel, or asking for help.

## 6.3 Keep a range of stories

A useful story bank can include:

- Funny moments.
- Small failures.
- Discoveries.
- Kind actions.
- First experiences.
- Work or study experiences.
- Travel experiences.
- Family experiences.
- Moments of change.
- Stories that explain a skill or idea.

Do not store only dramatic stories.

## Practice — Create five story cards

Create five entries in your story bank.

Do not write the complete stories. Store only the information that will help you remember them.

# PART 3 — BUILDING A STORY

# LESSON 7 — ORGANIZE EVENTS

## Learning goal

Arrange events so that the listener can follow them.

A listener should not have to solve a puzzle to understand a simple personal story.

## 7.1 The basic event sequence

A useful starting structure is:

1. **Situation:** What was happening?
2. **Change:** What happened that changed the situation?
3. **Response:** What did you or another person do?
4. **Outcome:** What happened in the end?
5. **Meaning:** Why are you telling the listener?

This is a planning aid, not a fixed formula.

Some stories need only two or three parts. Some need more.

## 7.2 Why order matters

Compare:

**Version A**

> I missed the train because I arrived late. I had forgotten my wallet. I ran to the station. I woke up late.

**Version B**

> I woke up late and rushed to the station. On the way, I realized that I had forgotten my wallet. I ran faster, but I still missed the train.

Version B gives the listener a clearer order.

Event order can also suggest cause and effect. If you say, "I missed the train, then I forgot my wallet," the listener may understand a different relationship from "I forgot my wallet, so I missed the train."

Do not create a false cause by arranging events carelessly.

## 7.3 Chronological order is useful, but not compulsory

**Chronological order** means telling events in the order in which they happened.

It is often the clearest choice for beginners.

You can also begin near the most important moment and explain the earlier events later.

Example:

> I was standing in front of the wrong classroom when the professor asked why I was there. Ten minutes earlier, I had followed a group of students without checking the room number.

This opening begins near the problem. It then moves backward to explain the cause.

Use a non-chronological opening only when the listener can still follow the account.

## 7.4 Use time words carefully

Useful words include:

- First.
- Then.
- After that.
- A few minutes later.
- Meanwhile.
- At that moment.
- Eventually.
- Finally.
- By the time.
- Earlier.
- Later.

Do not use a time word in every sentence. Use it when the listener needs help with the sequence.

## Practice — Put the events in order

Arrange these events:

- I noticed that my bag was missing.
- I got off the train.
- I called the station.
- I remembered that I had put it under the seat.
- A station worker found it.

**One clear order:**

1. I got off the train.
2. I noticed that my bag was missing.
3. I called the station.
4. A station worker found it.
5. I collected it.

The memory of where the bag was may be explained before or after the call. The order depends on what you want the listener to understand.

## Practice — Tell it twice

Tell one story:

- First, in chronological order.
- Second, starting at the most important moment.

Ask a listener which version was easier to follow and why.

# LESSON 8 — CHOOSE A BEGINNING

## Learning goal

Start a story in a way that gives the listener a reason to continue.

A beginning has two jobs:

1. Help the listener understand the situation.
2. Give the listener a reason to hear what happens next.

## 8.1 Four useful ways to begin

### A. Start with the situation

> Last winter, I was living in a small apartment near the university.

Use this when the place, time, or background is important.

### B. Start with an action

> I opened the door and found a stranger standing in my kitchen.

Use this when the event begins immediately.

### C. Start with a problem

> I had ten minutes to reach the station, and I could not find my shoes.

Use this when the problem gives the story direction.

### D. Start with a result or surprising fact

> I went to the library to return one book and came home with a job offer.

Use this when the result creates a question in the listener's mind.

## 8.2 Avoid long introductions

Weak opening:

> So, I was thinking about something that happened a while ago, and I am not sure if it is very interesting, but I thought I would tell you because it is kind of related to what we were discussing.

Stronger opening:

> That reminds me of the time I accidentally attended the wrong lecture.

The second opening reaches the event sooner.

## 8.3 Do not use a false hook

A **hook** is an opening that creates interest.

A hook must match the story.

Do not say:

> You will never believe what happened to me.

Then tell a normal event.

Do not exaggerate the event to force attention.

## Practice — Write five openings

Choose one story. Create five openings:

- Situation.
- Action.
- Problem.
- Result.
- A short question.

Tell the story using each opening. Choose the one that sounds most natural.

# LESSON 9 — GIVE ENOUGH CONTEXT

## Learning goal

Help the listener understand the people, place, and situation without giving too much background.

**Context** is the information that helps a listener understand an event.

It can include:

- Time.
- Place.
- People.
- Relationships.
- Relevant background.
- What was happening before the main event.

## 9.1 Give only necessary context

Imagine this opening:

> My friend called me.

The listener may need more information.

> My friend called me while I was waiting for an important interview.

Now the situation is clearer.

But this may be too much:

> My friend, whom I first met in 2017 during my second year at university, and who later moved to another city after completing a degree in engineering, called me...

Most of that information is not needed.

## 9.2 Introduce people clearly

When you introduce a person, give the listener a simple label.

Examples:

- "My supervisor."
- "A friend from university."
- "The woman at the front desk."
- "A student I had never met before."

Do not introduce five people at once if the story only needs two.

## 9.3 Explain unfamiliar terms

If a listener does not know a place, role, or technical term, explain it briefly.

Example:

> I was in the lab meeting. That is a weekly meeting where researchers discuss their work.

The explanation should be short. Return to the story after giving it.

## Practice — Context check

Tell a story to someone who does not know the people involved.

Ask them:

- Who was involved?
- Where did it happen?
- What did they need to know before the main event?
- Which details were unnecessary?

Add missing context. Remove information that does not help.

# LESSON 10 — CREATE INTEREST WITHOUT FORCING DRAMA

## Learning goal

Understand why a listener may want to know what happens next.

Interest often comes from a question in the listener's mind.

Examples:

- Will the person succeed?
- What caused the problem?
- Why did the person make that choice?
- What will happen next?
- What is unusual about this situation?
- What did the speaker discover?

## 10.1 Stakes

**Stakes** are what a person may gain, lose, protect, or change.

Examples:

- A student may fail an exam.
- A traveller may miss a flight.
- A person may lose an important object.
- A speaker may damage a relationship.
- A scientist may discover that an assumption is wrong.

Stakes do not need to be large.

In a small story, the stake may be simple:

> I had promised my friend that I would arrive on time.

That is enough to make the result matter.

## 10.2 Conflict

**Conflict** is a difficulty, disagreement, or force that works against a person's goal.

Conflict can come from:

- Another person.
- A rule.
- The environment.
- A lack of information.
- A personal fear.
- A mistake.
- Limited time.
- A difficult choice.

Not every story needs conflict. A story about a discovery or a meeting can work without a clear opponent.

## 10.3 Curiosity

**Curiosity** is the desire to know something.

A listener may become curious when you give enough information to understand the situation but leave one important question open.

Example:

> I had prepared for the interview for two weeks. But when I entered the room, the first question was not about my experience. It was about a photograph on my desk.

The listener may ask: Why did the interviewer notice the photograph?

Do not hide basic information just to create suspense. Give the listener enough to follow the event.

## 10.4 Surprise

A surprise happens when the result is different from what the listener expects.

A surprise works best when it is fair. The listener should be able to understand it after it happens.

Do not add a random twist that has no connection to the earlier events.

## 10.5 Contrast

**Contrast** means showing a difference.

Examples:

- What I expected versus what happened.
- What I believed before versus what I believe now.
- What the place looked like from outside versus inside.
- What I planned versus what I did.

Contrast can create interest without adding conflict.

## Practice — Find the source of interest

Choose a simple event.

Write one sentence that shows:

- The goal.
- The problem, if there is one.
- The unexpected detail.
- The change.

Tell the story without exaggerating any of these parts.

# LESSON 11 — CHOOSE DETAILS THAT HELP

## Learning goal

Use specific details to make a story clear and easy to imagine.

A detail is useful when it helps the listener understand the event, the person, or the importance of the moment.

## 11.1 General and specific language

**General**

> The room was strange.

**Specific**

> The room had no windows, and the only light came from a small lamp near the door.

The second version gives the listener more information.

However, more detail is not always better.

A story can become slow if the speaker describes every object.

## 11.2 Use details with a purpose

A detail can:

- Identify a place.
- Show what a person wanted.
- Explain a problem.
- Create a clear image.
- Show a change.
- Reveal an emotion through action.
- Help the listener understand why an event mattered.

Ask:

**"If I remove this detail, will the listener lose something important?"**

If the answer is no, remove it.

## 11.3 Show and tell

**Tell** the listener what happened or how someone felt.

> I was nervous.

**Show** the listener an action that helps them understand the feeling.

> I checked the room number three times before I opened the door.

Both methods are useful.

Use "show" when a specific action makes the event clearer. Use "tell" when the information is simple and does not need a full scene.

Do not try to show every emotion through action. That can make speech unnatural.

## 11.4 Sensory details

Sensory details describe what a person saw, heard, felt, smelled, or tasted.

Example:

> The train doors closed with a loud warning sound.

Use sensory details when they matter to the event. Do not add them only to make the story sound literary.

## Practice — Replace vague details

Improve these sentences:

1. "The food was bad."
2. "The person was angry."
3. "The place was crowded."
4. "Something strange happened."

Possible improvements:

1. "The rice was cold, and the soup had no taste."
2. "He stopped smiling, crossed his arms, and answered in a short voice."
3. "People were standing near the entrance, and I could not reach the counter."
4. "The lights went out, and I heard someone call my name."

Your version may be different. The goal is to make the meaning clear.

# LESSON 12 — END A STORY

## Learning goal

Finish the story at the right point.

A story should not continue after the main event has finished unless the extra information serves a purpose.

## 12.1 Four useful endings

### A. State the result

> I found the bag, collected it, and went home.

Use this when the result is enough.

### B. Show what changed

> Since then, I always check my seat before leaving a train.

Use this when the event changed your behaviour.

### C. Return to the opening

> So that is how I went to the station to buy one ticket and ended up taking a completely different train.

Use this when the ending connects naturally to the beginning.

### D. Leave a simple observation

> It was a small mistake, but it changed the rest of the day.

Use this when a short comment helps the listener understand why you told the story.

## 12.2 Do not explain the obvious

Weak ending:

> And that is why I learned that sometimes in life, when we face difficulties, we must always remain positive and believe in ourselves.

This may be much larger than the event.

Stronger ending:

> I was annoyed at first, but the walk home was actually useful.

The second ending fits the scale of the story.

## 12.3 Know when to stop

A story is usually ready to end when:

- The main event is complete.
- The listener understands the result.
- The point is clear.
- Another detail would not improve the account.

## Practice — Three endings

Choose a story and create three endings:

1. Result.
2. Change.
3. Observation.

Tell the story with each ending. Choose the one that fits the purpose.

# PART 4 — SPEAKING THE STORY

# LESSON 13 — WRITE FOR SPEAKING, NOT FOR READING

## Learning goal

Use language that sounds natural when spoken.

Written language and spoken language have different patterns.

A written paragraph can contain long sentences and carefully arranged clauses. In a conversation, the speaker must produce language in real time. The listener also hears the words only once.

For this reason, spoken stories usually need clear sentence patterns.

## 13.1 Use manageable sentences

Written style:

> Upon arriving at the station, having realized that the train was scheduled to depart within a few minutes, I proceeded to the platform, where I discovered that I had misunderstood the departure information.

Spoken style:

> When I reached the station, I saw that the train was leaving in a few minutes. I ran to the platform. Then I realized that I had read the wrong departure time.

The second version is easier to say and follow.

## 13.2 Use simple links between ideas

Useful spoken links include:

- So.
- But.
- Then.
- Because.
- That is why.
- At first.
- Actually.
- The problem was...
- What happened next was...
- The strange thing was...

Do not use these words in every sentence. Use them when they show the relationship between ideas.

## 13.3 Do not memorize every word

Memorizing a full script can make a speaker sound rigid. It can also make recovery difficult if one sentence is forgotten.

Instead, remember the main events:

- Beginning.
- Main change.
- Important action.
- Result.
- Point.

Practise the story several times. Allow the exact words to change.

## 13.4 Use a simple speaking plan

Before speaking, write five short notes:

- Station.
- Wrong platform.
- One minute left.
- Worker helped.
- Reached train.

These notes are enough to guide the story. They do not contain a full script.

## Practice — Convert written language to spoken language

Rewrite this sentence for natural speech:

> Due to the unexpected change in weather conditions, we were unable to continue our planned outdoor activity.

Possible spoken version:

> The weather changed suddenly, so we could not continue our plan outside.

Now do the same with three sentences from your own writing.

# LESSON 14 — USE DIALOGUE CAREFULLY

## Learning goal

Use reported speech and short dialogue to make an event clear.

Dialogue is the speech of a person in the story.

It can help the listener understand a conversation or a decision.

## 14.1 Reported speech

> The worker told me that the train would arrive in five minutes.

This is reported speech. It gives the meaning of what the worker said.

## 14.2 Direct speech

> The worker said, "The train will arrive in five minutes."

This gives the person's words directly.

Direct speech can make a moment easier to imagine. But do not invent exact words if you do not remember them.

You can say:

> He said something like, "You still have time. Go to platform three."

This shows that you remember the meaning, not necessarily the exact sentence.

## 14.3 Keep dialogue short

A long conversation can slow a story.

Use dialogue when:

- The words changed what happened.
- The words show a person's attitude.
- The words are surprising or funny.
- The listener needs to understand a disagreement.
- The words are central to the event.

Do not reproduce every sentence of an ordinary conversation.

## Practice — Turn dialogue into a story

Situation:

A student is late. A teacher asks why. The student explains that the bus did not arrive. The teacher says that the class started ten minutes ago.

Tell this in two ways:

1. Use reported speech.
2. Use one short line of direct speech.

Compare the versions. Which one is clearer?

# LESSON 15 — CONTROL PACE AND PAUSES

## Learning goal

Control the speed of your speech so that the listener can follow the story.

**Pace** is the speed and movement of your speech.

A story does not need one speed from beginning to end.

## 15.1 Slow down when information matters

Slow down when:

- You introduce an important person.
- You explain a difficult detail.
- The listener needs time to understand.
- You reach an important decision.
- You describe the main event.

You can speak faster when you summarize events that are less important.

Example:

> I left the house, walked to the station, bought a ticket, and got on the train.  
> Then I saw something unusual.

The first sentence summarizes routine actions. The second sentence creates a new point of attention.

## 15.2 Use pauses

A pause gives the listener time to process information.

A pause can also show that one event is important.

Example:

> I opened the message.  
> [Pause]  
> It was from the person I had been trying to contact for six months.

Do not pause after every sentence. Too many pauses can make speech sound disconnected.

## 15.3 Remove unnecessary words

Compare:

> So, basically, what happened was that I was actually going to the station, and then, you know, I kind of realized that I had forgotten my ticket.

With:

> I was going to the station when I realized that I had forgotten my ticket.

Some filler words are normal in conversation. The goal is not to remove every "well", "so", or "you know". The goal is to remove words that make the story difficult to follow.

## Practice — Three time limits

Tell one story in:

- 30 seconds.
- 60 seconds.
- 2 minutes.

Do not simply speak faster to make it shorter. Remove details that are not necessary.

Record all three versions.

---

# LESSON 16 — USE YOUR VOICE AND BODY NATURALLY

## Learning goal

Use voice and physical expression to support the story.

The main job of delivery is to make the story easy to hear and understand.

## 16.1 Voice

Pay attention to:

- Volume: Can the listener hear you?
- Speed: Can the listener follow you?
- Stress: Which words are important?
- Intonation: Does your voice show the meaning of the sentence?
- Pauses: Do you give the listener enough time?

You do not need a special storyteller voice.

## 16.2 Stress important words

Compare:

> I thought the train was leaving at NINE.

> I thought the TRAIN was leaving at nine.

The stressed word changes the meaning.

Use stress to guide attention. Do not stress every word.

## 16.3 Facial expression and gestures

A gesture can help show:

- Direction.
- Size.
- Distance.
- Movement.
- A person's action.

A facial expression can support the emotion in a story.

Do not force gestures. If you are comfortable using your hands, use them when they help. If you prefer fewer gestures, that is acceptable.

## 16.4 Eye contact

In a conversation, look at the listener naturally. You do not need to stare.

In a group, move your attention across the room. Do not look only at one person or at the floor.

For a recorded story, look near the camera when you want to connect with the viewer.

## Practice — Record two versions

Record a one-minute story.

Version A: Speak as you normally speak.

Version B: Pay attention to volume, stress, pauses, and eye contact.

Compare the recordings. Do not ask, "Do I look like a professional?" Ask, "Is the story easier to understand?"

# LESSON 17 — SOUND NATURAL, NOT REHEARSED

## Learning goal

Tell a prepared story without sounding as if you are reading a script.

Natural speech does not mean unplanned speech. A speaker can prepare carefully and still sound natural.

## 17.1 Know the story, not every sentence

Learn the sequence and the important details.

Do not depend on exact wording.

A useful preparation method:

1. Write the story once.
2. Identify the five main beats.
3. Close the text.
4. Tell the story from memory.
5. Notice where you become unclear.
6. Practise those parts.
7. Tell it again in your own words.

A **beat** is a small unit of action or meaning in a story.

Example:

- I arrived late.
- The room was empty.
- I checked the timetable.
- I had come on the wrong day.
- I decided to use the free time.

## 17.2 Allow small changes

You may use different words each time you tell the story. That is normal.

The important things should remain stable:

- The main event.
- The important sequence.
- The point.
- The facts.

## 17.3 Recover when you lose your place

If you forget what comes next:

- Pause.
- Return to the last event you remember.
- Use a simple link such as "Anyway, what happened next was..."
- Continue.

Do not apologize repeatedly. A short pause is usually less distracting than a long explanation about forgetting.

## Practice — Tell it without a script

Choose a two-minute story.

Write no more than six notes.

Tell it three times. Each time, use different wording but keep the main events accurate.

# PART 5 — USING STORIES WITH PEOPLE

# LESSON 18 — TELL STORIES IN CONVERSATION

## Learning goal

Recognize when a story fits a conversation and when to stop talking.

A conversation is shared. It is not a speech in which one person speaks for as long as they want.

## 18.1 Find a natural connection

A story often fits when it connects to what someone has just said.

Example:

**Person A:** "I had a terrible time finding my hotel yesterday."

**Person B:** "That reminds me of the time I went to the wrong hotel in Osaka."

The story connects to the topic.

Do not force a story into a conversation only because you want to tell it.

## 18.2 Give a short entry

Useful openings include:

- "That reminds me of something."
- "I had a similar experience."
- "Something like that happened to me."
- "I once made the same mistake."
- "There is a funny story about that."

Use these phrases only when they fit. You do not need a special opening every time.

## 18.3 Watch the listener

The listener may show interest by:

- Asking a question.
- Smiling or laughing.
- Looking at you.
- Adding a related experience.
- Asking what happened next.

The listener may show that they want to move on by:

- Giving very short replies.
- Looking away repeatedly.
- Changing the subject.
- Trying to speak but not finding a turn.

These signs are not perfect. Do not assume you know exactly what someone thinks. Use them as information.

## 18.4 Give the conversation back

After the story, invite the other person to speak.

Examples:

- "Has something like that happened to you?"
- "What would you have done?"
- "Have you ever been to that place?"
- "Anyway, what happened with your trip?"

A conversation should move between people.

## Practice — A two-person story exchange

With a partner:

1. Person A shares a short experience.
2. Person B responds with a related experience.
3. Person A asks one question.
4. Person B answers.
5. Both people move to a new topic.

Each story should last no more than one minute.

# LESSON 19 — CHANGE THE LENGTH AND DETAIL

## Learning goal

Tell the same story in different lengths.

A useful storyteller can adjust the level of detail.

## 19.1 The short version

Use the short version when:

- The listener has little time.
- The event is simple.
- You are answering a direct question.
- The story is only supporting another point.

Example:

> I missed my train yesterday because I went to the wrong platform. A station worker helped me find the right one.

## 19.2 The medium version

Add the details that make the event understandable.

> I arrived at the station with only a few minutes to spare. I checked the board and ran to platform two. When I got there, I realized that I had read the wrong line. A station worker saw me looking confused and pointed me to platform five. I made it just in time.

## 19.3 The long version

Use a longer version when the listener needs more context or when the details create the experience.

You may include:

- Why the journey mattered.
- What you expected.
- What you saw.
- What you tried.
- What the worker said.
- How you felt.
- What happened afterwards.

Do not add all these details automatically.

## Practice — Compression

Tell a two-minute story in one minute.

Then tell the one-minute version in 20 seconds.

Keep the central event and point. Remove background and repeated information.

## Practice — Expansion

Take a 20-second story and expand it to one minute.

Add only details that improve understanding or interest.

# LESSON 20 — USE HUMOUR WHEN IT FITS

## Learning goal

Understand how humour can support a story without forcing jokes.

Humour can come from:

- A surprising result.
- A misunderstanding.
- A difference between expectation and reality.
- A person's reaction.
- A repeated pattern.
- A small mistake.
- A strange detail.

## 20.1 The event can be funny without a joke

Example:

> I spent twenty minutes looking for my glasses. They were on my head.

The humour comes from the event.

You do not need to add a punchline.

## 20.2 Timing matters

A pause can help the listener notice the contrast.

> I searched the whole apartment.  
> I checked the kitchen, the bedroom, and the bathroom.  
> Then I looked in the mirror.

The final sentence changes how the listener understands the search.

## 20.3 Do not explain the joke

If the listener understands it, continue.

If the listener does not laugh, do not repeat the joke or explain why it should be funny.

## 20.4 Be careful with the target

Humour can harm people when it relies on stereotypes, humiliation, or personal information.

A safer choice is to make yourself, the situation, or the misunderstanding the target.

Do not use another person's private experience as entertainment without permission.

## Practice — Find the contrast

Write three short situations in which:

- You expected one result.
- Something different happened.

Tell each one in 20–30 seconds.

Do not add a joke. First see whether the event itself is clear and interesting.

# LESSON 21 — TELL A STORY WITHOUT PREPARATION

## Learning goal

Organize a story while speaking.

Spontaneous storytelling is difficult because you must choose information and produce language at the same time.

You can make this easier by using a small planning routine.

## 21.1 The five-question plan

When someone asks you to tell a story, think:

1. What happened?
2. What was the important change?
3. What did I do?
4. What happened in the end?
5. Why is it worth sharing?

You can use this plan in a few seconds.

## 21.2 Use a simple structure

Try:

> I was [situation].  
> Then [important event].  
> So I [response].  
> In the end [result].  
> The interesting part was [point].

Do not repeat these exact phrases every time. Use them as a support when you need it.

## 21.3 Accept a short pause

You are allowed to think before you speak.

A short pause is better than starting with many unclear words.

You can say:

- "Let me think."
- "One example comes to mind."
- "I remember something that happened last year."

## Practice — Random prompt

Choose one prompt without preparation:

- A time you were lost.
- A time someone helped you.
- A time you misunderstood something.
- A time you changed your mind.
- A time a plan failed.
- A time you learned something unexpectedly.
- A time you met someone interesting.
- A time you made a small mistake.

Take 10 seconds to think. Speak for 45 seconds.

Repeat with a new prompt.

# PART 6 — SPECIAL USES OF STORYTELLING

# LESSON 22 — EXPLAIN AN IDEA THROUGH A STORY

## Learning goal

Use an event or example to help someone understand an idea.

A story can make an abstract idea easier to understand because it places the idea in a situation.

But the story must not replace the explanation.

## 22.1 Start with the idea

Suppose you want to explain why checking a source matters.

You could say:

> We should check the source of information because a claim can change when it is repeated.

Then give an example:

> A student once copied a statistic from a social media post. The post had removed the sentence that explained the limits of the study. When the student checked the original paper, the result was much narrower than the post suggested.

Then state the connection:

> The example shows why we should check the original source before repeating a claim.

This is a story used to support an explanation.

## 22.2 Use an example that represents the idea

A useful example should show the principle clearly.

Do not use an unrelated personal story because it is entertaining.

## 22.3 State the limit of the example

One event does not prove that something happens in every case.

Use language such as:

- "This is one example."
- "The same problem can occur in other situations."
- "This example shows the process, but it does not tell us how often it happens."

## Practice — Explain a concept

Choose one concept:

- Feedback.
- Memory.
- Probability.
- Natural selection.
- Electricity.
- A language-learning mistake.
- A scientific experiment.

Explain it in three parts:

1. State the idea.
2. Give a short story or example.
3. Explain how the example connects to the idea.

Keep the complete explanation under two minutes.

# LESSON 23 — USE STORYTELLING IN A PRESENTATION

## Learning goal

Use a story to support a presentation without turning the whole presentation into a story.

A presentation usually has a clear purpose. The audience needs to understand an idea, a result, or a recommendation.

A story can help introduce the subject, show a problem, explain a process, or make a result easier to understand.

## 23.1 Four places to use a story

### Opening

Use a short event to introduce the problem.

### Explanation

Use an example to show how a process works.

### Evidence

Use a case to show how an issue appears in a real situation. Do not treat one case as proof of a general claim.

### Closing

Return to an opening event or show what changed.

## 23.2 Keep the presentation's main idea visible

A story should support the main idea.

After the story, make the connection clear.

Example:

> This event shows why the timing of the measurement matters.

Do not assume the audience will always identify the connection by itself.

## 23.3 Do not let the story replace evidence

If you make a scientific or statistical claim, provide suitable evidence.

A personal experience can show what happened to one person. It cannot establish what happens to a whole population.

## Practice — Build a presentation opening

Choose a topic you know.

Create a 60-second opening:

1. Introduce a real or clearly labelled hypothetical situation.
2. Show the problem.
3. Connect the situation to the main idea of your presentation.

# LESSON 24 — SCIENTIFIC AND EDUCATIONAL STORYTELLING

## Learning goal

Use narrative to make scientific ideas understandable while keeping the science accurate.

Science communication often uses stories about:

- A question.
- An observation.
- An experiment.
- A failed prediction.
- A discovery.
- A debate.
- A change in understanding.

## 24.1 Show the process of discovery

A scientific story can follow this pattern:

1. What did researchers want to understand?
2. What did they know?
3. What did they do?
4. What did they observe?
5. What did the result support?
6. What remains unknown?

This structure helps the audience understand how knowledge developed.

## 24.2 Do not turn science into a simple hero story

Scientific progress is often collaborative.

It can involve:

- Many researchers.
- Earlier work.
- Failed experiments.
- Different interpretations.
- New methods.
- Unresolved questions.

Do not present one person as the only cause of a discovery if the evidence does not support that account.

## 24.3 Separate observation from interpretation

Example:

**Observation:** The measured signal increased after the treatment.

**Interpretation:** The treatment may have affected the process being studied.

**Unsupported conclusion:** The treatment definitely caused the entire change.

A story should not make an uncertain result sound certain.

## 24.4 Explain uncertainty

Useful phrases include:

- "The result suggests..."
- "The researchers found..."
- "This experiment supports..."
- "The study does not show..."
- "One possible explanation is..."
- "More evidence is needed."

These phrases do not make the story weak. They make it accurate.

## Practice — Tell the story of an idea

Choose a scientific discovery or a concept you understand.

Tell it in two minutes:

- The question.
- The problem.
- The method or observation.
- The result.
- The limit of what is known.

Ask a listener to explain the main idea back to you.

If they cannot, improve the explanation.

# PART 7 — RESPONSIBILITY AND IMPROVEMENT

# LESSON 25 — TELL TRUE STORIES RESPONSIBLY

## Learning goal

Understand the difference between making a story clear and changing what happened.

A speaker selects and arranges information. This is normal. But selection must not create a false impression.

## 25.1 Do not invent facts

Do not invent:

- Exact dialogue.
- Events that did not happen.
- A person's intention.
- A result that you did not observe.
- Statistics.
- Evidence.
- Emotional reactions that you did not experience.

If you are not sure, say so.

## 25.2 Separate fact and interpretation

Example:

**Fact:** My friend did not answer my message.

**Interpretation:** My friend was angry with me.

The interpretation may be correct, but the fact alone does not prove it.

A more accurate account is:

> My friend did not answer my message. I thought they might be angry, but I did not know why.

## 25.3 Protect other people

Before telling a personal story, consider:

- Does it reveal private information?
- Could it harm another person?
- Do I have permission to share it?
- Can I remove identifying details?
- Is the story mine to tell?

A true story is not automatically yours to share.

## 25.4 Do not use emotion as proof

A moving story can help people understand an experience. It cannot replace evidence.

One person's experience cannot establish a general medical, scientific, or social claim.

## Practice — Check a story for accuracy

Review one story you have prepared.

Mark each statement as:

- Directly remembered.
- Approximate memory.
- Interpretation.
- Uncertain.
- Added for clarity.

Remove or correct any detail that could mislead the listener.

# LESSON 26 — LISTEN, REVIEW, AND IMPROVE

## Learning goal

Use feedback to improve one skill at a time.

Practice alone is useful. Practice with feedback is often more informative because you can discover what the listener understood.

## 26.1 Record your story

Use a phone or computer.

Record a one-minute story. Do not stop when you make a mistake. Continue to the end.

Listen to the recording.

Check:

- Can I identify the main point?
- Is the event order clear?
- Is there enough context?
- Are any details unnecessary?
- Can I hear every word?
- Do I speak too quickly?
- Does the ending feel complete?

## 26.2 Use listener feedback

Ask the listener three questions:

1. What do you think the story was about?
2. Which part was most clear or interesting?
3. Where did you become confused?

Do not ask only, "Was it good?"

That question produces little useful information.

## 26.3 Improve one thing

Choose one change.

Examples:

- Give context earlier.
- Remove two unnecessary details.
- Explain who the person is.
- Slow down before the main event.
- Make the ending shorter.
- State the point more clearly.

Record the story again.

Compare the two versions.

## Practice — Two-recording method

1. Record the first version.
2. Identify one problem.
3. Make one change.
4. Record the second version.
5. Compare both versions.

Do not try to fix everything in one attempt.

# LESSON 27 — DEVELOP YOUR OWN STYLE

## Learning goal

Use the skills in this course without copying another speaker's personality.

People tell stories in different ways.

Some speakers are:

- Quiet and reflective.
- Fast and energetic.
- Funny and informal.
- Careful and analytical.
- Warm and personal.
- Direct and concise.

There is no single correct personality for storytelling.

## 27.1 Style is not the same as skill

Style is how you choose to express yourself.

Skill is your ability to communicate effectively.

A quiet speaker can be skilled. An energetic speaker can be unclear.

Do not use personality as an excuse to avoid improvement. Also do not treat personality as a problem that must be removed.

## 27.2 Identify your natural strengths

After several recordings, ask:

- Do I explain events clearly?
- Do I notice small details?
- Do I use humour naturally?
- Do I create a clear sequence?
- Do I explain difficult ideas well?
- Do I listen and respond to people?
- Do I speak in a calm or energetic way?

Use your strengths. Improve the parts that make your stories difficult to follow.

## 27.3 Borrow techniques, not identities

You can learn from a comedian's timing, a teacher's clarity, or a scientist's careful explanation.

You do not need to copy their accent, gestures, personality, or exact phrases.

## Practice — Your storytelling profile

Complete these sentences:

- My natural speaking style is...
- One strength I have is...
- One skill I need to improve is...
- A technique I want to practise is...
- A type of story I enjoy telling is...

Use your answers to choose your next practice tasks.

## Learning goal

Use the full course to prepare and tell a clear, natural story.

## Project A — A short conversational story

**Time:** 30–60 seconds

Choose an ordinary experience.

Your story must:

- Have a clear event.
- Give enough context.
- Use a clear sequence.
- Include only useful details.
- End naturally.

Tell it to another person.

## Project B — A personal story

**Time:** 2–3 minutes

Choose an experience that includes a change, decision, discovery, or meaningful result.

Prepare the story with these questions:

1. What is the main point?
2. Why am I telling this story?
3. Who is the listener?
4. What context does the listener need?
5. What changed?
6. What did I do?
7. What happened in the end?
8. What should I leave out?

Use short notes, not a full script.

Record the story.

## Project C — An explanation through a story

**Time:** 2–3 minutes

Choose an idea from science, education, work, or everyday life.

Explain the idea. Use a story or example to help the listener understand it.

Make sure that:

- The example is relevant.
- The explanation is accurate.
- The listener can identify the main idea.
- You do not claim that one example proves a general rule.

## Final review

Use the assessment table below.

| Skill | 1 — Needs work | 2 — Developing | 3 — Effective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main point | Hard to identify | Mostly clear | Clear and relevant |
| Context | Important information is missing | Some context is missing | Enough context is provided |
| Sequence | Events are confusing | Mostly understandable | Easy to follow |
| Detail | Too much or too little | Some details need adjustment | Details support the event |
| Interest | No clear reason to continue | Some interesting parts | The event gives a clear reason to listen |
| Ending | Story stops suddenly or continues too long | Ending is understandable | Ending feels complete |
| Delivery | Difficult to hear or follow | Mostly clear | Natural and easy to follow |
| Audience fit | Not suited to the situation | Some adjustment is needed | Fits the listener and purpose |
| Accuracy | Important details are misleading | Some uncertainty is not clear | Facts and uncertainty are represented honestly |

Do not use this table to judge personality, accent, or confidence.

Your goal is improvement, not perfection.

# RESEARCH NOTES AND FURTHER STUDY

This course uses a practical teaching framework. It does not claim that every story follows one structure or that every technique has the same scientific support.

The resources below provide useful foundations. Some are academic sources. Others are professional guides. They should not be treated as equal forms of evidence.

## 1. Oral narrative structure

**Labov and Waletzky's work on oral personal narratives**

Their research helped establish a way to examine how people tell stories about personal experience. A developed oral narrative may include:

- Abstract: a short summary.
- Orientation: information about people, place, and time.
- Complicating action: the events that make up the main development.
- Evaluation: why the story matters to the speaker.
- Resolution: what happened in the end.
- Coda: a return to the present conversation.

This is an analytical model, not a mandatory formula. Natural stories may omit parts or use them in a different order.

Accessible discussion:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7969638/

Further discussion:
https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/narrative-in-english-conversation/towards-a-working-definition-of-conversational-narrative/8914D891DFE0744DA4B05ABBACC845A2

## 2. Narrative transportation and persuasion

Green and Brock (2000), "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

The researchers studied how absorption in a narrative can affect story-consistent beliefs and evaluations. Their work supports the idea that a listener's attention and involvement can matter.

It does **not** prove that every story is more persuasive than every explanation, or that stories always improve memory.

https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701

## 3. Oral storytelling and second-language learning

Yao, Xi, and Prieto (2025), "The effects of oral narrative interventions on second language learning: A systematic review and meta-analysis", *System*.

The review examined 50 studies and included 29 studies in its meta-analysis. It reported positive effects of oral narrative interventions on language outcomes, including speaking, reading, grammar, and vocabulary.

The results support the use of structured storytelling practice in language learning. They do not show that every storytelling exercise works equally well for every learner.

https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2025.103835

## 4. Professional oral storytelling

**The Moth — Tell Your Story**

The Moth provides practical guidance based on live personal storytelling. Its advice includes identifying stakes, starting clearly, knowing the story well, and practising for a time limit.

Its stage format is one form of storytelling. Its rules should not be applied to every casual conversation.

https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story

**The Moth — Storytelling Tips and Tricks**

https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks

**The Moth — How to Tell a Story**

A book that draws on the organization's experience developing true personal stories for live audiences.

https://themoth.org/how-to-tell-a-story

## 5. Practical storytelling books

These books provide different approaches. They are practitioner resources, not substitutes for research.

- Matthew Dicks, *Storyworthy* (2018). Focuses on finding and shaping personal stories.
- Meg Bowles and others, *How to Tell a Story: The Essential Guide to Memorable Storytelling from The Moth* (2022). Focuses on true stories for an audience.
- Doug Lipman, *Improving Your Storytelling* (1999). Focuses on oral storytelling practice.
- Jonathan Gottschall, *The Storytelling Animal* (2012). Explores the role of stories in human life.
- Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling* (2020). Connects narrative craft with ideas from psychology and human behaviour.

A book's popularity does not prove that every claim in it is correct. Compare advice with other sources and with your own practice.

## 6. Public speaking and storytelling

TED Masterclass treats storytelling as one part of a wider speaking skill set. It also covers ideas, structure, voice, connection, explanation, persuasion, and visual support.

https://masterclass.ted.com/course-overview

TED-Ed's Public Speaking 101 collection includes a lesson called "4 ways to tell a great story."

https://ed.ted.com/ted_ed_collections/public-speaking-101

These resources are useful for understanding the overlap between storytelling and presentations. This course remains focused on spoken stories in both everyday and formal situations.

# FINAL PRINCIPLES

1. A story is an account of connected events.
2. Storytelling is a choice about what to include, how to arrange it, and how to present it.
3. A story needs a reason to be told.
4. The listener needs enough context to understand the event.
5. A clear sequence is more useful than a complicated structure.
6. A story does not always need conflict or drama.
7. Details should help the listener understand.
8. A beginning should enter the event without unnecessary delay.
9. An ending should complete the account without explaining more than necessary.
10. Spoken language should be clear and natural.
11. Delivery should support the story, not hide its weaknesses.
12. A conversation belongs to both people.
13. A story can support an explanation, but it cannot replace evidence.
14. A personal story should not be changed to create a false impression.
15. The best way to improve is to tell stories, listen to the result, and make one useful change at a time.

**The central skill is simple to state: know what happened, know why it matters, and help another person understand it.**

- Speaking speed.
