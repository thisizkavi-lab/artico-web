# How Artico Works

## There is no single starting line

People do not arrive at English from the same place.

One learner may be meeting the alphabet for the first time. Another may have studied grammar for ten years but still struggle to follow an ordinary conversation. Someone else may read well, travel comfortably, and only want to make their pronunciation clearer.

Artico is built with beginners in mind, so it begins at the beginning. But the beginning is not a gate you must prove that you have passed. The Foundation is free to preview, revisit, or skip. There is no placement test and no penalty for changing your mind.

If the alphabet and basic sounds are already familiar, take a quick look. You may notice a gap that school never made visible. If the material is truly familiar, continue to Social Fluency. The purpose of the Foundation is to remove confusion, not to keep experienced learners waiting.

> [!important] Your route
> Start from the earliest point that is useful to you—not the earliest point that makes you look like a “good student.”

## Two stages, two ways of working

Artico is easiest to understand as a simple two-by-two structure.

### The two curriculum stages

1. **Foundation** — letters, sounds, mouth movement, rhythm, and tongue twisters.
2. **Social Fluency** — the language used to enter, continue, and navigate everyday interaction.

### The two modes

1. **Learn** — understand the situation, meaning, pattern, and reason.
2. **Practice** — listen, speak, retrieve, vary, and repeat until the language becomes easier to use.

These are not four separate courses. They are four views of one path.

```text
ORIENTATION
    ↓
FOUNDATION ─────────────────────→ SOCIAL FLUENCY
letters & sounds                  everyday situations
    ↓                                  ↓
TONGUE-TWISTER ROUTINE ─────────→ SPEAKING PRACTICE
begins here and continues alongside the course
```

Learn gives practice a direction. Practice gives learning a body.

## Stage one: the physical foundation

English writing uses 26 letters, but letters and sounds are not the same thing. A common teaching model organizes English into approximately 44 phonemes, or meaning-changing sound categories. The exact number changes with the accent and the way linguists analyse the sound system, so **44 is a useful learning map, not an eternal law**.

This small fact answers many questions before they become frustrations. Why can the same letter sound different in different words? Why can two spellings create the same sound? Why can a word look familiar on the page and disappear when somebody says it naturally?

In **Alphabets & Sounds**, you will:

- meet the 26 uppercase and lowercase letters;
- hear their names and common sound relationships;
- learn why spelling and pronunciation do not always match;
- notice basic mouth and tongue positions;
- begin hearing English rhythm as well as individual sounds.

You do not need to memorize every IPA symbol. IPA is introduced as a map: evidence that a letter and a sound are different kinds of thing. It is there to reduce mystery, not create another examination.

## Tongue twisters: where practice begins

The first serious speaking routine begins with tongue twisters.

At first they may look childish or trivial. They are not magic, and they do not teach conversation by themselves. Their value is narrower and more practical: they isolate difficult transitions and let you repeat them without having to invent a complete conversation at the same time.

For Japanese learners, this includes contrasts such as **/r/ and /l/**, but Artico does not reduce English pronunciation to one famous problem. The routine also works with consonant clusters, clean word endings, vowels, airflow, stress, and rhythm.

Each tongue twister has a reason for being there. Artico uses a small, reviewed set of classics rather than an endless feed of AI-generated sentences. You will see the target movement, hear an approved model, begin slowly, add rhythm, and transfer the same movement into an ordinary phrase.

The goal is not to say a ridiculous sentence as fast as possible. The goal is to make one transition accurate enough, often enough, that your attention is free for meaning later.

This work does not end after one lesson. The tongue-twister routine begins in the Foundation and continues beside Social Fluency. A four-week rotation can give the practice shape, but it is not a promise that pronunciation will be “finished” in one month. Coordination and automaticity grow through many accurate returns.

> [!tip] Accuracy before speed
> Slow and clean is training. Fast and unclear is only rehearsal of the mistake.

## Stage two: everyday social fluency

Once the physical foundation is visible—or whenever you decide you are ready—you move into Social Fluency.

The sequence begins with ordinary human contact: greetings, openings, small responses, questions, invitations, reactions, feelings, stories, and the many small moves that keep a conversation alive. From there, the situations and relationships become wider.

The course does not select language because it looks impressive in a textbook. It prioritizes high-frequency, reusable language that appears across many real situations. Every dialogue, phrase, and word must earn its place by helping the learner do something with another person.

The examples begin from life in Japan: a workplace, a café, a station, a shop, a hobby group, a video call, a visitor asking for help, or a conversation while travelling. Japan is the launch context, not the border of the language. The same social patterns should remain useful as the learner’s world expands.

## What happens inside a chapter

Every Social Fluency chapter follows the same movement:

1. **See the situation** — Who is speaking? What is happening? What does the learner need to do?
2. **Understand** — Read the theory in Japanese, English, or both. See the big picture, then the important detail.
3. **Hear it naturally** — Listen to believable speech at the speed and rhythm people actually use.
4. **Notice** — Pay attention to one useful phrase, social choice, sound change, or rhythm pattern.
5. **Practise with support** — Use the shadowing version, repeat in thought groups, and change one element at a time.
6. **Retrieve and vary** — Answer without reading every word; adapt the language to a new person or detail.
7. **Use it outside the lesson** — Complete one small mission in your own life.
8. **Return** — Revisit the language after a gap so that recall becomes less dependent on the page.

The loop is simple on purpose:

**understand → hear → notice → speak → retrieve → use → return**

## What theory is for

Theory is not decoration, and it is not a script to memorize.

Its job is to make the language intelligible. It shows the wide view—why this interaction matters and what the speakers are doing—and then moves closer: why this phrase fits, how direct it sounds, what the tone changes, and where a Japanese learner may reasonably become confused. Finally, it returns to the whole situation so the detail has somewhere to live.

You do not need to remember every paragraph. By the end, you should understand the idea well enough to explain it simply to another person. That is a better test of understanding than being able to repeat the page word for word.

Theory will include audio where hearing is necessary, an image where seeing removes confusion, and video only when movement or situation genuinely requires it. More media does not automatically mean better teaching. Each medium must have a job.

## What practice is for

Practice is where you will spend most of your time.

Reading an explanation can change what you know. It cannot, by itself, make a sentence arrive while another person is waiting. For that, you need repetitions: listening, speaking, pausing, retrieving, changing, failing, correcting, and returning.

Artico does not simply say, “Practise this.” It tells you what to do:

- listen once without speaking;
- notice one target;
- repeat slowly three times;
- speak with the shadowing track;
- remove part of the support;
- change one detail;
- use the pattern in an ordinary phrase;
- return at a stated time.

This is not a score, a streak, or a game. It is a calm training sequence. The application behaves like a course, a teacher, a coach, and a thinking guide in one place—but the learner still has to perform the repetitions.

## Two audio versions, two different jobs

The same approved language appears in two audio forms.

**Natural audio** belongs in Learn. It shows how people normally speak: believable pace, emotion, reductions, pauses, and turn-taking. It is not artificially slow just because the listener is learning.

**Shadowing audio** belongs in Practice. It preserves natural English but adds enough space and structure to follow: clear thought groups, repeatable segments, and a progression toward normal speed.

The shadowing track is not a robotic version of the natural track. It is a rehearsal tool for reaching it.

## Self-contained does not mean closed to the world

Each chapter should contain what you need to understand and practise its target. The explanation, examples, transcript, audio, visual support, instructions, and return cue should be in one place. You should not have to interrupt every lesson to search the internet and assemble your own course.

That does not mean Artico tries to contain the whole English language. No application can. A dictionary, another person, a film, a journey, or the unexpected sentence you hear tomorrow will always teach you something the course did not predict.

Artico is self-contained as a lesson and open to the world as a language.

## The mission beyond the screen

Practice cannot remain inside the application forever.

Some missions involve another person: greet a visitor, ask a colleague a small question, or use a repair phrase when you miss something. Others can be done alone: leave yourself a voice message, rewrite a line for your real life, notice a phrase in a video, or rehearse what you want to say before a call.

The app cannot verify every real conversation, and it should not pretend to. Its job is to make the action small, clear, safe, and relevant enough that you can attempt it.

The mission is where borrowed language begins to become your language.

## Clear speech, not a borrowed identity

Being understood is the first practical threshold, but it does not have to be the final ambition. If you enjoy the sound, precision, rhythm, or energy of a particular speaker or regional accent—even New York English—you may choose it as a model.

Artico will help you listen closely and improve what can be trained. It will not promise to make every learner sound native, and it will not treat one native accent as the definition of correct English. English belongs to people with many accents.

Aim first for speech that is clear, stable, and socially comfortable. Beyond that, refinement is a legitimate craft. Your accent can become more precise without your identity becoming less Japanese.

## How to begin

1. Choose Japanese, English, or bilingual guidance.
2. Read the Orientation once.
3. Preview the Foundation and decide where it is useful.
4. Begin the tongue-twister routine in Practice.
5. Start Social Fluency in Learn.
6. Spend more time speaking than rereading.
7. Complete one small mission.
8. Return when the lesson asks you to return.

You do not have to complete all of Foundation before touching everyday English. The two can overlap. Learn something useful today; keep training the physical foundation beside it.

## The honest promise

Artico will not make you fluent in 30 or 90 days. It will not reward you for tapping quickly through screens. It will not ask you to memorize explanations you cannot use.

It will give you a coherent path, careful theory, believable models, explicit practice, and repeated chances to carry English into life.

The application can organise the work. It can explain, demonstrate, remind, and guide. It cannot do the speaking for you.

That is the agreement: **Artico makes the next action clear. You return and do the action.**

## Where we go next

We begin with the smallest visible pieces of written English: 26 letters. Then we separate letters from sounds, meet the mouth that has to produce them, and begin the speaking routine that will continue through the course.

You may know some of this already. Look anyway—not because you must prove that you are a beginner, but because a strong structure is easier to build when you can see its foundation.
