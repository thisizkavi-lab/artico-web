# CH35 — At the Market

**Outcome:** Ask about a product and quantity, discuss a price, and accept or decline an offer.  

## Study

Start with the product: **How much are the strawberries?** If the amount is unclear, ask **Is that per basket or per pound?** The **price** is what something costs; the **quantity** is how much you receive.

Useful quantities include **a loaf of bread**, **a bunch of grapes**, **a jar of honey**, **a bag of apples**, **a dozen eggs**, and **a pound of potatoes**. A dozen means twelve. Use the unit on the stall's sign or ask the seller; do not silently assume a weight or package size.

### Buying fresh food

For this fictional practice stall: strawberries cost $5 per basket; bread costs $4 per loaf; eggs cost $6 per dozen. These are scene prices, not a price guide.

**Customer:** How much are the strawberries?  
**Seller:** Five dollars a basket.  
**Customer:** I'll take one basket and a loaf of bread, please.  
**Seller:** Sure. That's nine dollars.  
**Customer:** Can I pay by card?  
**Seller:** Yes, we accept cards.  
**Customer:** Great. Could I have a receipt?  

**I'll take it** signals a decision to buy. **I'm just looking** explains that you have not decided. **Can I have...?**, **Could I have...?**, and **I'd like...** are useful request choices; the scene, tone and whole exchange matter more than treating one opening as automatically polite and another as automatically impolite.

### Discussing an offer

Bargaining is a task for a setting where offers are welcome, not something to impose on every seller. In the next fictional scene, a secondhand stall displays a sign: **Offers welcome**. A lamp is marked $25.

**Buyer:** Would you take twenty dollars for the lamp?  
**Seller:** I could do twenty-three.  
**Buyer:** Twenty-three is more than I want to spend. How about twenty-one?  
**Seller:** Sorry, twenty-three is my lowest price.  
**Buyer:** Okay, I'll pass. Thanks anyway.  

**Would you take...?** proposes a price. **That's my lowest price** says the seller will not reduce it further. **I'll pass** declines the purchase. You can be firm without inventing a reason: **Twenty is my limit.** If you accept, say **Okay, twenty-three works. I'll take it.**

### Clarifying what you are buying

**Does that price include both pieces?** checks an unclear offer. **Can I buy just one?** asks about quantity. **Is this ripe?** asks about fruit condition; **Could I take a closer look?** asks to inspect the item.

## Practice

Work alone by reading the seller's line and saying the buyer's response, then reversing roles. No real purchase is needed.

1. At the food stall, buy two loaves of bread. Ask whether you can pay by card. Use the posted prices above.
2. At the offers-welcome stall, offer $20 for the lamp. The seller says **I can do twenty-three.** Accept, or decline because your limit is $20.
3. A seller says **They're five dollars.** You do not know whether this means one item or a basket. Ask for clarification before choosing.

**Sample possibilities:** “Two loaves, please.” — “That's eight dollars.” — “Can I pay by card?” / “Would you take twenty?” — “I can do twenty-three.” — “Okay, I'll take it.” Or “Thanks, but twenty is my limit. I'll pass.” / “Is that five dollars each or per basket?”  

Repeat later with a different quantity or limit. Check that both people know the item, quantity and agreed price before the purchase ends.
