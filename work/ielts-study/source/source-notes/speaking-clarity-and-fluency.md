### Vocabulary and grammar

Choose words that say what you mean accurately and fit the topic. The lesson’s examples contrast ordinary, precise wording with inflated phrasing that is harder to control. Its central teaching point is sound: do not insert rare vocabulary or elaborate grammar just to impress the examiner. At the same time, do not teach “simple language only” as a high-band formula. The official descriptors reward vocabulary that is flexible and precise, and grammar that shows range as well as accuracy. Develop range through meaningful practice and use the structure that fits the message; do not confuse complexity with quality. ### Fluency and idea development

Do not equate fluency with speaking fast or sounding confident. In the lesson’s examples, a measured speaker pauses while organizing an idea; the useful distinction is whether hesitation reflects content planning or difficulty retrieving language, and whether the response remains coherent. The official descriptors consider continuity, logical sequencing, relevance, and development. They do not endorse frequent hesitation as harmless: at the highest bands hesitation is very rare and content-related. ([7:19–10:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5_A5vjLgDec&t=439s); [IELTS Speaking band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf); [Speaking key assessment criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf))

For an unfamiliar subject, select one manageable, relevant point and explain it clearly rather than searching for expert knowledge or an impressive theory. This is a useful response strategy, not permission to go off-topic: the answer still needs to address the question and develop ideas coherently. The examiner assesses spoken English through the published criteria; the speaker does not need to prove specialist expertise.
