# Artico — Public Speaking & Presentations
## Research synthesis, curriculum rationale and evidence register

**Research audit:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** source review completed; course redesign specification  
**Purpose:** turn public-speaking and presentation research into a coherent, teachable Artico course—not a collection of presentation tips.

---

## 1. Executive synthesis

A presentation is a time-limited communication event in which a speaker helps a particular audience understand, assess, decide, learn, or participate. It is not simply a document read aloud, nor is it necessarily a slide show. The right structure and delivery depend on the audience, goal, occasion, evidence, constraints, and the kind of response sought.

The sources are complementary rather than interchangeable:

- **iBiology's Share Your Research** is the strongest practical backbone for research talks. It contributes a complete process: audience, goal, core message, speaking style, scientific narrative, outline, body, introduction, ending, title, connection, rehearsal, stage preparation, anxiety and Q&A.
- **General public-speaking curricula** supply the wider territory iBiology does not cover in depth: ethical speaking, listening, argument quality, informative, persuasive and ceremonial purposes, and different speech genres.
- **Stanford's Oral Communication Program** explains the distinctive demands of spoken communication: listeners cannot reread, so the speaker must orient, signpost, translate jargon, use clear examples, and keep the argument audible.
- **Michael Alley's assertion–evidence approach** gives a concrete design method for technical slides: a complete-message headline supported by visual evidence.
- **Richard Mayer's multimedia-learning research** helps explain why coherent, signalled, well-integrated words and graphics can support understanding. Its findings concern instructional multimedia, so transfer to a live presentation must be made carefully.
- **Garr Reynolds and Nancy Duarte** contribute useful practitioner frameworks: restraint and naturalness; and audience-centred narrative for persuasive communication. These are professional design models, not universal experimental laws.
- **Communication and educational research** adds important qualifications: narrative can influence attitudes and intentions without necessarily improving factual belief; statistical evidence is often better suited to factual belief; deliberate practice and specific feedback can improve performance, but feedback quality matters; psychological interventions can reduce public-speaking anxiety, with substantial variation across people and studies.
- **Ethics, statistical integrity and accessibility** are not optional finishing touches. They determine whether an audience can trust, interpret and access what is being presented.

The course should therefore teach a repeatable decision process:

> **Frame the situation → focus the message → build the reasoning → make the evidence legible → deliver and respond → evaluate and revise.**

This is an Artico synthesis, not a named model taken from any one author. It is iterative: evidence can change the story, audience feedback can change the explanation, and rehearsal can reveal that the original structure is not working.

#### What iBiology does particularly well

1. It begins with the communication framework, not slide software.
2. It distinguishes the **topic**, **goal**, **audience**, **core message**, and **“so what”** rather than treating them as synonyms.
3. It asks learners to apply every idea to one real talk.
4. It treats story as a logical progression of a research question and evidence, not just decorative anecdotes.
5. It connects each result to the technique used, the visual shown, what was learned, and what comes next.
6. It makes the introduction depend on the body and the audience.
7. It includes speaking style, rehearsal, peer feedback, technical readiness, anxiety and Q&A—not only slide design.

#### What should not be copied as a universal law

- The “15 words or less” message target is a useful constraint for discovery, not a rule that every scientific claim must fit into a slogan.
- The 60/30/15/8-second Half-Life exercise is a message-discovery activity, not a requirement to turn all complex research into sound bites.
- A hook is optional and must fit the audience and speaker.
- The suggested 20–30% introduction and under-10-word title are practical heuristics, not universal research-backed thresholds.
- The story-arc metaphor should not force every talk into a dramatic climax or encourage a speaker to invent a neat ending where the research is unresolved.
- The source's broad advice to simplify slides should not be misread as “all text is bad” or “one bullet count works for every slide.” Accessibility, equations, detailed handouts, and audience needs sometimes require more text.
- Advice about clothing, humour, smiling, eye contact, and memorising the opening is contextual. It should not become a rigid performance standard.

### 2.2 General public speaking: breadth beyond research talks

**Stand Up, Speak Out: The Practice and Ethics of Public Speaking** (University of Minnesota Libraries Publishing; CC BY-NC-SA 3.0) was reviewed as a general public-speaking reference:

- Open textbook: https://open.umn.edu/opentextbooks/textbooks/stand-up-speak-out-the-practice-and-ethics-of-public-speaking
- Online book: https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Public_Speaking/Stand_up_Speak_out_-_The_Practice_and_Ethics_of_Public_Speaking

Its coverage includes ethics, confidence, listening, audience analysis, purpose and topic, research, supporting arguments, introductions, body, conclusions, outlining, language, delivery, presentation aids, informative speaking, persuasive speaking, and entertaining/ceremonial speaking.

**Synthesis:** iBiology supplies the depth of a research-talk workflow; general public-speaking curricula prevent Artico from mistaking a scientific seminar for the whole discipline. The course must include ethical sourcing, listening, argument quality, informative/persuasive/ceremonial purposes, and the fact that not every talk has the same desired outcome.

The textbook is useful foundational coverage, not a current authority for every digital presentation or accessibility detail. Its edition and examples have age-related limitations.

### 2.3 Stanford Oral Communication Program

Reviewed:

- Paper ≠ Presentation: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/paper-presentation
- Presentation Content: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/presentation-content
- Scientific Presentation: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/tutoring/scientific-presentation
- Delivery: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/delivery
- Visuals and PowerPoint: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/visuals-and-powerpoint
- Speech Anxiety: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/speech-anxiety
- Exercises on Delivery: https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/exercises-delivery

The most transferable idea is that **a listener experiences the argument in time**. A reader can reread, scan backward and pause. A listener usually cannot. Consequently, oral communication needs visible structure in the spoken language: clear transitions, internal summaries, repeated key terms, short syntax, purposeful examples and explicit relationships between ideas.

Stanford also distinguishes word-rate from idea-pace and recommends practising small portions with specific attention. Its materials are university teaching guidance, not all primary experimental studies; the page “Paper ≠ Presentation” itself cites Allen (2008).

### 2.4 Scientific slide design: Michael Alley

- Assertion–Evidence approach: https://www.assertion-evidence.com/
- Garner, J. K. & Alley, M. P. (2013), *How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension: A case for the assertion–evidence approach*: https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf

The approach replaces a vague topic heading plus a list of bullets with a complete assertion supported by visual evidence. It asks the presenter to decide what the audience should understand from the slide before selecting its visual.

The 2013 study compared two slide approaches with 110 engineering students. The assertion–evidence condition, which also followed six multimedia principles, produced better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load and better delayed recall.

**Limit:** the study supports the combined instructional design package in an engineering-education setting. It does not prove that every slide must have a sentence headline, or isolate the effect of each design principle separately. It is a strong default for technical evidence, not a universal template for all presentations.

### 2.5 Multimedia learning: Richard Mayer

- Mayer (2017), *Using multimedia for e-learning*: https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12197
- Mayer & Fiorella, *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, principles for essential processing: https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108894333.025
- Fiorella & Mayer, principles for reducing extraneous processing: https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108894333.019
- Cromley, J. G. & Chen, R. (2025), *A meta-analysis of Richard Mayer's multimedia learning research: Searching for boundary conditions of design principles across multiple media types*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730

Mayer's theory distinguishes three kinds of cognitive processing:

- **Extraneous:** effort caused by irrelevant material or confusing design.
- **Essential:** effort needed to understand the actual material.
- **Generative:** effort used to organise and make sense of the material.

Relevant principles include coherence, signalling, spatial/temporal contiguity, segmentation, pre-training, modality, personalization, and generative activity. These suggest practical slide and lesson decisions: remove decorative clutter, label the part being discussed, place labels near the relevant visual, reveal complex processes in manageable stages, introduce essential terms, and ask learners to explain or apply ideas.

The 2025 meta-analysis examined 92 Mayer-authored articles, 181 studies and 591 effects from 1990–2022. It reported an overall effect of g = 0.37, with meaningful moderation by design principle, medium, learner age, academic domain and outcome. Effects were not uniform. Text plus diagrams showed more consistent learning effects than some other media types; active learning/generative involvement was especially important.

**Critical boundary:** these studies investigate multimedia instructional materials, not every live conference talk. Artico should use Mayer to design its own lessons and explain visual processing, but should not claim that every live slide must follow one immutable formula. Accessibility also means not removing essential on-screen text or captions simply because some multimedia experiments found redundancy effects.

### 2.6 Storytelling: what the evidence supports

Sources:

- Braddock, K. & Dillard, J. P. (2016), *Meta-analytic evidence for the persuasive effect of narratives on beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors*: https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555
- Zebregs, S. et al. (2015), *The differential impact of statistical and narrative evidence on beliefs, attitude, and intention*: https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2013.842528
- *Long-term Persuasive Effects in Narrative Communication Research: A Meta-Analysis* (2020): https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqaa017

The research does not support the simplistic claim that a story always makes information more believable or accurate.

- Braddock and Dillard found small positive relationships between narrative exposure and narrative-consistent beliefs (r = .17), attitudes (r = .19), intentions (r = .17) and behaviours (r = .23).
- Zebregs and colleagues found statistical evidence more influential than narrative evidence for beliefs and attitudes, while narrative evidence was more influential for intentions in the health-communication studies they examined.
- The 2020 meta-analysis found a small advantage for narratives in attitudes and intentions, but not beliefs. The overall immediate difference was d = .14; the delayed difference was d = .16.

**Synthesis:** use narrative to establish relevance, context, tension, human stakes, and a path through the material. Use evidence to justify factual claims. The story must not replace the data, hide counterevidence, or turn uncertainty into a false resolution.

For research talks, the story can be the logic of the investigation: what was unknown, why the question mattered, what evidence changed the picture, what remains unresolved. Personal vignettes are optional. A talk about a method or a technical update may need a clear explanatory sequence rather than dramatic storytelling.

### 2.7 Message discovery: Half-Life Your Message

Aurbach, E. L., Prater, K. E., Patterson, B. & Zikmund-Fisher, B. J. (2018), *Half-Life Your Message: A Quick, Flexible Tool for Message Discovery*: https://doi.org/10.1177/1075547018781917

This is an open-access article describing a teaching/practice tool, based on its authors' experience rather than a randomized efficacy trial. The exercise asks a speaker to express the same idea spontaneously in 60, 30, 15 and 8 seconds, with very short transitions between rounds. It is intended to discover what the speaker is really trying to say, not manufacture a catchy sound bite.

The article explicitly recommends preparation by free-association, then reflection afterwards: is the emerging message appropriate to the context, audience and goal? Does it preserve necessary nuance? What supporting ideas are needed? If not, repeat it with a better-defined purpose.

**Artico implication:** include the exercise, but label it a discovery method. Do not treat the shortest version as the final scientific claim.

### 2.8 Rehearsal, feedback and skill improvement

Selected studies:

- Heiman et al. (2012), *E-learning and deliberate practice for oral case presentation skills: a randomized trial*: https://doi.org/10.3109/0142159X.2012.714879
- Kim et al. (2005), *A randomized-controlled study of encounter cards to improve oral case presentation skills*: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1525-1497.2005.0140.x
- *Feedback on oral presentations during pediatric clerkships: a randomized controlled trial* (2014): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25349316/
- Ochoa & Dominguez (2020), *Controlled evaluation of a multimodal system to improve oral presentation skills in a real learning setting*: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.12987
- *Longitudinal Improvement in Public Speaking Skills Through Participation in a Resident Public Speaking Curriculum* (2024): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39121952/

The results support a practice–feedback–revision loop, but not the claim that any repetition or feedback automatically improves performance.

- Heiman et al.'s randomized study of 132 medical students found improvement after an online curriculum and deliberate practice, compared with a waitlist group.
- A 2014 randomized trial with 476 students found that detailed, faculty-led feedback was associated with better subsequent presentations than no formal feedback; the simple single-item feedback condition did not show the same significant result.
- The 2020 study of 180 students found a small but significant effect of an automated multimodal feedback tool on a later expert evaluation.
- The 2005 encounter-card trial found no significant improvement in its summative presentation score, despite the feasibility and usefulness of the assessment tool.

**Synthesis:** practice must have a target. Feedback should point to observable behaviours and specific parts of the talk. Learners should use an audience representative of the intended audience, not only a friend who already knows the subject. Self-assessment is useful but insufficient by itself.

### 2.9 Public-speaking anxiety

- McWilliam, A. M., Beattie, S. & Callow, N. (2025), *Review and Analysis of Successful PSA Interventions: An Applied Perspective*: https://doi.org/10.1080/00223980.2025.2459662
- *Psychological Interventions for the Fear of Public Speaking: A Meta-Analysis* (2019): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428748/

The 2025 review included 26 studies and 2,253 participants; interventions included exposure, cognitive modification, combined and other approaches. The overall effect was g = 1.17, but heterogeneity was high and individual effects varied. The 2019 meta-analysis included 30 randomized trials and 1,355 participants and found an overall effect of g = 0.74.

**Synthesis:** public-speaking anxiety is not a character flaw and is not solved by a universal breathing trick. A course can teach low-risk rehearsal, gradual practice in increasingly demanding settings, realistic preparation, and useful ways to interpret mistakes. It must not present itself as therapy or imply that severe anxiety can be fixed by slide tips.

### 2.10 Evidence integrity and accessibility

**Statistical integrity**

- Wasserstein & Lazar (2016), *The ASA Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose*: https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108

A p-value is not the probability that a hypothesis is true; it does not measure effect size or importance; and scientific or business decisions should not rest solely on whether p crosses a threshold. Artico must teach presenters to show the design, sample size, units, comparison, uncertainty and limitations needed to interpret a result.

**Accessibility**

- W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2: https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag/

Relevant requirements include not using colour as the only means of conveying information, minimum text contrast of 4.5:1 for normal text (3:1 for large text), and captions for prerecorded synchronized media. For a learning course, transcripts and equivalent alternatives should be available. A live presenter should also consider room acoustics, microphone use, audience sightlines and remote participation.

### 2.11 Practitioner frameworks: useful, but label their status

**Garr Reynolds — Presentation Zen**

- https://www.garrreynolds.com/tips
- https://www.garrreynolds.com/

Useful synthesis: restraint in preparation, simplicity in design, naturalness in delivery. This is a professional design philosophy, not a controlled scientific model. Artico should apply the question “what can be removed without damaging meaning?” rather than a blanket “fewer words are always better.”

**Nancy Duarte — Resonate**

- https://www.wiley.com/en-us/resonate-present-visual-stories-that-transform-audiences-p-9781118008935

Useful synthesis for persuasive talks: understand the audience's current situation, help them see a possible alternative, address resistance, and make the desired next step clear. The “audience as hero / speaker as guide” model is a practitioner framework for transformation-oriented communication. It is not appropriate to impose a hero's journey on every scientific update, classroom explanation or ceremonial speech.

**Peak–end caution**

iBiology cites Kahneman et al. (1993), *When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End* (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x), to support the importance of a strong ending. That experiment concerned retrospective evaluations of painful cold-water experiences. It is not direct evidence that a particular presentation ending improves recall. The practical case for a clear ending stands on its own: listeners need to know what the talk concluded and what happens next.

## 3. The integrated Artico model

This model synthesizes the sources; it is not quoted from them.

### 1. FRAME — define the communication situation

- **Occasion:** what kind of event is this, and how long is available?
- **Audience:** what do listeners know, care about, expect, and question?
- **Goal:** what should the audience understand, assess, decide, learn, or feel?
- **Message:** what is the central defensible takeaway?
- **Constraints:** room, platform, time, access needs, language, technology and format.

A topic is not a goal. A goal is not a message. “Talk about protein dynamics” is a topic; “help a mixed-discipline group assess whether this simulation supports a claim about conformational populations” is a goal.

### 2. FOCUS — find the message and its evidence boundary

- Write a complete, defensible central claim.
- Explain why this audience should care.
- Identify the evidence needed to support it.
- Identify what the evidence cannot establish.
- Use the 60/30/15/8-second exercise to discover the core idea, then restore necessary nuance.

### 3. ARCHITECT — build a logical path

Choose a structure that fits the audience's task:

- Question → method → result → interpretation → next question.
- Decision → options → evidence → trade-offs → recommendation.
- Problem → investigation → result → implication.
- Demonstration → observation → explanation → limitation.
- Concept → example → guided practice → check for understanding.
- Shared occasion → specific memory → meaning → recognition.

The introduction, body and ending are useful components, not a compulsory formula. Build the evidence-bearing body early if that helps reveal what background is actually needed.

### 4. RENDER — make the reasoning audible and visible

- Use slide titles that express the point when appropriate.
- Support assertions with visual evidence.
- Orient the audience to axes, units, samples and comparisons.
- Use words, graphics, diagrams, demonstrations or no slides according to the task.
- Define essential terms, use analogies carefully, and preserve technical precision.
- Cite sources, credit collaborators, disclose limitations and make materials accessible.

### 5. DELIVER — create a comprehensible live experience

- Use clear speech, meaningful pace and pauses.
- Connect with listeners; do not simply read the slide deck.
- Check understanding when useful.
- Prepare for nerves, room conditions and technical failure.
- Treat Q&A as scholarly exchange or decision clarification, not a performance duel.

### 6. LEARN — rehearse, collect evidence and revise

- Rehearse the argument without slides, then rehearse with the actual visuals.
- Time the full talk.
- Ask a listener to report the main takeaway and the point where they became confused.
- Get feedback on observable behaviours and the audience's actual task.
- Make one or two high-value revisions and test again.

The loop returns to the frame. A change in audience or purpose can require a different talk, even when the underlying research is unchanged.

## 4. How the model changes by presentation type

| Type | Audience outcome | Useful structure | What must not be sacrificed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scientific conference talk | Understand the contribution and judge the evidence | Question → rationale → methods/results → interpretation → limitations/next questions | Controls, uncertainty, methodological clarity |
| Lab meeting | Decide what to test, debug or prioritise next | Progress → key result → obstacle → decision needed | Negative results, open problems, ownership |
| Thesis defence / job talk | Assess the candidate's reasoning, independence and research contribution | Research programme → key problem → evidence → contribution → future work | Defensibility, methodological understanding, honest boundaries |
| Business decision briefing | Make a decision under constraints | Decision needed → baseline → evidence → risks → recommendation | Costs, assumptions, denominator, alternative explanations |
| Product demonstration | Judge whether a workflow solves a real user problem | User task → live workflow → observed result → limitations → next test | Actual product behaviour and known failure modes |
| Classroom teaching | Build and apply a mental model | Question/phenomenon → explanation → worked example → learner practice → check | Prerequisites, comprehension checks, accuracy |
| Public proposal / persuasive talk | Evaluate a claim and decide whether to act | Need → evidence → options → trade-offs → request | Source quality, fair treatment of objections, audience agency |
| Special-occasion speech | Recognise a person or event and share meaning | Occasion → truthful specific memory → meaning → recognition | Sincerity, respect, privacy |
| Poster / online presentation | Navigate information and engage in dialogue under different constraints | Short orientation → audience-selected depth → discussion | Readability, navigation, accessible alternatives |

A single topic can therefore require very different messages, structures and visual choices. This is not inconsistency; it is audience adaptation.

## 5. Revised Artico curriculum specification

**18 lessons across four modules.** Each lesson should include a learning objective, one central principle, an accurate example, a decision or analysis activity, a practice task, and a way to carry the result into the learner's own plan.

### Module I — FRAME

1. **What makes a talk effective?** Different purposes and observable outcomes; presentation is not synonymous with slide design.
2. **Read the room.** Audience knowledge, interest, expectations, diversity, occasion and constraints.
3. **Goal, message and “so what”.** Separate topic, intended outcome, core claim and audience relevance.
4. **Discover the message; find your voice.** Half-Life Your Message, nuance, reflection and individual speaking style.

### Module II — ARCHITECT

5. **A talk is not a paper.** Select, order and omit material for a live audience without concealing important evidence.
6. **Build the reasoning path.** Narrative as logic; outline options; turning points; iterative and body-first planning.
7. **Make the body carry the claim.** For each result: why the method, what the visual shows, what can be inferred, what remains uncertain, and what follows.
8. **Open with relevance.** Research question, optional hook, audience-specific significance and essential background.
9. **Close and title the talk.** Core message, key findings, implications, next questions and a title that accurately invites the right audience.

### Module III — RENDER

10. **Slides that make a point.** Assertion–evidence design, message titles, visual hierarchy and when no slides are better.
11. **Data that can be inspected.** Axes, units, samples, denominators, uncertainty, scale, comparisons and statistical interpretation.
12. **Words that travel.** Spoken syntax, signposting, jargon, definitions, analogy, examples and repetition.
13. **Story, attention and ethical persuasion.** Narrative structure, optional vignettes, evidence versus emotion, fair argument, attribution and source integrity.

### Module IV — DELIVER & LEARN

14. **Speak so people can follow.** Voice, rate versus idea-pace, pauses, gestures, presence and authentic style.
15. **Rehearse and use feedback.** Timed runs, recordings, audience-matched observers, specific feedback and revision.
16. **Nerves, stagecraft and contingencies.** Gradual practice, realistic preparation, venue and technology, accessibility and recovery.
17. **Q&A as dialogue.** Listen, clarify, answer, distinguish what is known, and follow up honestly.
18. **Build, present and revise.** A complete capstone in a real or selected context, evaluated against audience-specific criteria.

### Cumulative Presentation Plan

The existing simple blueprint should be expanded into a real working document with these fields:

1. Occasion, format and time limit
2. Audience profile and prior knowledge
3. Audience interests, concerns and likely objections
4. Accessibility, venue and technology constraints
5. One or two presentation goals
6. Core message (one defensible sentence)
7. Audience-specific “so what”
8. Evidence that supports the message
9. Evidence limitations and open questions
10. Chosen structure and logical sequence
11. Research narrative or explanatory path
12. Optional story vignette / hook
13. Introduction and necessary background
14. Body sections: claim, evidence, explanation, implication
15. Conclusion and next step
16. Talk title
17. Slide/visual plan
18. Data-figure integrity checklist
19. Jargon, definitions and analogies
20. Delivery and speaking-style priorities
21. Rehearsal plan
22. Feedback observers and evaluation questions
23. Likely Q&A and evidence-based responses
24. Venue/technology fallback
25. Feedback received
26. Revisions made and next presentation goal

This plan is not just a form. It is the learner's evolving work product. It should support repeated presentations, context changes and revisions over time.

### Assessment and feedback

Avoid treating multiple-choice correctness as proof of speaking skill. The course should combine:

- Short knowledge checks for conceptual distinctions.
- Original case analysis for audience and purpose.
- A cumulative written blueprint.
- A spoken practice task (recording remains optional and local).
- Feedback from someone who represents the target audience.
- A revision note explaining what changed and why.

A useful assessment rubric:

| Dimension | What the reviewer should look for |
|---|---|
| Purpose and audience | Is the intended outcome clear and appropriate? |
| Core message | Can the listener state the central claim accurately? |
| Reasoning and evidence | Do the examples/data support the claim without overstatement? |
| Structure | Can the listener follow why each part comes next? |
| Explanation | Are necessary concepts defined and understandable? |
| Visuals and accessibility | Are visuals legible, accurate and useful? |
| Delivery | Can the listener hear and follow the speaker? |
| Interaction / Q&A | Does the speaker respond honestly and usefully? |
| Timing | Does the talk fit its real constraint? |

Use a rubric to guide specific feedback, not to pretend that a single number fully captures a speaker's ability.

## 6. Editorial and implementation safeguards

1. Do not teach “one perfect formula” for all presentations.
2. Do not confuse a dramatic hook with a meaningful introduction.
3. Do not make storytelling a substitute for evidence.
4. Do not remove necessary detail merely to achieve minimal slides.
5. Do not teach fixed rules for gestures, eye contact, speed, slide counts or the number of main points without evidence and context.
6. Do not treat a p-value as effect size, proof, or the probability a hypothesis is true.
7. Do not invent empirical results in examples. Clearly label synthetic training data.
8. Do not treat anxiety as a weakness or promise that presentation tips are treatment.
9. Do not reward charisma over understanding, accuracy, relevance and ethics.
10. Do not copy iBiology's protected course materials; create original Artico explanations, exercises, visuals and case examples with source links.
11. Provide keyboard-usable controls, readable contrast, non-colour cues, captions/transcripts for audio/video, and printable/exportable learning notes.
12. Preserve the distinction between **source-derived findings**, **professional guidance**, **practitioner heuristics**, and **Artico's own synthesis**.

- iBiology, *Share Your Research: How to Give a Good Talk* — full public course document and educator index. Original materials CC BY-NC-ND 3.0.
- University of Minnesota Libraries Publishing, *Stand Up, Speak Out: The Practice and Ethics of Public Speaking* — open textbook, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0.
- Stanford Oral Communication Program — professional guidance and exercises for content, delivery, visuals, anxiety and scientific presentations.
- Michael Alley, *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*, 2nd ed. (2013), DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4419-8279-7.
- Garr Reynolds, *Presentation Zen* / Presentation Zen Tips — practitioner design philosophy.
- Nancy Duarte, *Resonate* (Wiley) — practitioner framework for audience-centred persuasive storytelling.
