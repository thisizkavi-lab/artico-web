# CH94 — Messaging and Video Calls

**You can:** send a useful short message, manage a group conversation, and ask for a change during a video call.  

## Study

### A short message still needs useful information

A text or chat message can be brief, but the reader needs to know what you want them to do.

> **Ava:** Traffic's barely moving. I'll be about 20 minutes late. Please start lunch without me.  
> **Ben:** Okay. Should we order anything for you?  
> **Ava:** A veggie sandwich, please. Thanks!  
> **Ben:** Got it. See you soon.  

“I'll be late” gives less help than a likely delay and a request. If you do not know your arrival time, say so: “I'm stuck in traffic. I'm not sure when I'll get there. I'll update you.” Do not invent a precise time just to complete the message.

A message marked read does not tell you that the person agrees. If the decision matters, ask for a response: “Can you confirm by noon?” You can also choose another channel: “Is it easier to talk for a minute?”

### Abbreviations and tone

These spellings appear in informal messages. You can use ones that fit your relationship and style; none is required.

| Form | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| BTW | “By the way”: introduces an extra point |
| IDK | “I don't know” |
| TBH | “To be honest”: introduces a candid opinion |
| OMG | “Oh my God”: an exclamation of surprise or strong feeling |
| LOL | “Laughing out loud”: can signal amusement or a playful tone |
| RU | “Are you”: shortened spelling in a question |

“LOL” does not prove someone is literally laughing. “OMG” can sound more intense than “Oh!” Choose the effect you want rather than inserting an abbreviation into every message. “RU free?” and “Are you free?” ask the same basic question, but the shorter spelling gives a different written style.

Punctuation, capitalization, and a reader's expectations can affect how a message feels. Compare “okay,” “Okay.” and “Okay!” You cannot reliably decide that the sender is angry from a period alone. If their meaning matters, ask: “Does that plan work for you?”

### A group chat

> **Maya:** Should we add Leo to the hiking chat?  
> **Owen:** Sure. Could you ask him first? He's trying to reduce notifications.  
> **Maya:** I'll ask. Leo, do you want to join the group, or should I send you the plan separately?  
> **Leo:** Separately, please. I'm still interested in Saturday.  
> **Maya:** No problem. I'll send you the meeting place.  

To ask for a useful link, say, “Could you message me the campsite link?” A reply can be “Sure, I'll send it after we choose the site.”

Joining the group and joining the activity are different choices. Someone can want the information without wanting every conversation. “Could you send me just the final plan?” gives that preference clearly.

### A video call

Describe what you can actually see or hear.

> **Grandpa:** Can you see me?  
> **Riley:** I can see the top of your head, but not your face. Could you tilt the tablet down a little?  
> **Grandpa:** Like this?  
> **Riley:** Yes, that's better. I can see your face now.  

Other useful lines: “Your picture froze,” “I can see you, but I can't hear you,” and “Could you move a little closer to the microphone?” Say whether a suggested change helped. “Still no sound. Should we switch to a phone call?” gives a next choice without claiming a guaranteed technical fix.

## Practice

### 1. Write two different updates

Fictional situation: lunch starts at noon. You expect to arrive at 12:15 and want the others to wait. Write that request. One possibility: “I'll be about 15 minutes late. Could you wait for me?”

Now you do not know when you will arrive and want them to start. Change both the information and the request: “I'm delayed and don't know when I'll get there. Please start without me.”

Partner replies: first, “We can wait until 12:15.” Then, in a new attempt, “We need to start at noon.” Respond to each. “Thanks, I'll keep you updated” and “Okay, go ahead. I'll join you when I can” fit the different replies.

### 2. Choose your written voice

Write a casual opinion about a fictional movie: you enjoyed the acting but thought it was too long. Try it with and without TBH: “TBH, I liked the acting, but it was too long.” / “I liked the acting, but it was too long.” Neither version needs an abbreviation to be complete.

### 3. Repair a call and a group arrangement

Cue: “Can you hear me?” Your answer must show that you can hear the person but their picture is frozen. Possible answer: “I can hear you, but your picture froze.” Partner: “How about now?” It is still frozen; report that and suggest another channel.

Then answer an invitation to a group chat when you want only the meeting details. Cover the study model before replying.

**Solo route:** write the messages and read the supplied partner cues aloud before answering. Change the delay or the picture problem for another round. Check whether your response matches the actual facts and gives the other person a usable next step.
