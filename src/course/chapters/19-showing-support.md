# CH19 — Showing Support

**Your goal:** Acknowledge a difficulty, encourage someone without promising an outcome, and offer or respond to practical help.  

## Study: Acknowledge before suggesting

**That sounds hard** recognizes a difficult experience. **I'm sorry you're dealing with that** expresses concern, not responsibility for causing it. A person may want listening, help, advice or space; ask if you do not know.

- **Do you want to talk about it?** Invite conversation.
- **Would it help to talk, or would you rather have some space?** Offer a choice.
- **Do you want advice, or do you want me to listen?** Clarify the kind of support.
- **I know this hasn't been easy.** Acknowledge difficulty you know about.

**Before an exam**

**Em:** I'm stressed about the exam next week.  
**Luis:** That sounds hard. Do you want to talk about it?  
**Em:** I keep worrying I'll forget everything.  
**Luis:** Would it help if I practiced the questions with you?  
**Em:** Yes. That would help.  

Luis offers a specific action. The words do not guarantee an exam result, but they give Em a useful next step.

## Study: Encourage effort

| Expression | A possible use |
| --- | --- |
| **Hang in there.** | Encourage someone to continue through difficulty. |
| **It's worth a try. / It's worth a shot.** | Encourage an attempt without guaranteeing success. |
| **You've got this.** | Express confidence in the person for the task. |
| **We've got this.** | Express shared confidence in a group task. |
| **You've already made progress.** | Recognize progress when there is evidence for it. |

**Considering a team tryout**

**Em:** I'm not sure I should try out.  
**Luis:** I think it's worth a shot. You've been practicing a lot.  
**Em:** True. I could give it a try.  
**Luis:** I can go with you if you'd like.  

Strong encouragement can express belief in someone. It need not become **I know you'll win** when you do not know the outcome. If **Hang in there** feels too brief for the situation, add acknowledgment or a concrete offer.

## Study: Make a usable offer

- **I can help for half an hour this evening.** Give a specific availability.
- **Would you like me to bring dinner?** Offer a concrete action.
- **Let me know if you'd like company.** Offer companionship.
- **We're here for you.** Express shared support.
- **We've got your back.** Informally express that you support the person.

**A friend overwhelmed by moving**

**Em:** I still have so much to pack.  
**Luis:** I can help with a couple of boxes Saturday morning.  
**Em:** That would be great. Thank you.  
**Luis:** What time should I come?  
**Em:** Ten would work.  

A limited offer can still be useful. **You can talk to me anytime** expresses broad availability; use it only if it matches what you want to offer. It is not required to show care.

## Study: Respond honestly to support

**Thanks. That means a lot** says the support matters. **I really appreciate it** expresses gratitude. You can also decline or redirect:

- **Thanks, but I'd rather be alone tonight.** Ask for space.
- **I don't need advice right now. Could you just listen?** State what would help.
- **Could you help me with one box instead?** Change the task.

Optional informal **That sucks** can express that the situation is bad: **You missed the last bus? That sucks**. In this scene it criticizes the situation, not the person. It is a stronger casual response; a fuller acknowledgment may communicate more when someone is sharing serious distress.

## Practice: Offer the right kind of help

The fictional tasks require no specialist advice. Speak both roles alone, or alternate with a partner.

1. A friend says **I'm worried about my presentation**. Acknowledge the feeling and offer rehearsal. Support: **Would it help to practice it with me?** Their reply is **Yes, please**.
2. Your friend is considering a tryout and has practiced regularly. Encourage the attempt without guaranteeing selection.
3. You can help a moving friend Saturday from ten to eleven. Offer that availability. They need help with two boxes. Agree on the task and time.
4. Change the response: they say **Thanks, but I'd rather do it alone**. Acknowledge that choice.
5. You are the person receiving advice and want listening instead. Explain that need. The other person's reply is **Okay. I'm listening**.
6. Hide Study. Rehearse a difficulty, acknowledgment, specific offer and acceptance or refusal. Change the kind of support on your next attempt.

### Sample possibilities

1. **That sounds stressful. Would it help to practice it with me? — Yes, please.**
2. **I think it's worth a shot. You've been practicing.**
3. **I can help from ten to eleven Saturday. — Could you help with two boxes? — Sure. I'll come at ten.**
4. **Okay. Let me know if that changes.**
5. **I don't need advice right now. Could you just listen? — Okay. I'm listening.**

**Check:** Did you acknowledge the difficulty rather than erase it? Was your offer realistic for the role? Could the person accept, decline or request a different kind of help?
