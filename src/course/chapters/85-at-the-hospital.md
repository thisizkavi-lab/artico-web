# CH85 — At the Hospital

**Outcome:** Identify your visit, give important information, ask about a procedure, and report recovery needs.  

## Study

A **department** is a hospital service area. **Outpatient** describes care without an overnight admission under that arrangement. **Admission** here means being accepted into hospital care. **Observation** means monitoring or assessment. An **operating room** is used for surgery; **ICU**, intensive care unit, names a unit providing intensive care. A **ward / unit** is an area for a particular group of patients.

### Arrive and identify the purpose

**Patient:** I have a scan appointment at two thirty under Sam Rivera.  
**Employee:** Could I see your confirmation?  
**Patient:** Here it is. Which department do I need?  
**Employee:** Imaging. Take this corridor to the reception desk there.  
**Patient:** Is there a step-free route?  
**Employee:** Yes, I'll show you where the elevator is.  

A **scan** is an imaging examination; ask which type if you do not know. For urgent help, say “I need help urgently” and describe the problem rather than waiting to complete a routine appointment script. Ask staff how to reach the appropriate clinical team. Emergency reporting is developed in Chapter 84.

### Share relevant information and ask about assessment

**Nurse:** When did the pain start?  
**Patient:** About two hours ago. I have the names of my regular medicines here.  
**Nurse:** Have you had any allergic reactions?  
**Patient:** I've had a reaction to penicillin. I can describe what happened.  
**Nurse:** Thank you. I'll record that and ask some more questions.  
**Patient:** Could you arrange an interpreter? I want to understand the explanation.  

Use your actual history in care, not the model's history. “I don't know the medicine's name, but I have the packaging” describes a useful limitation. “Could I call someone?” asks for contact with a support person. “How soon can someone assess me?” asks about the next step without presuming a priority or wait time.

### Understand a proposed stay or procedure

**Clinician:** We'd like to keep you overnight for observation.  
**Patient:** What are you monitoring, and what would happen next?  
**Clinician:** I'll explain what we know and the plan we're considering.  
**Patient:** Does that mean surgery is being considered?  
**Clinician:** Let's discuss that separately so the options are clear.  

**Surgery / operation** names a procedure involving surgical treatment. **Anesthesia** relates to reducing or preventing sensation during care; different types have different effects. Ask “What type are you proposing?” rather than treating every use as being fully unconscious. **Blood test**, **temperature**, **pulse**, and **blood pressure** name assessments you may hear about.

Before a procedure, useful questions are “What is the procedure for?” “What are the alternatives?” “What do I need to know about preparation?” “Who can answer my remaining questions?” “How will pain be managed?” The model does not supply individualized consent, preparation, or treatment advice.

### Recovery and discharge

**Nurse:** How are you feeling now?  
**Patient:** Still groggy, and the pain has increased.  
**Nurse:** Tell me where it hurts, and I'll contact the team.  
**Patient:** Around the incision. Could someone review it with me?  

**Groggy** means dazed or not fully alert. **Incision** is a surgical cut. “I feel light-headed” reports a feeling of faintness or dizziness. “I need help getting up” describes a need; it does not assert you have permission to walk independently.

**Patient:** Before I leave, could we review the discharge instructions?  
**Employee:** Yes. What would you like clarified?  
**Patient:** The medicines, follow-up appointment, and who to contact if I notice a problem.  
**Employee:** Let's go through those details with the clinical team.  
**Patient:** Could I have that information in writing?  

**Discharge** means release from a care setting under its arrangements; **follow-up** is further contact or care. Ask “Are there any results still pending?” **Pending** means not yet completed or available. For a visitor, ask “What are the visiting hours for this unit?” rather than assuming one rule for the whole hospital.

## Practice

Fictional role A: Sam Rivera, scan at 2:30, confirmation available, step-free route needed. Role B: pain started two hours ago, medicine list available, prior penicillin reaction. No diagnosis, treatment, or discharge plan is selected.

1. Check in for Role A. Partner asks for the confirmation. Identify the visit and ask for the route.
2. Give Role B's history. Partner asks onset, medicines, and reactions. Supply only the stated information.
3. Partner says overnight observation is proposed. Ask what it involves and whether surgery is being discussed; do not treat one as automatically meaning the other.
4. Report increased pain during recovery. Ask for review. Then ask three questions about a proposed discharge without inventing medical instructions.

Solo: read the staff prompts and answer out loud, then summarize your own questions with the models hidden. Change the visit type on a later attempt.

**Possibilities:** “What are you monitoring overnight?” “I'm still groggy, and the pain is worse.” “Who should I contact after discharge?” Check that the visit, history, current need, and unanswered question are clear. No recovery outcome is guaranteed by the models.
