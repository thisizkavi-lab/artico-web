# CH60 — Household Chores

**Outcome:** Discuss who does a chore, ask about progress, and renegotiate an arrangement clearly.  

## Study

Useful chores include **do the dishes**, **do the laundry**, **vacuum**, **sweep the floor**, **take out the trash**, **empty the dishwasher**, **clean the bathroom**, **tidy up**, and **make the bed**. **Tidy up** means put things in order; **clean** may involve removing dirt. Learn the combinations rather than assuming one verb works with every task: **do the laundry**, but **make the bed**.

A **chore** is a regular household task. A **chore chart** or **schedule** records an arrangement. **Your turn** means the task is assigned to you at that point in the arrangement; it does not prove everyone agrees that the arrangement is fair.

### Ask about activity and responsibility

**A:** What are you up to?  
**B:** I'm making a chore schedule. We keep forgetting whose turn it is.  
**A:** What do you want to change?  
**B:** I'd like us to write down who does the dishes each evening.  
**A:** Okay. Let's talk about which nights work for each of us.  

Here **What are you up to?** asks what the person is doing. It is not automatically the same as a quick **What's up?** greeting.

For practice, this household agrees: Alex does Monday's dishes; Tessa does Tuesday's; both handle their own laundry; Alex empties the dishwasher Wednesday. These are scene conditions, not a rule for dividing household work.

**Tessa:** Whose turn is it to do the dishes tonight?  
**Alex:** It's Monday, so mine.  
**Tessa:** Thanks. Could you also put the clean dishes away?  
**Alex:** I can, but I need to finish something first. I'll do it after dinner.  
**Tessa:** Okay. Thanks for letting me know.  

### Ask about progress or help

**Have you finished the laundry?** asks whether the task is complete. Supported responses include **Not yet**, **Nearly**, **I've finished washing it, but I haven't put it away**, and **Yes, it's done**.

**A:** Have you finished the laundry?  
**B:** Nearly. I still need to put it away.  
**A:** Would you like some help?  
**B:** Yes, please. Could you put the towels in the bathroom?  
**A:** Sure.  

**Still need to...** identifies the remaining part. **Could you help me with...?** asks for cooperation. When you cannot help: **I can't right now. I could help after eight** offers a time instead of implying immediate agreement.

### Discuss a disagreement

**Tessa:** I did the dishes yesterday, and I don't think the chart reflects that.  
**Alex:** I thought it was your turn tonight, too.  
**Tessa:** That's not what I understood. Can we check the arrangement?  
**Alex:** Yes. The chart says Tuesday, but we changed it yesterday.  
**Tessa:** Then let's update it. I don't want us to keep having this argument.  

A firm boundary is **I can't take on all the cleaning this week**. A proposal is **Could we split the bathroom and kitchen tasks?** Distinguish a request for help from assigning responsibility without discussion.

## Practice

Use the household chart and speak both roles alone, or switch roles with a partner.

1. It is Monday. Ask whose turn it is to do the dishes. Give Alex's answer and a planned time.
2. Laundry is washed but not put away. Answer a progress question and request help with the towels.
3. You cannot help now but can help after eight. Respond to **Can you help with the dishes?**
4. Yesterday's swap was not recorded. State the misunderstanding and propose checking and updating the chart.

**Sample possibilities:** “It's my turn. I'll do it after dinner.” / “I've finished washing it, but I still need to put it away. Could you help with the towels?” / “Not right now, but I could help after eight.” / “That's not what I understood. Let's check the arrangement and update the chart.”  

Return later and change the chart or unfinished task. Check whether you communicated ownership, progress and a request separately rather than letting one stand for all three.
