## Eight claims and the useful teaching point

### 1. “Use lots of high-level vocabulary” — use precise, appropriate vocabulary

The video rejects the idea that loading an essay with C1/C2 words automatically raises the score. Its electric-car comparison shows how a simpler sentence can communicate a relevant idea more clearly than an inaccurate stack of fancy words. Preserve this principle: teach range together with accuracy, spelling, collocation, meaning, and appropriacy. The official Lexical Resource criterion assesses all of these; it does not give a bonus for using the largest possible number of advanced words. ([0:28–6:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=28s); [official IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

### 2. “Use lots of advanced linking words” — organize ideas; use cohesive devices where they help

The video shows a paragraph where frequent linkers produce repetition and mechanical progression. Teach learners to make the relationship between ideas clear, using linking words, reference, substitution, and paragraph structure as needed. Do not count linkers or require every sentence to begin with one. IELTS describes cohesion as using cohesive devices appropriately; its advice explicitly warns against putting a linking word in every sentence. ([6:29–13:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=389s); [IELTS guidance on high-scoring essays](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/10-steps-to-writing-high-scoring-ielts-essays))

### 3. “Range matters more than accuracy” — aim for both; the video invents a numerical threshold

The source is right to reject a range-versus-accuracy tradeoff: the grammar criterion considers the range, appropriacy, and accuracy of structures. Use a mix of structures the learner can control and extend range through task-relevant sentences, rather than forcing complex forms. However, its interpretation that “frequent error-free sentences” means **more than 50%** of all sentences are error-free is not stated in the descriptor. Do not teach a numeric threshold that the rubric does not provide. ([13:01–20:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=781s); [official IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

### 4. “Never repeat a word” — vary vocabulary when a reliable alternative exists

The video correctly warns that forced synonym swaps can introduce errors in meaning, spelling, or collocation. Let necessary topic words repeat when that is the clearest option; choose a more precise alternative when the learner knows it fits. Vocabulary range matters, but replacing every repeated word with an uncertain synonym is not a sound rule. ([20:18–25:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=1218s); [IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

### 5. “Add a hook and memorize a template” — answer the prompt directly

The worked introduction shows how a generic hook and prewritten claims can add irrelevant content, introduce grammar errors, and force the body paragraphs to defend unsupported statements. Teach learners to paraphrase the prompt accurately, establish the position or response the question requires, and keep the introduction aligned with what the essay actually develops. Avoid memorized essays and canned content. Distinguish that from a flexible structural scaffold: organizing an answer into an introduction, body paragraphs, and a conclusion is a planning tool, not by itself a memorized answer. The video’s assertion that any memorized template automatically receives Band 0 is too broad; IELTS warns against plagiarized or wholly memorized responses, but this is not an automatic Band 0 rule for every familiar structure or phrase. ([25:16–31:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=1516s); [British Council Writing advice](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/prepare/ielts-academic/writing); [IELTS Writing criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf))

### 6. “Use statistics and surveys” — support ideas with relevant examples; do not invent research

The video is right that a made-up percentage can undermine an example and distract from the argument. It then turns a sensible caution into an absolute ban on statistics. Official Task 2 guidance invites relevant examples from a candidate’s own knowledge or experience. IDP says statistics are not necessary and specifically warns against invented statistics; a precise figure adds little if the learner cannot support it. Teach clear illustrative examples and reasoning rather than fabricated studies or obligatory percentages. ([31:03–34:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=1863s); [IELTS Writing Task 2 format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing); [IDP guidance on examples and statistics](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-essays-impactful-examples))

### 7. “Use lots of idioms” — idiomatic items are assessed when appropriate

The video correctly warns against inserting a quota of conversational idioms into an academic essay. Its claim that the Writing rubric is silent on idioms is wrong: the official Writing Band Descriptors explicitly mention skilful use of uncommon and/or idiomatic items **when appropriate**, and the Lexical Resource criteria include control of idiomatic expressions. Teach idiomatic language as one optional part of precise, natural vocabulary, with register and task fit as checks. No idiom count guarantees a band. ([34:38–37:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=2078s); [official IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

### 8. “More words mean a higher score” — meet the minimum and develop the answer fully

IELTS Task 2 requires **at least 250 words**. Exactly 250 satisfies the stated minimum; the video’s instruction to write “more than 250” is unnecessarily strict. More words do not earn a bonus by themselves. A very long answer can cost time, reduce checking, or drift away from the prompt, but there is no official 300-word ceiling. The 300-word point is the instructor’s practical heuristic, not a scoring boundary. Aim for enough words to answer every part, extend and support ideas, and leave time to review. ([37:09–38:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=684xymRpBc0&t=2229s); [official IELTS Task 2 format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing))

## Transferable checklist for the course

Before accepting a Writing tip, ask which official criterion it affects and what the learner would do differently on the page. Prefer advice that improves task response, relevance, organization, clarity, vocabulary precision, or grammatical control. Avoid point-counting rules for vocabulary, linkers, idioms, or sentences; avoid unsupported percentages; and distinguish a useful structure from memorized answer content. The video’s own examples and VIP-student claims are instructional demonstrations or anecdotes, not official scoring rules or independent outcome studies.

## Related video: “The ONLY IELTS Writing Task 2 Tips You Need in 2026”

The 46-minute companion demonstrates seven tips using two deliberately contrasting introductions, sample paragraphs, and a conclusion. Its practical theme is accurate, appropriate writing over forced complexity. The source’s private essay sets and NotebookLM searches are not published, so its numerical analyses and claims about outcomes remain unverified.

1. **“High-level vocabulary improves your score.”** The side-by-side exercise contrasts a natural introduction with one forced to include a list of advanced words. The latter uses items such as *proliferation* with the wrong implication and *ubiquitous* without support for a universal claim. Teach meaning, scope, collocation, spelling, and register along with range; there is no C1/C2 quota. ([0:25–11:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0zhWWHYNpk&t=25s))
2. **“Linking words improve your score.”** The student is prompted to overpack one paragraph with connectives, showing how devices can become repetitive, mechanical, or semantically mismatched. Preserve the accurate point: use cohesion and linkers when they clarify an actual relationship; do not add them to reach a count or begin every sentence with one. ([11:37–18:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0zhWWHYNpk&t=697s))
3. **“Grammatical range is more important than accuracy.”** The video correctly notes that the official criterion considers both range and accuracy, and models how a learner may communicate the same idea with controlled sentence forms. Its claims that half or more of every essay’s sentences must be error-free, that assessors can simply count them, that a grammar score is determined by the lower of two sub-scores, and that grammar errors mechanically lower other criteria are not established by the descriptors. Teach increasing range while retaining control; do not convert “frequent error-free sentences” into a numerical threshold. ([18:55–28:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0zhWWHYNpk&t=1135s); [official IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

([40:13–44:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0zhWWHYNpk&t=2413s); [official IELTS Writing descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf))

The 260–300 range is a coaching heuristic, not a maximum, a guaranteed sweet spot, or a different official minimum. Teach candidates to meet the requirement, cover every part, and leave time to check rather than drafting to a narrow target. ([44:17–46:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P0zhWWHYNpk&t=2657s); [official IELTS Academic Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing); [official IELTS General Training Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-writing))

The video also presents a list-heavy “advanced vocabulary” exercise, forced linker use, and an essay exceeding 300 words. These are deliberately constructed demonstrations, not a controlled study. Its NotebookLM search of hundreds of VIP interviews, 100-essay lexical analysis, claims about teachers or students, and 6.5-to-8 score anecdotes cannot be audited from the video. The “100 rule”—use only words you are 100% sure about—is a test-day heuristic, not an IELTS threshold. Teach learners to prefer an accurate, appropriate word over an uncertain display word, while still building and demonstrating lexical range. Similarly, avoiding clichés and memorized chunks is useful when they make an answer generic or irrelevant; do not tell learners that every familiar phrase automatically loses marks. A clear message must still respond fully to the task and meet all four Writing criteria.
