## The reusable routine

The repeated routine is: **understand the task → predict the kind of information → locate the relevant passage → read that part closely → answer and check.** The instructor calls this “where before what”: find the answer’s likely location before deciding what the answer is. It works well for many local-detail tasks, but it should not be forced onto tasks that require a global reading of paragraphs, such as headings.

- Read the item wording and instructions. Check how many answers to give and the word limit.
- Understand the whole question or statement before hunting for matching words. Generate likely paraphrases rather than expecting exact wording.
- Scan for the relevant location. Read the local passage carefully and make the decision from what it says.
- For completion tasks, check grammar, answer form, spelling, and the word limit. Copy wording exactly when the instructions require words from the passage.
- Manage time by leaving an item that is consuming too much time, then return if possible. The instructor’s proposed order for question sets is a practice option, not a fixed paper rule.

## The 12 routines in the video

### 1. Sentence completion

Read the instructions and incomplete sentences first; predict the answer meaning and grammatical form; consider synonyms; scan for the location; read that section closely; then fill the gap with the required word(s) and verify spelling. The video stresses that a copied answer can still lose credit if misspelled. ([1:23–4:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=83s))

### 2. Summary completion

Read the instructions and the summary before the passage. Predict both likely content and part of speech (noun, verb, adjective); locate the relevant passage through paraphrases; read closely; check that the proposed answer fits the meaning and grammar of the sentence. ([4:13–6:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=253s))

### 3. Multiple choice

Read the question and options carefully, then explain to yourself how the options differ. The teacher suggests skimming for the passage’s general meaning, locating the evidence for each question, reading that section closely, and eliminating options that the text rules out. If uncertain, narrow the choices before deciding. Do not select an option just because it is plausible in the real world. ([6:24–9:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=384s))

### 4. Short answer

First identify exactly what the question asks. Mark useful names or noun phrases and think of paraphrases; scan for the relevant location; read that section closely; return to the question wording and answer concisely within the stated limit. The instructor calls this format rare, but the current official Academic list includes it. ([9:22–11:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=562s))

### 5. Diagram labelling

Read the instructions and inspect the diagram before reading. Work out the arrows, labels, and what each gap refers to; do not panic if the object is unfamiliar because the task tests reading the description and relating it to the visual. Predict the kind of label, locate the relevant text, read closely, and check spelling and answer limits. ([11:25–13:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=685s))

### 6. True / False / Not Given

Read each complete statement and restate its meaning; generate paraphrases; locate its evidence; then compare the whole statement against the relevant passage, not a single matching keyword. **True** means the text agrees, **False** means it contradicts, and **Not Given** means the text neither confirms nor contradicts it. Do not use outside knowledge. Stop searching once the passage provides no basis to confirm or contradict the proposition. ([13:08–18:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=788s); [official IELTS definitions](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading))

### 7. Yes / No / Not Given

Use the same evidence discipline, but assess the **writer’s view or claim**, not whether a factual statement is true. Identify whose view is being reported; a source, researcher, or character quoted by the writer is not automatically the writer’s position. **Yes** agrees with the writer, **No** contradicts the writer, and **Not Given** is neither confirmed nor contradicted. Do not use outside knowledge. ([18:07–20:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1087s); [official IELTS definitions](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading))

### 8. Matching sentence endings

Understand the sentence beginnings and predict what kind of ending could complete each idea. Read the options, match any clear cases, eliminate endings that cannot work, and use the text to decide between the remaining plausible options. Grammar can help check a choice, but logic or grammatical fit alone does not prove it is supported by the passage. ([20:17–22:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1217s))

### 9. Matching names / features

Scan for the names in the passage and mark where each occurs. Read the surrounding evidence before matching it to a statement. The teacher suggests starting with names mentioned only once because their evidence may be easier to isolate, then removing already matched statements from consideration. These are optional efficiency tactics; follow the task instructions because some matching tasks permit reuse of an option. ([22:03–24:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1323s); [official IELTS matching-features guidance](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading))

### 10. Matching information to paragraphs

This task asks where specific information appears, so the instructor treats it as a broad search. He recommends understanding the statements and their synonyms, skimming for the structure and topic of the whole passage, then scanning paragraph by paragraph and marking a match. He suggests doing it after other questions on that passage so the learner already knows the text. That order is a personal time-management heuristic; compare it in practice with the official paper’s layout and the learner’s own accuracy. Move on quickly when a paragraph does not contain the target information. ([24:16–27:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1456s))

### 11. Table / flow-chart completion

Check the instructions and answer limit; use the labels and sequence in the table or flow chart to find the relevant paragraph; read it closely; transfer the answer in the form the task requires; check spelling. The video groups table and flow-chart completion together. IELTS also lists note completion and summary completion, so teach these as related completion formats with their own task instructions. ([27:07–28:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1627s); [official IELTS Academic Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading))

### 12. Matching headings

The task tests the paragraph’s main idea, not a keyword match. The instructor’s routine is to read a paragraph and write a short heading in your own words before looking at the supplied headings; then compare meaning, match obvious cases, and read remaining paragraphs more closely to distinguish close options. He recommends doing headings first because it gives a whole-passage overview. Treat that order and the “write your own heading first” step as a strategy to test, not a rule: IELTS materials also teach heading-first review and topic-sentence skimming, and a full slow read can cost time. ([28:20–31:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=1700s); [IELTS teacher guidance on matching headings](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-prepare-learners-for-matching-headings-task-on-the-ielts-reading-test))

### Related Short: a quick matching-headings routine

A related 35-second Short, [Simple Tip for Matching Headings in IELTS Reading](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/apQ3r2iou-U), repeats this method: skim each paragraph, write a one-line heading, then compare it with the options. Captions cover 0:00–0:30 of the clip. The Short claims headings will match “90% of the time”; this is not substantiated and should not be used as a prediction or accuracy promise. The transferable exercise is the attempt to understand each paragraph’s gist before comparing options.

## What to teach as exam fact, and what to keep as a coach’s method

- IELTS Academic Reading is 60 minutes, 40 questions, across three sections. The Reading tests assess a range of skills; Academic and General Training use different texts, while official IELTS describes the core question types as common to both versions. ([IELTS Academic Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading); [British Council Reading overview](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/en-cm/what-is-ielts/how-it-works/test-format/reading))
- The teacher’s “12 types” is a useful lesson map, not a complete official taxonomy. Current IELTS material lists additional types such as matching features and note completion; it also treats table, flow-chart, and summary completion as related forms. The video’s “matching names” corresponds broadly to matching features.
- The distinction between factual T/F/NG and writer-view Y/N/NG, the meaning of “not given,” and the need to rely on the passage are confirmed by official guidance.
- “Where before what,” eliminating wrong choices, beginning with headings, leaving matching information until last, and prioritizing names mentioned once are instructor strategies. Let learners compare their speed and accuracy across practice sets; do not present the proposed order as a universal test instruction.
- The opening claim that the instructor achieved Band 9 in 20 minutes and that these exact methods helped thousands of students is his personal/promotional claim, not independently verified evidence. The actual Academic Reading test allows 60 minutes. Teach the procedures; do not sell 20-minute completion or a guaranteed band score. ([0:27–1:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3KDP8P-pvEw&t=27s); [IELTS Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading))

## Course adaptation

For each task family, teach (1) what the task asks for, (2) a timed worked example showing how to locate and verify evidence, and (3) a short error review. Track whether misses came from misunderstanding the prompt, missing a paraphrase, choosing a plausible but unsupported answer, misreading the passage, spelling, or exceeding the word limit. Teach skimming and scanning as purposeful tools alongside close reading; no single keyword trick substitutes for understanding the question and checking the passage.
