# CH84 — Injuries and Emergencies

**Outcome:** State an urgent problem and location, answer key emergency questions, and report an injury without choosing its diagnosis.  

## Study

In the United States, **911** is the emergency number. This lesson is for reporting and asking for help; it is not first-aid or rescue training. Practice the examples locally without placing a real call.

### Make the essential facts clear

**Call-taker:** What's the location of the emergency?  
**Caller:** We're at 24 Cedar Street, apartment three, Harbor City.  
**Call-taker:** Tell me what happened.  
**Caller:** My husband has severe chest pain. He's here with me.  
**Call-taker:** What number can we reach you on?  
**Caller:** My callback number is 202-555-0118.  
**Call-taker:** I'll ask you some more questions.  
**Caller:** Okay. Please repeat anything I don't understand.  

The address, callback number, and people are fictional. Give your actual location, callback information, and observed details in a real emergency. National 911 guidance describes these question categories and says to follow the call-taker's instructions and remain on the call until told to end it. The model's exact question order is not universal.

Short urgent reports include “I need an ambulance,” “Someone fell,” “There's a fire,” “She's bleeding,” “He's having trouble breathing,” and “He's having a seizure.” You may also hear *fit* used to mean a **seizure** in a medical discussion. If you do not know whether a person is conscious, breathing normally, or has a particular condition, say what you can observe and answer further questions; do not invent facts.

### Ask bystanders for a specific kind of help

**Person:** I need help. Please call 911. We're beside the library's front entrance.  
**Bystander:** What happened?  
**Person:** Someone fell and can't get up.  
**Bystander:** I'll make the call.  
**Person:** Thank you. I'll tell you what I know.  

A **front entrance**, **floor**, **apartment number**, or nearby **landmark** can make a location more precise. “Over there” alone may leave the location unclear. Ask “Could you help me explain this?” if you need communication support.

### Report an injury at a care setting

**Patient:** I fell this afternoon and hurt my ankle. It hurts when I put weight on it.  
**Clinician:** Where is the pain?  
**Patient:** Around the outside of the ankle. I don't know if it's sprained or broken.  
**Clinician:** We'll assess it.  
**Patient:** Could you explain what you're checking?  

**Scrape** is a skin injury; **cut** is an opening in the skin; **nosebleed** is bleeding from the nose; **burn** is an injury caused by heat or another damaging source. **Stitches** close some wounds; naming them does not establish that a particular injury needs them. “I burned my hand on the stove” reports an event. “My child cut her arm on broken glass” gives a cause without choosing treatment.

## Practice

Fictional cases: a fall beside the library entrance; severe chest pain at 24 Cedar Street, apartment three, callback 202-555-0118; ankle pain after a fall this afternoon. No clinical findings are supplied.

1. Simulate the emergency exchange. Read the call-taker prompts and give the location and known event. Do not dial a real number.
2. Ask a bystander to call and explain the landmark. Partner asks what happened. Respond with the fall case.
3. At a care setting, describe the ankle problem. Partner asks where it hurts. Report the location and uncertainty.
4. Repeat with a hand burned on the stove. State what happened rather than choosing a remedy.

Alone, speak your turns after each written prompt. Later retrieve the location and problem with the model hidden.

**Possibilities:** “Beside the library's front entrance.” “It hurts when I put weight on it.” “I don't know if it's broken.” Check that urgent need, location, observed event, and uncertainties are understandable. Do not score a learner on medical decisions.  

*Source note: [National 911 Program calling guidance](https://www.911.gov/calling-911/frequently-asked-questions/).*
