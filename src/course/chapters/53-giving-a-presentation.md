# CH53 — Giving a Presentation

**Outcome:** Deliver a connected short presentation with a clear purpose, organized points and an ending, then handle a question.  

## Study

A presentation needs something to communicate, not just a list of transitions. Choose what you want the audience to understand or decide. Then organize a beginning, a small number of connected points, and an ending.

Useful openings include **Today I'd like to explain...** and **The purpose of this presentation is...** To show the route: **I'll start with the problem, then explain the proposal.** Transitions include **First**, **Next**, **Now let's look at...**, and **Finally**. An ending can summarize or request action: **To sum up...** and **I'd like your feedback before we decide**.

### Facts for an original model

This fictional team has difficulty finding the approved version of a document. The proposal is to use separate folders named **Drafts** and **Approved**, record a document's owner, and review the trial after one week. It is a proposal for this scene, not a required software method.

### Connected model

“Good morning, everyone. Today I'd like to explain a proposal for organizing our shared documents. I'll start with the problem, then describe a small change we could try.

“At the moment, several versions of a document can have similar names. People sometimes open an older version because it isn't clear which one is approved. That creates extra checking and repeated questions.

“My proposal has two parts. First, we would use separate folders called Drafts and Approved. That would make the status easier to see. Second, we would record who is responsible for each document, so people know whom to ask when something is unclear.

“Now let's look at the trial. We could test the arrangement with one project for a week. At the end of the week, we would ask the team what was easier and what still caused confusion. We haven't measured an improvement yet; the trial would help us find out.

“To sum up, the proposal is to separate draft and approved versions and identify each document's owner. I'd like your feedback before we decide on the trial. What questions or concerns do you have?”

Notice that the transitions organize real information. The speaker distinguishes the current problem, a proposal, and a result not yet known. **Could** and **would** present the change as a possibility rather than an already approved instruction.

### Answer an audience question

**Audience member:** Who would move the approved documents?  
**Presenter:** That's something we need to decide. My suggestion is that each document's owner would do it. What do you think?  
**Audience member:** Would the whole team use this immediately?  
**Presenter:** No. The proposal is a one-week trial with one project.  

If you do not know: **I don't have that information yet. I'll need to check.** If the question is unclear: **Are you asking about the trial or the later rollout?** **Rollout** means introducing a change more widely. You can promise a follow-up only when you intend to provide it: **I'll check and get back to you tomorrow**.

### Plan your own short version

Use these five prompts: What is the topic? Why does it matter? What are two points the audience needs? What remains uncertain? What response do you want?

You do not need every formal opening in the chapter. **To kick things off...** can give a conversational opening; **The purpose of this presentation is...** makes the purpose explicit. Choose the wording that fits the talk you want to give rather than adding formality to every sentence.

## Practice

1. Read the connected model aloud once. Then cover it and explain the problem, two proposed changes and the trial using the facts above. If alone, read an audience question afterward and answer it.
2. Use a second fictional proposal: a team wants a shared lunch schedule because break times are unclear; post the planned times; identify who updates them; ask for feedback after a week. Plan a beginning, two connected points and an ending. Say the complete talk, not just its headings.
3. Your audience asks a question whose answer is not in the facts. State that you need to check, rather than inventing a rule.

**Sample short talk:** “Today I'd like to explain a proposal for our lunch schedule. At the moment, break times are unclear. First, we could post the planned times in one place. Second, we could name the person responsible for updates. We could try this for a week and ask what still needs work. What concerns do you have?”  

**Possible unknown-answer response:** “I don't have that information yet. I'll need to check before answering.”  

Return later and give the talk without the model. Check that a listener could state your purpose, distinguish facts from proposals, and identify the response you wanted. Speed and exact wording are secondary to a clear connected message.
